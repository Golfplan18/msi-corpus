The warning marked a sharp escalation in the standoff over Greenland's future: a Danish political journalist noted Monday that Frederiksen — who would have flatly rejected the prospect of an American takeover in earlier months — now felt compelled to acknowledge it as a scenario requiring a response, as Trump signaled he would revisit the subject within 20 days.

COPENHAGEN, Denmark — Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen said Monday that an American military takeover of Greenland would mean the end of NATO, issuing her starkest public warning after President Donald Trump renewed calls for U.S. control of the strategic, mineral-rich Arctic island following the weekend U.S. military operation in Venezuela that captured leader Nicolás Maduro.

"If the United States chooses to attack another NATO country militarily, then everything stops," Frederiksen told Danish broadcaster TV2. "That is, including our NATO and thus the security that has been provided since the end of the Second World War."

Frederiksen said Trump "should be taken seriously" when he says he wants Greenland. "We will not accept a situation where we and Greenland are threatened in this way," she added.

Numerous European leaders expressed solidarity with Frederiksen and her Greenlandic counterpart on Monday.

## Trump signals a 20-day window

Trump told reporters Sunday, as he flew back to Washington from his home in Florida, "let's talk about Greenland in 20 days" — a remark that deepened fears in Copenhagen and Nuuk that the administration was moving toward a near-term intervention. He has called repeatedly since his presidential transition for U.S. jurisdiction over Greenland and has not ruled out military force.

The comments followed the early-Saturday U.S. military operation in Caracas to capture Maduro and his wife, a development that left European governments on edge about what military actions the administration might consider permissible next.

Ask Rostrup, a TV2 political journalist, wrote on the broadcaster's live blog Monday that Frederiksen — who would have flatly rejected the prospect of an American takeover in earlier months — now felt compelled to acknowledge the possibility, reflecting how significantly the rhetoric had escalated.

## Greenlandic leader urges calm

Greenlandic Prime Minister Jens Frederik Nielsen, speaking at a news conference Monday, urged his constituents to stay calm and drew a firm line between his territory and Venezuela.

"We are not in a situation where we think that there might be a takeover of the country overnight and that is why we are insisting that we want good cooperation," Nielsen said. "The situation is not such that the United States can simply conquer Greenland."

Greenland is a semiautonomous territory of the Danish kingdom and, as such, part of NATO.

## Trump's Arctic claims disputed

Trump also mocked Denmark's security posture in Greenland on Sunday, saying the Danes had added "one more dog sled" to the territory's defenses.

"It's so strategic right now," Trump told reporters. "Greenland is covered with Russian and Chinese ships all over the place. We need Greenland from the standpoint of national security, and Denmark is not going to be able to do it."

A security assessment published last year by Ulrik Pram Gad, a global security expert at the Danish Institute for International Studies, offered a different picture of Russian and Chinese naval activity in the region: those vessels "are too far away to see from Greenland with or without binoculars," Gad wrote.

## 'SOON'

Concerns in Copenhagen and Nuuk deepened further over the weekend after Katie Miller, a former Trump administration official who is married to Trump's deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller, posted an illustrated map of Greenland rendered in the colors of the American flag. The caption read: "SOON."

Denmark's chief envoy to Washington, Ambassador Jesper Møller Sørensen, responded directly. "We expect full respect for the territorial integrity of the Kingdom of Denmark," Sørensen said in a post.

## U.S. presence and alliance ties

The U.S. Department of Defense already operates Pituffik Space Base in northwestern Greenland under a bilateral defense agreement struck in 1951. The installation supports missile warning, missile defense, and space surveillance for the U.S. and NATO.

On the Danish mainland, the defense partnership has expanded in recent years. Denmark's parliament last year approved legislation allowing U.S. military bases on Danish soil — critics said the vote ceded Danish sovereignty — widening a 2023 agreement with the Biden administration under which U.S. troops had broad access to Danish air bases.
