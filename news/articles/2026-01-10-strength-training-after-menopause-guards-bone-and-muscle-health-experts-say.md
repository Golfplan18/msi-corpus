Medical experts say strength training is one of the most effective steps women can take after menopause to protect bone density and muscle mass, countering accelerated tissue loss that follows estrogen decline. Physical therapists and OB-GYNs say the benefits extend to weight management and fall prevention — and that programs accessible to beginners can deliver substantial results.

"People underestimate how powerful it is," said Dr. Christina DeAngelis, an OB-GYN at Penn State Health.

Women in midlife who begin or expand resistance exercise — working muscles against weights, bodyweight, or other load — can substantially offset the physical changes menopause accelerates, experts say, with options ranging from free weights and gym machines to squats, pushups, and balance drills that require no equipment at all.

## Why the stakes rise after menopause

Estrogen loss that accompanies menopause speeds up a reduction in bone density and contributes to the gradual loss of muscle mass, according to medical experts. The consequences compound over years: weaker bones and reduced muscle strength raise the risk of falls and fractures.

Hilary Granat, a physical therapist who owns Core Total Wellness in Washington, D.C., said bone and muscle health are directly linked. When muscle pulls on bone during resistance training, it stimulates bone-building cells — making the exercise a direct intervention in the process menopause accelerates.

Bone mass peaks between ages 25 and 30 and begins to lessen around age 40, experts say, adding value to starting strength training well before menopause while keeping it relevant for women at any stage.

## Working with weights

Effective strength training requires real effort, Granat said.

"You really have to push yourself," she said.

That means using weights heavy enough to be challenging and completing somewhere between six and 30 repetitions per set. Signs that the load is appropriate include slowing tempo, reduced speed, or the inability to complete another repetition with proper form.

For an exercise like a bicep curl, Granat said a practical starting weight is 5 to 20 pounds (2.3 to 9.1 kilograms), with beginners at the lower end. Resistance can come from dumbbells, free weights, or machines such as rowers. The chest press — lying on a bench or the ground, pressing weights up from chest level and lowering them — is one example of an effective movement.

## No equipment required

Building bone and muscle strength does not require a gym. Pushups, squats, lunges, situps, crunches, planks, and wall sits all count as resistance exercises.

Even the transition from sitting to standing has value, DeAngelis said.

"You have to engage your core going from sitting to standing," she said. "That also allows you to work on your coordination and balance."

Impact training — walking, hiking, running, jumping, skipping, and climbing stairs — also benefits bone health. Granat describes jumping up and down from a step 10 to 30 times as a "rebound jump," a high-impact option that three sessions per week is enough to sustain.

## Balance training

Experts say women in midlife should also include balance exercises, which play a crucial role in preventing falls that can break or fracture bones.

Tai chi, yoga, and standing on one leg for around 30 seconds while brushing one's teeth are effective and low-barrier options, experts say. The same exercises benefit younger women, who begin losing bone mass around age 40.

## One practitioner's experience

Sarah Baldassaro, 52, of Alexandria, Virginia, began working with a fitness coach on strength training when she turned 50. Before, her routine was weighted toward cardiovascular exercise.

"Now I would say I'm stronger overall than I ever have been at any age," Baldassaro said. "My fitness level is much more like when I was in my early 30s."

She said she has worked up to lifting 20 pounds (9.1 kilograms) for some exercises and found the shift transformative.

"The strength training has really been the difference maker for me," she said.

Her advice to other women in midlife: "Don't wait. Get started."
