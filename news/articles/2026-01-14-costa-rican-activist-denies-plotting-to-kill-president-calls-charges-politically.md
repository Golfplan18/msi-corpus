The accusations surfaced as Chaves hosted El Salvador's President Nayib Bukele at a groundbreaking for a new Costa Rican prison modeled on Bukele's infamous gang detention facility — and as Costa Rica grapples with a violent crime surge that produced 877 homicides in 2025.

A Costa Rican activist and government critic accused of plotting to kill President Rodrigo Chaves denied the allegations Wednesday, calling the evidence against her fabricated and the prosecution politically motivated. Stella Chinchilla, vice president of the human rights advocacy group Friends for Peace Center, appeared before the Public Ministry in San José to hear the formal details of the case.

Costa Rica's national security chief Jorge Torres had told prosecutors Tuesday that a hitman allegedly received payment to assassinate Chaves, though the specifics of the accusation were not publicly disclosed at that time. Hours later, local media published screenshots of messages Torres included in his complaint against Chinchilla, purportedly showing her complaining to alleged hitmen that they had not carried out the killing.

Chinchilla confirmed the screenshots were part of the case presented against her but told the Associated Press they were fake. "I have not written a single comma of what is there," she said. "Morally, I would not order the killing of this president; he has to leave on his own feet, from the government and the country, because he has done too much damage."

She alleged that media outlets aligned with Chaves had obtained the purported evidence before it had even been received by prosecutors, and characterized the accusations as part of a broader pattern of government harassment directed at critics of the administration.

## Security reinforced as Bukele visits

The president's office said Tuesday night that Chaves' security had been reinforced in response to the alleged threat. Jeffrey Cerdas, head of presidential security, said the situation amounted to more than a routine warning. "This is not a minor warning, nor speculation; it is a serious threat to the country's democratic stability," Cerdas said.

The accusations emerged on the same day Chaves hosted El Salvador's President Nayib Bukele at a groundbreaking for a new Costa Rican prison modeled on Bukele's mega-detention facility, which was built to hold alleged gang members. Bukele, whose security crackdown has drawn both widespread domestic support and international criticism over due-process concerns, praised Chaves for following El Salvador's example.

"When we arrived we had to change everything, courts, judges, prosecutors, laws, Congress, in the elections, of course," Bukele said Wednesday. He described the gang prison as a "pillar" of El Salvador's fight against crime and said that prior to his intervention, prisons had functioned as training grounds for criminals.

Chaves has sent Costa Rican officials to El Salvador to study Bukele's law enforcement model.

## Crime surge backdrop

Costa Rica has recorded historically high homicide rates in recent years, attributed to drug trafficking gangs. The country ended 2025 with 877 homicides, three fewer than in 2024, while 2023 registered the highest total on record at 907 killings.

Bukele framed the violence in stark economic terms during his remarks Wednesday. "If criminality grows, more Costa Ricans will die, but not just that will happen," he said. "The way of life will change... tourism will disappear and all of the foreign currency that comes with it."

The alleged assassination plot remains under investigation. No charges had been formally filed against Chinchilla as of Wednesday, according to reporting by the Associated Press.
