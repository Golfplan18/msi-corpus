Police from Brazil, French Guiana, Guyana and Suriname arrested nearly 200 people in their first-ever joint cross-border operation targeting illegal gold mining in the Amazon region, authorities announced Thursday. The December operation, backed by Interpol, the European Union and Dutch police specializing in environmental crime, conducted more than 24,500 checks on vehicles and people in remote border areas and seized unprocessed gold, mercury, firearms and mining equipment.

Illegal gold mining has become a major driver of deforestation and river pollution in the Amazon, contaminating waterways with toxic mercury and damaging lands relied on by Indigenous communities. The activity has expanded rapidly as global gold prices climbed to near-record highs, pushing miners deeper into remote forest regions and turning gold into one of the most profitable commodities for organized crime operating across borders.

## Key Arrests and Organized Crime Networks

Among those arrested were three men detained in Guyana on suspicion of gold smuggling and money laundering after officers seized unprocessed gold and about $590,000 in cash. Investigators said the suspects are believed to be part of an organized crime group and may have links to a major gold exporting company in Guyana.

## Mercury Trafficking and Toxic Hazards

Authorities also seized cylinders of mercury worth more than $60,000 in Guyana and Suriname. Mercury is commonly used in illegal gold mining to separate gold from other materials, but it is highly toxic and can contaminate rivers, wildlife and people. According to authorities, the mercury had been hidden inside solar panels and transported by bus.

Police in South America carried out coordinated checks along shared borders, including inspections on both sides of rivers that separate the countries. Officers searched vehicles, boats and small riverside shops that sell fuel, tools and other supplies commonly used in illegal mining. Some of the shops are suspected of helping smuggle gold and mercury across borders.

## Additional Seizures and Humanitarian Concerns

During the operation, police seized counterfeit medicines, alcohol and cigarettes worth more than $40,000, along with mining pumps, mats used to collect gold, firearms and mobile phones.

Authorities also said officers stopped a bus carrying undocumented migrants, including several minors. Some of the children are suspected of being victims of forced labor or sexual exploitation, highlighting the human toll of illegal mining networks.

## Regional Cooperation and Enforcement Challenges

Interpol Secretary General Valdecy Urquiza said in a statement: "Illegal gold mining is growing rapidly and causing serious harm to the environment and local communities, especially in remote and fragile areas."

The operation, known as Operation Guyana Shield, marked a significant step forward in cooperation between countries in the Amazon region, where dense rainforest, long distances and porous borders have long made it difficult to police illegal mining.
