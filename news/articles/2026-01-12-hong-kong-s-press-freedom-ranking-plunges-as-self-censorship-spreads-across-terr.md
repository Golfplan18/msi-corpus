The decline in press freedom, journalism academics and press advocates say, reflects not only direct legal action against reporters and outlets but a broader chilling effect in which sources have grown unwilling to speak, advertisers discourage coverage that offends powerful interests, and journalists weigh legal exposure before publishing. The Hong Kong government has maintained that its laws protect rights and freedoms guaranteed under the territory's Basic Law.

HONG KONG — Hong Kong's standing in a global press freedom index has fallen from 18th place to 140th over roughly two decades, according to the media freedom organization Reporters Without Borders, as Beijing's 2020 national security law shuttered two of the territory's most prominent independent outlets, sent their editors and founders to prison, and drove self-censorship deep into journalism and public life.

Sentencing hearings for Apple Daily founder Jimmy Lai opened Monday before Hong Kong courts. Lai was convicted in December of conspiring with others to collude with foreign forces and of conspiracy to publish seditious articles, and faces up to life in prison. The hearings are being watched closely by foreign governments and political observers as a measure of press freedom conditions in the former British colony, which returned to Chinese rule in 1997. The Hong Kong government has maintained that his case has nothing to do with press freedom.

## Outlets shut down, journalists convicted

Hong Kong's media environment was once characterized by aggressive questioning of officials and regular critical reporting, even from outlets whose owners held pro-Beijing positions. That space narrowed sharply after Beijing imposed the national security law, which it said was necessary for stability after large anti-government protests in 2019.

In 2020, Lai became one of the first prominent figures charged under the law. By June 2021, authorities had raided Apple Daily's offices and frozen $2.3 million of its assets, effectively forcing the newspaper to close. Six Apple Daily executives charged in the same case as Lai had entered guilty pleas, admitting they conspired with Lai to request sanctions, blockades, or other hostile activities against Hong Kong or China.

Stand News met a similar fate in December 2021, shutting down after arrests, police raids, and asset freezes. By 2022, Hong Kong had fallen 68 places to 148th in the Reporters Without Borders index.

In 2024, two Stand News editors became the first journalists since 1997 to be convicted of conspiracy to publish seditious articles under a separate, colonial-era law.

## Narrowing legal ground for reporters

Francis Lee, a journalism and communication professor at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, said the Apple Daily and Stand News cases show that some common news practices of the past are no longer permitted. The Stand News case indicated that strongly critical commentaries with relatively intense expression might be considered seditious, he said. Lai's case involved allegations of calling for foreign sanctions.

"Maybe some advocacy journalism was at least permitted within the legal framework back then," Lee said, referring to the period before the security law. "Today, it's no longer allowed."

Lee said mainstream outlets also face growing pressure not to upset advertisers and large companies, many of which value access to mainland Chinese markets and ties with the government.

Self-censorship has extended beyond newsrooms. "In Hong Kong nowadays, when some topics and perspectives cannot be reported, it's not just because of media outlets practicing self-censorship," Lee said. "No one is willing to speak. Self-censorship is a broad social phenomenon."

Many opposition politicians and leading activists have been jailed under the security law. Dozens of civil society groups have shut down. Some residents have grown more reluctant to speak with reporters.

Selina Cheng, chairperson of the Hong Kong Journalists Association, said stories perceived as politically sensitive or potentially questioning the authorities are not always easily published. She said there is an outsized concern about including responses from the government and pro-China groups to create balance.

"To do journalism in Hong Kong means that people always have to worry at the back of their heads: What are the risks that they may get involved in?" Cheng said.

## A deadly fire and its aftermath

A fire that killed at least 161 people in an apartment complex on Nov. 26 offered a recent illustration of the pressures on coverage. Reporters, including those from newer online outlets, covered what authorities described as Hong Kong's deadliest blaze in decades, interviewing affected residents and investigating scaffolding nettings that authorities said had contributed to the blaze's rapid spread.

On Dec. 6, Beijing's national security arm in Hong Kong summoned representatives of several foreign news outlets, including the Associated Press. The Office for Safeguarding National Security said some foreign media had spread false information and smeared the government's relief efforts after the fire and attacked the legislative election.

Arrests of non-journalists who posted allegedly seditious content online or organized a petition followed, leaving reporters with fewer people willing to speak on the record, Lee said. A planned news conference related to the fire was canceled. Bruce Liu, an organizer, was summoned by police for a meeting the same day. An investigative report on the fire's maintenance project by a pro-Beijing newspaper is no longer viewable on that publication's website.

Ellie Yuen, who had written a social media post questioning regulators' oversight that drew wide circulation, said she stopped posting about the fire for "obvious reasons" without elaborating.

Cheng raised concerns about what she described as the "more covert muscling of people speaking out."

"If this keeps happening, then it's much harder for the public to know what they're missing out on," she said.

The Hong Kong government, in a written reply to Associated Press questions, condemned attempts to use the fire as an excuse to smear the administration with what it called baseless accusations. "Human rights and freedoms of Hong Kong residents have all along been firmly protected by the constitution and the Basic Law," the government said.

## Pressures beyond reporting

Cheng's trade union has also raised concerns about journalists facing unwarranted tax audits and harassment through anonymous messages. The Inland Revenue Department has maintained that the background of a taxpayer has no bearing on its reviews. Cheng has separately launched a lawsuit against her former employer, The Wall Street Journal, for allegedly terminating her over her union role.

Both Lee and Cheng said journalists in the territory are still learning to navigate a narrower operating space. In October, the Hong Kong Journalists Association reported that journalists' ratings of the city's press freedom index had rebounded slightly.

"Today's situation is far from the previous state of freedom," Lee said. "Self-censorship throughout society is severe. Yet some media outlets are still finding ways."
