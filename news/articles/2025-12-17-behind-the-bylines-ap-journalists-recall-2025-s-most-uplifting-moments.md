The accounts illustrate how wire-service journalists routinely become witnesses to private moments of joy and resilience — and, in some cases, the first to deliver life-altering news — amid a year dominated by political strife and natural disaster.

Associated Press journalists shared Wednesday the behind-the-scenes accounts of some of 2025's most resonant moments, from a papal phone call in a suburban Chicago basement to a Nobel Prize announced before sunrise in Seattle. AP video journalist Obed Lamy said he was "shaking" as he recorded Pope Leo XIV — the first American pope — greeting his brother John Prevost by phone, hours after the new pontiff appeared on the balcony of St. Peter's Basilica. In other accounts, AP correspondents documented a wedding that proceeded through typhoon floodwaters north of Manila and a California youth theater production staged weeks after the Palisades wildfire destroyed cast members' homes.

As MSI reported when the AP's year-end uplifting-moments dispatches first published, the accounts offered a counterpoint to a year defined largely by political upheaval and natural disaster — <a href='/articles/2025-12-17-ap-journalists-share-how-they-captured-2025-s-most-uplifting-moments'>that coverage</a> drew on the same events that AP journalists described Wednesday in first-person form.

## The pope was on the line

Lamy, based in Indianapolis, drove three hours to reach the New Lenox, Illinois, home of John Prevost after Prevost's brother was elected pope. Lamy said Prevost let him in and the two were talking when a sound came from the basement.

Prevost hurried downstairs to a tablet. Several missed calls from his brother awaited. He called the pope back on speaker, and Lamy followed with his camera on.

"I was shaking because I didn't know what the pope would say," Lamy said. "Am I supposed to say something or not say anything?"

The pope's first question, according to Lamy: why hadn't his brother been answering his calls.

"Well, first you need to know you're on the air right now," Prevost told the pope. "This is the first time I'm hearing that this thing rang."

The conversation lasted a few minutes. When Prevost said the family was coming to Rome, the pope replied: "Oh, where are you going to sleep?" The new pontiff, Lamy said, apparently had not yet been briefed on arrangements for his family.

## Breaking the news to a Nobel winner

AP photographer Lindsey Wasson was dispatched before dawn to the North Seattle home of Mary E. Brunkow after Brunkow was named a co-recipient of the Nobel Prize in medicine. Wasson said she arrived at approximately 3:45 a.m., before the Nobel committee had reached Brunkow by phone.

After navigating through a neighbor's darkened driveway, Wasson knocked. Brunkow's husband Ross answered through the glass door. Seeing no other way to explain her presence, Wasson told him: "Sir, your wife just won the Nobel Prize."

Ross brought Mary to the kitchen. When he had first gone into their bedroom with the news, he told Wasson, she had said: "Don't be ridiculous."

Only when Ross read aloud a portion of the AP's initial report on his phone — and the couple recognized that the overnight calls from Sweden were not spam — did the mood shift to joy. Wasson said she followed along with her camera as Brunkow worked through an avalanche of emails, texts, and calls from family, friends, and other journalists.

## A wedding in the flood

AP photographer Aaron Favila covered a wedding at a church north of Manila after a typhoon left floodwaters blocking the roads. Favila, who has covered floods in the Philippines for years and said the country experiences at least 20 typhoons annually, said his vehicle could not cross the deepest stretches and he caught a ride on a passing rescue truck.

He arrived just before the doors opened for the bride.

"We've been through a lot. This is just one of the struggles that we've overcome," said the groom, Jade Rick Verdillo, according to Favila.

Favila said he stayed through the entire ceremony rather than leaving after the first kiss, which a deadline story would have required.

## Theater after the fire

AP reporter Jocelyn Gecker covered the opening night of a Southern California youth theater group's production of "Crazy for You" — a musical built on songs by George and Ira Gershwin — weeks after the Palisades wildfire destroyed their theater and the homes of many cast members. Rehearsals had begun January 6; the fire started the next day, January 7. Director Lara Ganz, whose own family lost their home in the blaze, said the production was about restoring routine and hope for young people who had lost so much.

After the article was published, members of the Gershwin family trust read the story and attended a later performance. They delivered a letter to the cast and crew.

"On behalf of the families of George and Ira Gershwin, we applaud your resilience," the letter said, praising the group's "amazing dedication" in the face of hardship.
