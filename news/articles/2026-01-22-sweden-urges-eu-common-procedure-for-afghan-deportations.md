The initiative reflects mounting pressure across Europe to manage deportations while avoiding any official arrangement that would grant legitimacy to Afghanistan's Taliban government — a tension Swedish officials say can be resolved through technical-level EU coordination.

Sweden's migration minister on Thursday urged the European Union to develop a coordinated system for issuing identification and travel documents to Afghan nationals whose asylum applications have been rejected or who have committed crimes in their host countries.

Johan Forssell said the current inability to deport failed asylum seekers and criminal offenders stems from a fundamental obstacle: Afghan embassies in Europe are not recognized by the Taliban regime, making it effectively impossible for Afghan nationals to obtain passports or identification.

"It is a major concern for us that we are seeing quite a few cases of people that have committed crimes, Afghan people that committed crimes in Sweden and it is more or less impossible to expel them today," Forssell told the Associated Press on the sidelines of an informal meeting of EU Justice and Home Affairs ministers in the Cypriot capital of Nicosia.

## Building EU Consensus

The documentation barrier affects failed Syrian asylum seekers as well, Forssell said, though Afghan nationals remain the priority for EU coordination. The European Union's executive arm has recently made contacts in the Afghan capital on the issue—what Forssell described as a "very positive first step"—and there is "broad consensus" among many EU countries facing similar challenges to accelerate deportations of rejected asylum applicants and criminal offenders.

Forssell proposed that Afghan nationals scheduled for deportation from different EU countries could be grouped together and repatriated aboard chartered flights, a measure he said would improve efficiency. European Commissioner for Internal Affairs and Migration Magnus Brunner confirmed that EU member states are engaging at a technical level with Afghanistan's "effective authorities" to better facilitate repatriations, an approach that allows cooperation without granting the Taliban regime official legitimacy.

## Public Support and Legitimacy

According to Forssell, more than half of Afghan asylum seekers will have their applications rejected. He argued that failed applicants must return home to maintain public support for admitting those who qualify: "If they don't go back, otherwise public support for admitting those who meet the asylum criteria will diminish."

## Sweden's Migration Policy Transformation

Sweden's traditionally generous asylum policies have shifted significantly over the past decade amid what Forssell characterized as growing public alarm over challenges created by mass migration. That shift prompted the formation of Sweden's current government three years ago, which was established with support from the Sweden Democrats, a hard-right anti-immigration party.

The magnitude of the policy change is evident in asylum application statistics. According to Forssell, asylum applications in Sweden have reached their lowest level since 1985. "So I think we are doing very well and we are really delivering what the Swedish population wants to see from us," he said.
