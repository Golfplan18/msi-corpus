Families of imprisoned Venezuelan critics, journalists, and opposition figures demanded the release of nearly 800 detained prisoners on January 20, after waiting outside prisons for two weeks as the government slowed prisoner releases it had promised following Nicolás Maduro's removal from power.

The families' plea came as acting President Delcy Rodríguez vowed to continue freeing those detained under Maduro, yet only 145 people had been verified released so far, according to the Venezuelan prisoner rights organization Foro Penal.

The releases mark a significant shift following Maduro's removal in early January, but human rights monitors warn that even released prisoners are not truly free. They face government restrictions that amount to continued state control over their lives.

## Families await follow-through on prisoner releases

Outside the Helicoide prison in Caracas, families have spent two weeks camped in tents, displaying posters of missing loved ones and holding vigils with candles lit in the darkness.

Francis Quiñones waits there for her son, imprisoned for more than five years. She has not spoken to him in over six months. "Here we are waiting," she said. "Every day, we're out here outside the Helicoide. The government is killing us psychologically."

## Releases fall far short of promises

The experience of families like Quiñones reflects Venezuela's current predicament. The government promised significant prisoner releases following the removal of Maduro in early January, when the president was deposed in a U.S. military raid. Acting President Delcy Rodríguez called it "a new political moment" for the nation.

Yet the releases have advanced slowly. Foro Penal, Venezuela's leading prisoner rights organization, had verified the release of only 145 people by January 20. Around 775 more remained in detention, according to the organization's director, Alfredo Romero.

## Freedom with conditions

Romero said the released prisoners are far from free. Most face government restrictions, including gag orders on speaking to the media and mandatory check-ins with authorities. Others are barred from leaving the country.

"These people are not free," Romero said. "They are subjected to constant, latent persecution."

While much of Venezuela has begun to move past the political transition that toppled Maduro in January, families like Quiñones remain in limbo, waiting for the prisoner releases the government has promised.
