The deal delivers a major geopolitical signal for the EU amid rising U.S. protectionism, but still faces a contested ratification vote in the European Parliament, with France remaining openly opposed and powerful agricultural lobbies working to block it.

The European Union and the Mercosur bloc of South American nations formally signed a long-sought free trade agreement Saturday in Asunción, Paraguay, capping more than a quarter-century of negotiations and creating one of the world's largest free trade zones.

The accord will gradually eliminate more than 90% of tariffs on goods ranging from Argentine beef to German automobiles, covering more than 700 million consumers across the member states of both blocs.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, who heads the EU's executive branch, framed the signing as a deliberate embrace of open trade. "It reflects a clear and deliberate choice: We choose fair trade over tariffs. We choose a productive long-term partnership over isolation," von der Leyen said at the ceremony. Her remarks came as U.S. President Donald Trump announced 10% tariffs on eight European nations over their opposition to American control of Greenland, according to the Associated Press. The White House did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

## Lula skips ceremony amid residual tensions

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a longtime advocate of the accord, did not attend the signing ceremony, signaling persistent strain between the two blocs. Brazil had been preparing to host the signing last month when European nations called it off, demanding more concessions for farmers concerned about competition from cheaper South American agricultural imports. The last-minute demand was widely seen across South America as an example of European overbearing.

"The EU's maximalist wish lists of demands from developing economies willing to sign free trade agreements are often perceived as patronizing," said Agathe Demarais, a senior policy fellow at the European Council on Foreign Relations.

Lula endorsed the agreement from a distance. "At a time when unilateralism isolates markets and protectionism inhibits global growth, two regions that share democratic values and a commitment to multilateralism choose a different path," he posted on X.

## European Parliament ratification remains the key hurdle

The agreement must be ratified by the European Parliament before taking effect — a vote considered far less certain than ratification in South America, where the deal has broad support. France remains the most prominent opponent, with President Emmanuel Macron warning that farmers' frustration with the EU could push more voters toward the country's far right ahead of the 2027 presidential election. Powerful agricultural lobbies across Europe were working to block ratification even as leaders signed Saturday.

The EU moved to shore up domestic support by incorporating strict quotas on farm imports, including beef and sugar, alongside environmental regulations on South American agricultural practices, and by promising hefty subsidies to European farmers. That package was enough to bring Italy — a major agricultural power — on board earlier this month.

EU trade commissioner Maroš Šefčovič said he would begin lobbying European Parliament members on Monday. "We'll have very intense communication with all the members on a group level, on an individual level," Šefčovič told the Associated Press, expressing confidence that the deal could pass in the first half of the year.

## Milei reversal marks changed political landscape

Among the more notable aspects of Saturday's ceremony was the presence of Argentine President Javier Milei, who came to office in late 2023 calling Mercosur a "prison," threatening to withdraw from the bloc, and skipping the 2024 summit. His enthusiasm for the deal's free-trade terms overcame his longstanding skepticism of multilateral institutions.

"Argentina understands firsthand that closure and protectionism, sheltered by rhetoric rather than results, are the greatest causes of economic stagnation," Milei said before signing.

Mercosur consists of Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, and Uruguay. Bolivia, the bloc's newest member, is not included in the agreement but could join in coming years. Venezuela has been suspended from Mercosur and is not part of the deal.
