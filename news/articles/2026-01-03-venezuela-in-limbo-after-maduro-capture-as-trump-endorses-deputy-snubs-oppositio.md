Venezuela's capital fell under an uneasy calm Saturday as residents scrambled to determine who held authority over their country, hours after U.S. military forces captured President Nicolás Maduro. President Donald Trump publicly endorsed Delcy Rodríguez — Maduro's vice president since 2018 and constitutional successor — as a potential governing partner, while dismissing Nobel Peace Prize-winning opposition leader Maria Corina Machado as lacking the support to lead the nation of 29 million.

Venezuela's high court ordered Rodríguez to assume the role of interim president, but her remarks on state television offered no sign of cooperation with Washington. Surrounded by top civilian officials and military commanders, she demanded the immediate release of Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, called the U.S. operation "a flagrant violation of the United Nations charter," and declared: "There is only one president in this country, and his name is Nicolás Maduro."

The contradictory signals — Trump describing Rodríguez as willing to cooperate while she publicly defied the U.S. military action — left Venezuela's political future unresolved less than 24 hours after Maduro's capture, with key officials still in place and no swearing-in ceremony broadcast on state television.

CARACAS, Venezuela — Streets in Venezuela's capital emptied Saturday as residents lined up at supermarkets and gas stations, fear spreading through Caracas after U.S. military forces captured President Nicolás Maduro in an overnight operation that left the country's leadership structure unresolved.

"What will happen tomorrow?" asked Juan Pablo Petrone, a Caracas resident. "What will happen in the next hour?"

President Donald Trump offered a striking answer: the United States would take control of Venezuela, potentially in coordination with Delcy Rodríguez, Maduro's vice president and next in the constitutional line of succession.

## Trump endorses Rodríguez, claims cooperation

Trump told reporters that Rodríguez had spoken at length with U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and agreed to U.S. terms.

"She's essentially willing to do what we think is necessary to make Venezuela great again," Trump said, claiming Rodríguez had told Rubio: "We'll do whatever you need."

Trump indicated Rodríguez had already been sworn in as president under Venezuela's constitutional transfer-of-power provisions. Venezuela's high court had ordered her to assume the interim presidency.

"I think she was quite gracious," Trump said. "We can't take a chance that somebody else takes over Venezuela that doesn't have the good of the Venezuelan people in mind."

## Rodríguez presents a different face on state TV

Rodríguez's public posture offered no sign of the cooperation Trump described.

Appearing on state television surrounded by top civilian officials and military commanders, she demanded the immediate release of Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, and called the U.S. operation unlawful.

"What is being done to Venezuela is an atrocity that violates international law," she said. "History and justice will make the extremists who promoted this armed aggression pay."

No swearing-in ceremony was broadcast on state television. A ticker at the bottom of the screen identified her as the vice president. She made no reference to cooperating with Washington and did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

## Military commanders vow defiance

Senior Venezuelan military officials appeared to have survived the U.S. military operation and held their positions.

Defense Minister Gen. Vladimir Padrino López, dressed in fatigues, said in a video message: "They have attacked us but will not break us."

Interior Minister Diosdado Cabello urged Venezuelans to "get out on the streets" to defend the country's sovereignty.

"These rats attacked and they will regret what they did," Cabello said of the United States.

Some residents heeded the call, with scattered gatherings across Caracas burning American flags. But most people stayed inside.

"What's happening is unprecedented," said Yanire Lucas, a Caracas resident who was picking up broken glass from an explosion at a nearby military base that had blown out her windows. "We're still on edge, and now we're unsure what to do."

## Opposition calls for democratic transition — then is rebuffed

Shortly before Trump's press conference, opposition leader Maria Corina Machado — who was awarded the 2025 Nobel Peace Prize, according to the Associated Press — called on her ally Edmundo González, a retired diplomat widely considered to have won Venezuela's disputed 2024 presidential election, to "immediately assume his constitutional mandate and be recognized as commander-in-chief."

"Today we are prepared to assert our mandate and take power," Machado said.

Trump moved in a different direction. Asked about Machado, he was blunt: "I think it would be very tough for (Machado) to be the leader. She doesn't have the support or respect within the country."

Machado had not responded to Trump's remarks as of Saturday evening.

## Constitutional questions unresolved

Venezuela's constitution requires a new election to be called within a month in the event of the president's absence. But experts debated whether that succession scenario applies given the government's lack of popular legitimacy and the nature of the U.S. military intervention, according to the Associated Press.

## Rodríguez's profile and political ties

Rodríguez, a lawyer educated in Britain and France, has represented Venezuela on the world stage since the era of the late President Hugo Chávez. She and her brother, Jorge Rodríguez — who heads the Maduro-controlled National Assembly — carry significant credentials within the Venezuelan left: their father was a socialist leader who died in police custody in the 1970s.

Unlike many in Maduro's inner circle, Rodríguez has not faced criminal indictment in the United States. She developed ties with Republicans in the oil industry and on Wall Street, and her past interlocutors have included Blackwater founder Erik Prince and Richard Grenell, a Trump special envoy who sought to negotiate a deal with Maduro for greater U.S. influence in Venezuela.

Rodríguez faced U.S. sanctions during Trump's first administration for her role in undermining Venezuelan democracy, according to the Associated Press.

## Internal power structure under strain

Rodríguez's ability to consolidate authority faces a significant obstacle: Cabello and other military hard-liners who joined Chávez in the 1990s armed uprising against Venezuela's democratically elected president continue to hold sway over the armed forces. Many are wanted in the United States on drug trafficking charges and stand accused of serious human rights abuses, according to the Associated Press.

David Smilde, a sociology professor at Tulane University who has studied Venezuela's political dynamics for three decades, said he expected the ruling bloc to hold together.

"These leaders have all seen the value of staying united. Cabello has always taken a second seat or third seat, knowing that his fate is tied up with Maduro's, and now he very well might do that again," Smilde said.

The durability of that unity depends in part on the condition of Venezuela's military following the U.S. operation, Smilde added: "If it doesn't have much firepower anymore, they're more vulnerable and diminished."
