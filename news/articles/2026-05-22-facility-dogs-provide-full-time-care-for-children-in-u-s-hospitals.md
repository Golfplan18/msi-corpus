## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- Children’s hospitals in the United States are increasingly employing full-time facility dogs to comfort young patients, motivate them through rehabilitation, and provide companionship during long hospital stays, the Associated Press reported.
- Among those dogs is Hadley, a golden retriever mix at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital, who recently helped 5-year-old Calvin Owens stand and play outside for the first time in more than a month.
- Facility dogs are hospital employees trained to assist child life specialists with medical procedures, physical therapy, and emotional support, differing from volunteer therapy animals in their specialized roles and full-time presence.
- Research cited by the AP, including studies led by the University of Arizona’s Kerri Rodriguez, has found that interactions with facility dogs can significantly reduce patient stress and anxiety and lead to measurable improvements in mobility during rehabilitation.
- At St. Louis Children’s Hospital, facility dog specialist Brittney Franklin works with dogs that help calm children during MRI and CT scans, blood draws, and cast placements, the AP said.
- Handlers and hospital staff credit the dogs with giving children the motivation to participate in treatment, and parents describe the animals as a crucial emotional anchor during grueling hospitalizations.

On a sunny hospital patio in Cincinnati, 5-year-old Calvin Owens met his canine friend Hadley for the first time in weeks — and managed, despite being tethered to equipment with wires and tubes, to stand up from his wheelchair long enough to toss her a ball. He smiled as she chased it. His caregivers cheered.
