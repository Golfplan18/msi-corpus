President Donald Trump on Wednesday abandoned his threat to impose tariffs on eight European allies and withdrew the option of using military force to acquire Greenland, according to the Associated Press. The reversals came as major stock indexes plummeted over concerns about a potential NATO rupture. Trump announced he had reached a "framework" agreement with NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte on Greenland during his keynote at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland.

The market volatility demonstrated that investor confidence in stable U.S.-allied relationships is foundational to broader economic stability.

During his keynote at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Trump announced on Wednesday that he would not use military force to acquire Greenland. "I won't do that. OK?" he said to the packed conference room.

Later that day, Trump went further, announcing a retreat from his tariff threat against eight European allies. He said he had reached terms with NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte on a "framework" on Greenland that "gets us everything we needed to get" if the agreement is consummated.

Trump told financial network CNBC just before Wall Street trading ended that the framework would benefit both the United States and allied nations. When asked whether the jittery market had played a role in his tariff retreat, Trump downplayed that factor.

"No, we took that off because it looks like we have pretty much a concept of a deal," Trump said.

The S&P 500 rallied 1.2% after his remarks, recovering about half the ground it had lost the day before. The Dow Jones Industrial Average also rose 1.2%, as did the Nasdaq Composite.

## Framework Details Remain Vague

Denmark's foreign minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen cautiously offered that "the day is ending on a better note than it began" but said the details of the agreement still need to be worked out.

One idea NATO members discussed as part of the compromise would see Denmark and the alliance working with the U.S. to build out more U.S. bases in Greenland, according to a European official familiar with the matter but not authorized to comment publicly.

Rutte gave little hint about what precisely he and Trump agreed to. "We agreed that he's right, and he's right that collectively we have to protect the Arctic regions," Rutte said on Fox News. "But also, of course, the U.S. continue its conversations with Greenland and Denmark when it comes to how can we make sure that the Russians and China will not gain access to the economy or a military sense of Greenland."

## Market Losses and Policy Concerns

Market losses in the days before Trump's Davos appearance had sparked serious talk within NATO about a fundamental rupture to the transatlantic military alliance. U.S. officials had been concerned about Trump's hardline stance toward Greenland, Denmark and other NATO allies because they feared it could harm other foreign policy goals, particularly his effort to form the Board of Peace.

Max Bergmann, director of the Europe, Russia and Eurasia Program at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, offered an interpretation of the European response.

"The interpretation that European leaders are going to take from this is that actually standing up and being firm defused the crisis," Bergmann said. "Trump is acting like a bully, and the only way that we're going to have a stable relationship is if we push back."

Matthew Kroenig, vice president and senior director of the Atlantic Council's Scowcroft Center for Strategy and Security, took a different view of Trump's tactics. He said the idea that Trump would seize Greenland seemed more like a bluff all along—and one that may have worked. "Most of the world was freaking out over these threats," Kroenig said.

The diplomatic incident drove the Canadian prime minister to propose that smaller countries unite against aggressive superpowers, according to Daniel Fried, a former U.S. ambassador to Poland who is now a distinguished senior fellow at the Atlantic Council.

"It's been unnecessarily dramatic, costly and damaging, but all the damages so far are repairable," Fried said. A continuation of the path Trump appeared to be heading on would have made that possibility harder to achieve, he added.
