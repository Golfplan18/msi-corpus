TEL AVIV — Exiled Iranian Crown Prince Reza Pahlavi released a video Thursday laying out a post-theocracy agenda for Iran: no nuclear weapons development, crackdowns on drug trafficking, immediate recognition of Israel, and large-scale oil and gas exports. Analysts say the list was crafted to appeal to U.S. President Donald Trump, not to the Iranians who have been dying in the streets since late December.

Pahlavi posted the video on X in English, not Farsi, as Iranian security forces continued a crackdown that has killed 2,637 people since protests began Dec. 28 over the collapse of the Iranian rial, according to the U.S.-based Human Rights Activists News Agency.

"The fall of the Islamic Republic and the establishment of a secular, democratic government in Iran will not only restore dignity to my people, it will benefit the region and the world," Pahlavi said in the video.

Analysts said the platform is aimed at winning Washington's backing, not at building a domestic base. "He's trying to tick the boxes of everything that can correlate with Trump," said Danny Citrinowicz, a senior researcher with the Institute for National Security Studies and a former head of Iran research in one of the Israeli military's intelligence branches.

Trita Parsi, executive vice president of the Quincy Institute, a Washington think tank and author of several books on Iran, said the approach reveals a fundamental weakness. "He's really struggling to win Trump's approval, to give the impression he has that strong support, but it doesn't seem to be working," Parsi said.

"He's trying to get support from the U.S. government because he's not trying to do a revolution from below, but he's trying to get installed from above. That's a reflection of the lack of confidence and shows he has a real lack of a base of support," Parsi said.

## Trump's reservations

Trump has not fully embraced Pahlavi, repeatedly calling him a "nice guy" in interviews while expressing doubt about whether Iranians would accept his leadership.

"He seems very nice, but I don't know how he'd play within his own country," Trump told Reuters on Wednesday. "And we really aren't up to that point yet."

Pahlavi released his plan on X shortly after the Reuters interview. Trump added: "I don't know whether or not his country would accept his leadership, and certainly if they would, that would be fine with me."

## A platform aimed at a foreign audience

Citrinowicz said Pahlavi's decision to post the video in English rather than Farsi reflects the intended audience. Pahlavi's only realistic path to power runs through Washington, including possible U.S. military support, Citrinowicz said. Trump has appeared to walk back threats of U.S. military intervention in recent days but has not ruled it out.

How much support Pahlavi has among the public inside Iran is unclear, although he has a "small modicum," Parsi said. Citrinowicz said protesters chanting "Pahlavi" in the streets are not necessarily expressing a preference for the crown prince; nearly 50 years of theocratic rule has left little opportunity for major opposition figures to coalesce, making Pahlavi one of only a few recognizable names. Pahlavi has done little to build or strengthen an opposition movement during more than four decades in exile, Parsi added.

"They don't want to replace one dictatorship with another, and he's not a unifying figure," Citrinowicz said. "He has been trying to build himself as the role of the successor, but it's not there."

Pahlavi's agenda also aligns with the priorities of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who has maintained a close partnership with Trump. Pahlavi visited Israel in 2023 and met with Netanyahu. Both Iran and Israel are still recovering from a 12-day war last summer; Israeli strikes on Iran killed 1,190 people and wounded another 4,475, according to the Human Rights Activists News Agency, while Iran's missile barrages killed nearly 30 people in Israel and wounded 1,000.

## Historical liabilities

Pahlavi's father, Shah Mohammed Reza Pahlavi, was overthrown in the 1979 Islamic Revolution that ushered in the current Islamic Republic. Bitter memories of the elder Shah's rule and suppression of any opposition by the feared SAVAK intelligence agency, along with the perception that Pahlavi and his family have spent nearly 50 years out of touch with Iran, have limited his appeal among Iranians, analysts say.

Iranian state media has blamed "monarchist terrorist elements" for the current demonstrations. On Thursday, the Student News Network — a media agency believed to be close to the Revolutionary Guard's Basij force — broadcast street interviews dismissive of Pahlavi. "He's making a big mistake. Tell him to get lost," one person said. Another yelled, "Death to the shah!"

The death toll of 2,637 since last month exceeds that of any other round of protest or unrest in Iran in decades, according to the Human Rights Activists News Agency, which relies on a network of activists inside Iran that confirms all reported fatalities. With communications greatly limited inside the country, the Associated Press has been unable to independently confirm the figure. Iran's government has not provided overall casualty data for the demonstrations.
