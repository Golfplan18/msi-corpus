NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte may have been instrumental in persuading President Donald Trump to abandon threats of tariffs against eight European nations, according to Trump's announcement Wednesday at the World Economic Forum in Davos. Trump said he had agreed with Rutte on a "framework of a future deal" on Arctic security, appearing to represent a dramatic reversal from his previous insistence on total U.S. control of Greenland. Little is known about what the agreement entails, and Trump could change course again.

The apparent agreement marks a rare example of European diplomatic success in managing U.S. policy shifts and underscores the role of personal relationships in steering the Trump administration's approach to NATO and Arctic security.

For days, the standoff appeared unresolvable. President Donald Trump had insisted he must have Greenland and would accept nothing short of total ownership. He threatened to slap punitive tariffs on eight European nations to press his demands.

Even after Trump dropped the threat of force in a speech in Davos on Wednesday, the impasse remained.

Then Mark Rutte intervened.

The NATO secretary-general had sent Trump a flattering text message before the World Economic Forum opened. In it, Rutte addressed Trump as "Mr. President, dear Donald" and praised Trump for his diplomacy in Syria, Gaza and Ukraine, ending with: "I am committed to finding a way forward on Greenland. Can't wait to see you. Yours, Mark."

Trump published the message on his Truth Social platform shortly before departing for Davos.

## The Diplomat's Touch

At the World Economic Forum, Trump announced that he had agreed with Rutte on a "framework of a future deal" on Arctic security. The announcement represented a dramatic reversal from his insistence on total U.S. control of Greenland and potentially defused tensions with geopolitical implications for NATO.

Matthew Kroenig, vice president and senior director of the Atlantic Council's Scowcroft Center for Strategy and Security, assessed the impact. "I think Secretary-General Rutte has emerged as one of Europe's most effective diplomats and Trump whisperers," Kroenig said. "He does seem to have a way of speaking to Trump that keeps the United States and the Trump administration engaged in NATO in a constructive way."

Rutte's success with Trump revolves around his willingness to use charm and flattery while revealing little of what the two leaders discuss—a tactic he refined during thirteen years as Dutch prime minister, when he used it to manage coalition partners.

## The Making of Teflon Mark

Rutte earned the nickname "Teflon Mark" during his domination of Dutch politics. Political fallout never seemed to stick to him for long. A 2016 biography by Dutch journalist Sheila Sitalsing described him navigating the fragmented political landscape with "indestructible cheerfulness."

His record included surviving multiple scandals. Rutte and his government resigned in 2021 to take responsibility for a child care allowance scandal in which thousands of parents were wrongly accused of fraud. But he bounced back to win national elections two months later with a slightly larger share of the vote and began his fourth term.

In another test, Rutte said he could not recall being informed about a Dutch bombing of Hawija, Iraq in 2015 that killed dozens of civilians. In 2022, he survived a no-confidence motion in parliament over accusations of concealing state activity by deleting messages from his old-school Nokia cellphone.

His image helped cement his popularity. Rutte was known for riding his bicycle to work while eating an apple, behavior prized in the Netherlands as down-to-earth. When his last coalition collapsed in 2023 in a dispute over migration policy, he drove an old Saab station wagon to a royal palace to hand his resignation to King Willem-Alexander.

## NATO's New Leader

Securing the NATO chief's job demonstrated his diplomatic acumen. He managed to convince skeptics including Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orbán and Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan to back his candidacy. "It took a very long time. It's a complicated process, but it's an honor that it appears to have happened," Rutte told reporters after securing the required support.

Rutte's soft diplomatic skills were viewed as valuable assets for leading the 32-nation alliance as it faced Trump's repeated criticism while navigating how to support Ukraine in the war against Russia.

Several hours before Trump announced his reversal on Greenland, Finnish President Alexander Stubb—another European leader credited with rapport with Trump—was asked during a panel discussion on European security in Davos who or what could diffuse the tensions. "Oh, Mark Rutte," Stubb said, drawing laughter from the audience and the panel, which included Rutte himself.
