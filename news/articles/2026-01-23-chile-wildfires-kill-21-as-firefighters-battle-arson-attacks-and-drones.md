Firefighters and security forces in Chile are battling deadly forest fires that have killed at least 21 people and destroyed 45,700 hectares of woodland. The blazes, described as among the worst tragedies in the country's recent memory, have forced hundreds into makeshift shelters and destroyed 2,359 homes in the Biobío region.

The fires have exposed systemic challenges facing emergency responders—from criminal arson to public interference—that complicate containment efforts even as international support arrives to manage the disaster.

## The Disaster

The death toll rose to at least 21 by Thursday as firefighters continued battling forest fires across Chile's Biobío region. An additional 305 people were injured. Skeletal remains recovered by search teams were sent for forensic analysis.

President Gabriel Boric visited the affected region and decreed two days of national mourning. "Amid the profound sorrow caused by the forest fires in the south-central region of our country, I have decided to decree two days of national mourning," Boric said.

The current fires have already surpassed the 2024 Valparaíso and Viña del Mar fire, which destroyed 8,500 hectares and claimed 131 lives.

## Challenges on the Ground

Firefighters have faced dangers beyond the flames. At least two attacks on firefighters have been reported since the emergency began Sunday. In one case, a fire brigade was attacked with gunfire, according to a Fire Department statement. Police were investigating both cases but had not made arrests.

Boric said "any aggression against firefighters is absolutely unacceptable" and vowed enforcement: "Those who attack firefighters will be identified and punished to the full extent of the law."

Unauthorized drone flights have forced the suspension of firefighting aircraft operations around the small town of Florida. "The use of unmanned aircraft can endanger the lives of helicopter and airplane pilots working to control these fires," the National Forestry Corporation said.

Arson has been a persistent problem. At least 70 people have been arrested for causing or attempting to cause a fire during the current fire season, Boric said. On Thursday, police arrested a person accused of starting a fire in Punta de Parra, adjacent to Lirquén.

"Residents of the Punta de Parra area were alerted that there was a group of people with some materials with the intention of starting fires," said Claudia Chamorro, head of the Biobío police region.

For those on the ground, the crisis has taken an emotional toll. Pamela Crisostomo, a neighborhood association president in Lirquén, described the impact on emergency workers: "The firefighters and police worked tirelessly. It was the first time I ever saw them both cry."

A volunteer assisting affected families said: "We are going around and handling this form, which allows people to access the benefits and support provided by the State."

## International Support

Support from multiple countries has begun arriving. Mexico sent 145 firefighters who landed in Concepción on Thursday morning. Uruguay provided approximately 30 professionals and a Uruguayan air force plane. The U.S. Embassy contributed firefighting equipment delivered directly to the Chilean Fire Department. Brazil and Argentina were also among countries joining the effort.

Foreign Minister Alberto van Klaveren said Chile's government is in talks with United Nations agencies and the European Union's humanitarian aid agency for assistance. "We are not only thinking about fighting the fires, but also about reconstruction efforts," he said.
