## Trump declares Iran "dead" as commanders say war cannot be sustained

Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell confirmed Monday that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth regularly receives formal military objections from senior commanders and called such "non-concurs" standard. Parnell was responding to a Washington Post report that four four-star officers had advised against prolonging the war in Iran.

Sean Parnell, the Pentagon's chief spokesman, wrote on X that "Of course, we demand candid military advice. Non-concurs are standard. The Secretary sees them constantly. That's how you run a real military." In responding to the Post's account, Parnell did not dispute that the objections had been filed or that Hegseth fields such dissent on an ongoing basis.

The Post reported that the four four-star commanders had lodged formal "non-concurs" against a plan to keep more than 50,000 U.S. troops in the Middle East through September, with some deployments potentially running into 2027. The objections appeared in the Aug. 14 edition of the secretary of defense orders book, a classified document typically produced twice a month that tracks the availability and deployment of U.S. forces worldwide.

According to the Post, which cited people familiar with the assessment, Adm. Daryl Caudle, the chief of naval operations, told Hegseth the Navy could not sustain its current level of commitment because no end date for the operation had been set. Adm. Samuel Paparo, the chief of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, objected to the continued demand for an aircraft carrier strike group and destroyers. Gen. Alexus Grynkewich, head of U.S. European Command, and Gen. Francis L. Donovan, chief of U.S. Southern Command, said shifting ships and surveillance aircraft to Central Command was already degrading their ability to fulfill homeland-defense obligations. The Army and Air Force concurred with extending their deployments but warned of significant risks, the Post reported.

Parnell's response escalated beyond the Post's specific reporting. "Publishing highly classified assessments from the Secretary's orders book is a crime," Parnell wrote. "It's a betrayal of the force. And much of what they're publishing is inaccurate … We are in great shape around the world. This story isn't about readiness. It's TDS and PDS – they hate President Trump and Secretary Hegseth more than they love America." The Pentagon did not specify which parts of the story it considers inaccurate.

A community note attached to Parnell's post pointed out that, while leaking classified material is illegal for government employees, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in 1971 that the First Amendment protects the press's right to publish classified information of public interest.

Dan Lamothe, the Post reporter who broke the story, wrote on X that "independent journalism is not a crime" and that such reporting was necessary when officials withhold details about a war funded by taxpayers.

In a statement to the Post issued before publication, Parnell had used more measured language, saying troop-commitment decisions were "based on the current threat assessment, strategic priorities established by the President, and the advice" of military leadership. He said the department does not discuss "internal operational processes" or specific non-concurrences.

The exchange came days after U.S. and Iranian forces traded fire for the first time in roughly a month, and six weeks after Hegseth told senators the war had cost an estimated $37.5 billion, including projected costs through September.

On Monday morning, President Donald Trump wrote on Truth Social that "Iran is officially a Failed Nation. IT IS DEAD!" He listed what he described as Iran's absent or collapsed capabilities — no Navy, no Air Force, no currency, unpaid soldiers and police, and inflation at 300 percent — and accused Iran's government of killing "more than 100,000 protesters," saying its leaders "must be tried for war crimes against humanity."

Neither Trump nor Parnell explained how those declarations squared with what four of the Pentagon's own four-star commanders had put in writing: that they cannot sustain the war effort as ordered.
