President Donald Trump is establishing a Board of Peace to mediate global conflicts beyond the Israel-Hamas war in Gaza, marking the latest U.S. attempt to sidestep the United Nations Security Council and raising questions about the 80-year-old organization's relevance and future. The move comes as the U.N. has embarked on major reforms intended to modernize the organization and preserve its standing amid Trump administration threats to cut billions in funding.

## Trump Launches Board to Mediate Global Conflicts, Challenging UN Authority

President Trump said Wednesday the United Nations has "never lived up to its potential," articulating frustration that has driven him to establish the Board of Peace as a forum to mediate conflicts beyond the Israel-Hamas war in Gaza. "The U.N. just hasn't been very helpful," he told reporters at a White House briefing. "The U.N. should have settled every one of the wars that I settled."

The Board of Peace, authorized by the UN Security Council in November as a transitional mechanism to oversee a U.S.-brokered Gaza ceasefire, is now being positioned by Trump as a potential rival to the Security Council itself — a move that signals deepening skepticism about multilateral institutions that have anchored global peacemaking since the end of World War II.

## Allies Divided on the Expansion

The expansion has produced divergent responses among U.S. allies. Eight Muslim countries — Qatar, Pakistan, and the United Arab Emirates among them — accepted invitations to join the board on Wednesday, though they reaffirmed their commitment to the board's original Gaza-focused mission. France, one of five permanent Security Council members, declined outright. Russia, China, and Britain are still evaluating the invitation. Norway, Sweden, and Slovenia also declined, with Slovenia expressing concern that the board's mandate is too broad and could undermine the international order based on the UN Charter.

Retired U.S. Ambassador Robert Wood, who represented the U.S. at the United Nations under Republican and Democratic administrations, questioned whether the board could succeed as a Security Council replacement. "If Trump is trying to replace the Security Council with a Board of Peace dealing with issues beyond Gaza, I don't think there's going to be a lot of interest," Wood said. "What I would say to U.N. member states, including the United States: Let's try to work together to try to make the United Nations a better instrument. It really is the best instrument we have, given all its warts."

UN Deputy Spokesperson Farhan Haq countered that regional organizations and defense alliances have coexisted with the United Nations throughout its 80-year history without replacing it. "It's too early to tell what the Board of Peace will look like," Haq said. "There have been any number of organizations — regional organizations, defense alliances and others — that have coexisted with the U.N. over the 80 years that the U.N.'s been in existence."

## The Broader Threat to Global Cooperation

The board's formation comes as the UN has accelerated an internal restructuring effort to modernize its operations and preserve its institutional standing. The changes include consolidating major agencies, moving personnel out of its New York headquarters, and streamlining humanitarian programs. These reforms have been undertaken in response to Trump administration pressure to cut U.S. contributions to international organizations — pressure that included the U.S. refusal to pay its mandatory UN dues last year.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, speaking Tuesday at the World Economic Forum in Davos, raised concerns about the deteriorating state of global cooperation. "The multilateral institutions on which the middle powers have relied ... the very architecture of collective problem solving, are under threat," Carney said. "And as a result, many countries are drawing the same conclusions — that they must develop greater strategic autonomy, in energy, food, critical minerals, in finance and supply chains."

UN Secretary-General António Guterres, asked whether the United Nations can survive a Trump presidency, voiced confidence in the organization's future. "I have no doubt about it," he said in an interview with the BBC on Monday. "I have a lot of confidence in the future of humankind, and I'm fighting as much as I can in order to make sure the U.N. is part of that renewal that I believe will become inevitable."

Trump's criticism of the UN has centered on the Security Council's inability to prevent or resolve recent conflicts, particularly in Gaza and Ukraine. The board's expansion beyond Gaza reflects his view that the traditional multilateral framework cannot address contemporary security challenges. Whether member states will treat the board as a functional alternative or simply as a parallel institution remains to be seen as countries make their decisions.
