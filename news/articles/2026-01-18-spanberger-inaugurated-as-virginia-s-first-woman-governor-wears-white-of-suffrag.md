RICHMOND, Va. — Abigail Spanberger was sworn in as Virginia's governor on Saturday, becoming the first woman to hold the office, and she marked the occasion by setting aside more than a century of inaugural dress tradition.

Instead of the morning coat worn by her male predecessors, Spanberger wore white — a long coat with gold buttons paired with white gloves — the color that has symbolized the women's suffrage movement since the campaign for women's right to vote.

"I maintain an abiding sense of gratitude to those who work, generation after generation, to ensure women could be among those casting ballots, but who could only dream of a day like today," Spanberger said in her inaugural address.

## Tradition set aside

Virginia's inaugural dress code, as set out in "A Guide to Virginia Protocol and Traditions," calls for males in the official party to wear morning coats — formal daytime suits with long tails — and women to wear dark suits. Spanberger's husband was among those who kept to that tradition on Saturday.

Spanberger had signaled her intentions before the ceremony. In a recent interview with NPR, she said plainly that the traditional garment would not be part of her swearing-in.

"I'm not going to wear a morning coat, not to disappoint anyone," she said.

Her office did not respond to a request from the Associated Press for comment on the choice of attire.

## The suffrage connection

White became a recognized symbol of the suffrage movement in the United States and Britain in the early twentieth century, adopted by activists who fought for women's right to vote. American women secured that right with the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920.

In her address, Spanberger acknowledged that suffragists "refused to give up" and described the moment as one they could only have imagined. The visual reference to that history, through her attire on the day she took the state's highest executive office, reinforced the theme she articulated at the podium.
