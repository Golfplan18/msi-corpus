Michelle Francine Ngonmo, a 38-year-old Cameroonian-Italian, founded the Afrofashion Association a decade ago to increase representation of people of color in Italian fashion. Through runway shows, mentorship programs, and the Black Carpet Awards, the organization has worked with 3,000 people of color, with 92 advancing to sustainable professional paths in creative fields.

The Association's work gained momentum after the Black Lives Matter movement sparked a public reckoning about racial representation in Italian fashion. This month, the organization supported the Milan runway debut of Ghanaian designer Victor Reginald Bob Abbey-Hart, whose collection of mostly denim looks drew industry attention.

Despite waning media attention to diversity initiatives over recent years, the Afrofashion Association continues to focus on institutional partners that have remained committed, including the Italian National Fashion Chamber and prominent figures like Anna Wintour.

## A Decade of Work for Representation

Ngonmo's commitment to increasing representation stems from what she described as a foundational absence. "There was, let's say, a lack of representation of people like me," she said.

The organization she founded operates through multiple channels: runway shows that showcase emerging designers, mentorship of fashion students, and regular trips to Africa to work with designers there. The Black Carpet Awards, launched in 2023, recognize trailblazing achievements.

## The turning point

The movement for systemic change accelerated after the Black Lives Matter movement sparked public discussion about racial representation in Italian fashion. Designers Stella Jean and Edward Buchanan joined Ngonmo in pressing major fashion houses to move beyond public statements of solidarity to concrete action.

The trio mentored creatives of color under the catchphrase "We Are Made in Italy" for several seasons. But institutional attention and funding for diversity initiatives declined over time.

"At the time there was a reaction, indeed a very strong request to have to deal with creatives, especially Blacks in Italy," Ngonmo said. "And then slowly the curtain closed because the attention was no longer on that."

## Doubling down on committed partners

Ngonmo has shifted focus to what she sees as the institutions that remained committed. "I now focus my attention on those companies, those institutions that have remained with us during these years, and look at the result we have brought," she said.

The Italian National Fashion Chamber emerged as a key partner, backing Ngonmo's efforts and giving platforms to emerging Black designers on the Milan Fashion Week calendar. One example is Abbey-Hart, whose recent progression from the Black Carpet Awards to a featured Milan runway show demonstrates the pathway the partnership creates.

Abbey-Hart's collection this month drew prominent attendance. Carlo Capasa, president of the Milan fashion chamber, sat in the front row for the packed show, wearing one of Abbey-Hart's statuesque denim coats.

## The persistent challenge

Abbey-Hart said his early ambitions were shaped in Ghana. "I realized I want to go where it was made," he said, referencing his first encounter with a Gucci bag. "So that was the dream."

Since moving to Italy nine years ago, Abbey-Hart has built a career collaborating with Ngonmo to raise his profile. His recent work designing a denim collection for Max & Co. and his Milan debut represent significant milestones. Yet barriers remain.

"Sometimes, before you even get to the room for the interview, you've been disqualified already," Abbey-Hart said. "It's really tough, and I want people to understand. Take away the color, take away what I represent, just look at the job."

Capasa estimated that Afrofashion Association projects have given visibility and behind-the-scenes support to more than 30 designers of color during recent fashion weeks. He acknowledged the work ahead.

"There is a lot to do in diversity and inclusion everywhere in the world, for sure also in Italy," he said, adding that Ngonmo's role has been essential in helping institutions understand the needs of underrepresented communities.

Ngonmo's work has drawn support from prominent figures in international fashion. Anna Wintour, editor-in-chief of Condé Nast, has met with Black Carpet Award nominees at multiple Milan Fashion weeks, signaling institutional recognition of the Association's mission.
