## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- The Science journal study found an abrupt withdrawal of U.S. Agency for International Development resources was followed by a significant and sustained increase in conflict across Africa’s most USAID-dependent regions.
- The study’s authors said their findings show that “large-scale, sudden aid cuts can destabilize fragile settings.”
- The Science study said it does not provide evidence that more aid reduces conflict and instead reflects “the effect of a sudden and unexpected disruption.”
- The study said USAID’s shutdown interrupted contracts, staffing and aid procurement in the affected regions.
- Nathaniel Raymond of Yale’s Humanitarian Research Lab said the “experience is gone” after USAID shuttering, even if funding were later restored.
- ACLED analyst Ladd Serwat said the end of some USAID programs coincided with “increasing insurgency and spillover,” suggesting support may have helped communities face insurgent threats.

The report comes as conflict analysts say jihadist violence remains a major driver of insecurity across Africa. The Science study, published Thursday, links the timing of violence increases to the U.S. decision to dissolve USAID, a major global aid operator that had supported governments and aid groups working in conflict-affected areas.

According to the study, researchers examined whether regions that historically received the most USAID support experienced increased violence after USAID’s sudden shutdown. The authors said they found a correlation and that the increase was “significant and sustained” across “Africa’s most USAID-dependent regions,” language used in the paper.

The researchers said their results were not an argument that foreign aid reduces conflict in general. Instead, they said the study demonstrates the effect of abrupt and unexpected disruption. They wrote that “large-scale, sudden aid cuts can destabilize fragile settings,” while adding that the findings are not evidence that additional aid lowers conflict levels.

The study attributed part of the mechanism to USAID’s abrupt operational changes. It said the withdrawal interrupted contracts, staffing and aid procurement, disrupting how assistance and conflict-related programming moved to communities on the ground.

Outside researchers who were not part of the study described the concern as lasting even if money later returns. Nathaniel Raymond, executive director of the Humanitarian Research Lab at the Yale School of Public Health, said the “lasting problem with the shuttering of USAID is likely going to be that for much of its conflict prevention work, even if you put back all the money ... the experience is gone.” He added that some institutional know-how and ongoing prevention work do not restart instantly after a shutdown.

Ladd Serwat, senior Africa analyst at the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data, said some USAID programs may have worked to limit spillover from conflict zones into nearby communities. “We now see increasing insurgency and spillover, so some of those programs may have supported these communities from insurgent threats, and now they are no longer active,” Serwat said.

The study and analysts cited examples of USAID’s role in different conflict contexts across Africa, including support tied to victims of Boko Haram in Nigeria, reliance on U.S. funding in Ethiopia’s Tigray region during recovery efforts, and financial commitments in northern Ivory Coast aimed at countering al-Qaida and Islamic State. As conflict continues to draw attention from researchers and monitoring groups, the Science paper’s conclusion centers on the downstream stability effects of sudden aid cutoffs in already fragile settings.
