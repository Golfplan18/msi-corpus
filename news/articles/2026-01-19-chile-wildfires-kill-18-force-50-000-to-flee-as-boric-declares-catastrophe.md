The disaster marks the latest in a string of catastrophic summer fire seasons for Chile, where wildfires in 2024 killed at least 130 people along the central coastline — the deadliest natural disaster Chile had recorded since the 2010 earthquake. Officials warned extreme temperatures were forecast to persist through Monday with no improvement in firefighting conditions.

Wildfires racing through central and southern Chile killed at least 18 people, displaced 50,000 residents and burned 8,500 hectares (21,000 acres) of land Sunday, as temperatures surpassed 38 degrees Celsius (100 degrees Fahrenheit) and strong winds spread more than two dozen active blazes across the Biobio and Ñuble regions, Chilean authorities said.

President Gabriel Boric declared a state of catastrophe in both regions, roughly 500 kilometers (300 miles) south of Santiago — a designation that allows greater coordination with the military to contain the fires. Speaking from Concepción, the largest city in Biobio, Boric said initial tallies of 18 dead and 300 houses destroyed were almost certain to grow as the full scale of destruction came into view.

"The first priority, as you know, in these emergencies is always to fight and extinguish the fire. But we cannot forget, at any time, that there are human tragedies here, families who are suffering," Boric said. "These are difficult times."

He estimated homes affected in Biobio alone at "certainly more than a thousand, just so far." Charred bodies were found in fields, homes, along roads and in vehicles. Authorities imposed a nighttime curfew across the affected zone as thousands of displaced residents sought refuge in emergency shelters.

## Fires overtook communities overnight

The speed of the fires caught many residents unprepared. In Penco, a coastal city in Biobio, residents said the fires reached them after midnight. The blaze engulfed most of the community, burning cars, a school and a church.

"Many people didn't evacuate. They stayed in their houses because they thought the fire would stop at the edge of the forest," said John Guzmán, 55, surveying smoke-blanketed skies over Penco. "It was completely out of control. No one expected it."

Juan Lagos, 52, described his family's flight from their home: "We fled running, with the kids, in the dark."

Víctor Burboa, 54, said the community absorbed losses in real time: "From what we can see, there are people who died ... and we knew them well. Everyone here knew them."

Interior Minister Álvaro Elizalde offered little reassurance about conditions in the hours ahead. "Weather conditions for coming hours are not good and indicate extreme temperatures," he said.

## Local official publicly challenged government response time

As Boric addressed reporters in Concepción, the mayor of Penco had already gone public with sharp criticism of the government's response. Rodrigo Vera said on a local radio station: "Dear President Boric, from the bottom of my heart, I have been here for four hours, a community is burning and there is no (government) presence. How can a minister do nothing but call me to tell me that the military is going to arrive at some point?"

Boric's public address followed Vera's broadcast challenge and included an acknowledgment that human losses were ongoing even as firefighting operations continued.

## Chile's recurring summer fire crisis

Wildfires strike central and southern Chile each summer, typically peaking in February as temperatures rise and drought conditions that have persisted for years compound fire risk. Chile's 2024 fire season killed at least 130 people along the central coastline — the deadliest natural disaster in Chile since the 2010 earthquake.

Argentina's Patagonia region was also contending with active wildfires consuming thousands of acres in recent weeks amid a stretch of hot, dry weather, the Associated Press reported.
