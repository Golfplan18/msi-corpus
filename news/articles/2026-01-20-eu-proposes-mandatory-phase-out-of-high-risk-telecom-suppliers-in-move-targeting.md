The European Union's executive commission proposed legislation Tuesday that would require member states to remove telecommunications equipment from companies based in "high risk" countries from critical infrastructure networks within three years, in a move widely understood to target Chinese manufacturers Huawei and ZTE. The draft rules would also extend to security scanners at border checkpoints, water supply systems, and health and medical devices.

The proposal marks a significant escalation from previous EU guidance on 5G cybersecurity, which was voluntary and produced uneven compliance across the bloc's 27 member states — with some countries buying Chinese gear while others shunned it. If adopted by the European Parliament, the new rules would for the first time give the EU mandatory, bloc-wide authority to exclude suppliers deemed security risks.

The European Commission proposed legislation Tuesday that would require member states to remove telecommunications equipment from suppliers based in "high risk" countries from critical networks within three years, in a move widely understood to target Chinese companies Huawei and ZTE.

The draft rules would make cybersecurity requirements mandatory across the European Union's 27 member states, replacing a previous voluntary framework for 5G networks that the commission said had produced uneven results — with some countries purchasing Chinese equipment while others declined to do so.

"Our proposal is about protecting EU citizens and businesses by securing the ICT supply chains that support the critical sectors of our economy and society," European Commission Vice President Henna Virkkunen told lawmakers at the European Parliament in Strasbourg, France, which will need to approve the proposals.

## Scope extends beyond telecom

The draft legislation covers more than high-speed telecom networks. The proposed restrictions also apply to equipment in other sectors, including security scanners used at border checkpoints, water supply systems, and health and medical devices, according to the Associated Press.

The proposals do not name any countries or companies. The "high risk" designation has previously been applied by EU officials to China-based suppliers. In 2023, the European Commission said EU member states were justified in restricting or excluding Huawei and ZTE — which the United States has sanctioned — because they posed elevated security risks.

Huawei, which the AP described as the world's biggest maker of networking equipment, has long been banned from the United States. ZTE is also a U.S.-sanctioned Chinese tech company.

## Huawei pushes back

Huawei disputed the legal basis of the commission's approach in a statement issued Tuesday.

"A legislative proposal to limit or exclude non-EU suppliers based on country of origin, rather than factual evidence and technical standards, violates the EU's basic legal principles of fairness, non-discrimination, and proportionality, as well as its WTO obligations," the company said.

Huawei also said that as a "legally operating company in Europe," it reserves the right to safeguard its "legitimate interests."

The proposals must be approved by the European Parliament before taking effect. EU officials have described the measures as a response to concerns about the bloc's vulnerability both to the dominance of Chinese high-tech manufacturing and to U.S. Big Tech services in its digital infrastructure.
