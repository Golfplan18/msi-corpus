A Barcelona commuter train crashed on January 20 after a retaining wall collapsed onto the tracks, killing at least 1 and injuring 37, according to Spanish regional authorities. The accident occurred two days after a separate train collision in southern Spain that left at least 42 people dead and dozens more injured, marking an unusually deadly period for a rail network Spain considers among Europe's most reliable.

The two accidents within 48 hours prompted Spain's government to announce three days of mourning and raised questions about infrastructure maintenance and safety oversight on commuter lines plagued by reliability issues, even as the high-speed network maintained its reputation for safety.

## Barcelona Crash Kills One; Two Trains Derail Near Gelida

On January 20, a commuter train near Barcelona struck a collapsed retaining wall that had fallen across the tracks, according to Spanish regional authorities. The crash killed at least one person and injured 37 others, with five sustaining serious injuries.

The accident occurred in Gelida, about 35 minutes outside Barcelona in Catalonia, northeastern Spain. Spain's railway operator ADIF said heavy rainfall that swept across the region this week likely caused the retaining wall to collapse. Service on the commuter line was suspended after the crash.

Rescue workers said most of the injured were passengers in the first train car. Twenty ambulances were dispatched to the site, and the injured were taken to hospitals across the region.

## Two Days Earlier: A Far Deadlier Collision in Southern Spain

The Barcelona accident came two days after a far more severe collision in Spain's south. On January 18, a train carrying 289 passengers from Malaga to Madrid derailed at 7:45 p.m. and collided with an incoming train traveling from Madrid to Huelva. The collision killed at least 42 people.

The front of the second train bore the brunt of the impact. The first two carriages were knocked off the tracks and down a 4-meter slope, with bodies discovered hundreds of meters from the crash site. By Tuesday, 39 people remained hospitalized while 83 others had been treated and discharged.

One survivor, Emil Johnson, a Swedish citizen, described the moment of impact. "It was probably two, three seconds. And everything was broken," he said from a wheelchair, nursing injuries to his ribs and back. "When we crashed, I didn't know who was alive and who was dead."

Among the survivors was a 6-year-old girl who emerged virtually unscathed while her parents, brother, and cousin all perished. The mayor of the family's hometown called her survival a miracle.

Fidel Sáez lost his mother in the collision but said his brother and two children survived. His brother, he recounted, "told me that it was a miracle that he is alive. He had to get the children through a window."

## Investigators Focus on Track Damage and Train Maintenance

Officials said both trains were traveling well under the speed limit of 250 kilometers per hour, and human error has been ruled out as a cause. Investigators found a broken section of track near where the derailment began, though they said determining whether it was a cause or a consequence of the crash could take weeks.

"All hypotheses are open," Interior Minister Fernando Grande Marlaska told reporters. Accident investigators were analyzing the rails and planning to inspect the wheels of the first train in a laboratory.

The train that derailed belonged to Iryo, a private company. Iryo said the train was manufactured in 2022 and had passed a safety check on January 15. The second train belonged to Spain's public railway company, Renfe.

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez acknowledged both accidents, writing on social media: "All my affection and solidarity with the victims and their families."

## National Mourning and Ongoing Recovery

Spain announced three days of national mourning as rescue operations continued at the January 18 crash site. Spain's King Felipe and Queen Letizia visited the accident scene, where they greeted emergency workers and residents who aided in rescue efforts. The queen told reporters afterward: "We are all responsible for not looking away when the debris of a catastrophe is being cleared away."

Emergency personnel were still searching for more victims and collecting DNA samples from families of the missing, with some bodies remaining unidentified.

The accidents struck a country that leads Europe in high-speed rail mileage and takes considerable pride in its advanced rail network. Though high-speed trains have maintained a strong safety record, the commuter rail service has long faced reliability challenges.

High-speed rail service resumed Tuesday from Madrid to Seville and Malaga, though passengers were required to travel a portion of the journey by bus. Officials said normal train service would not resume until early February. Spanish airline Iberia added flights to southern cities, and bus companies reinforced their services to accommodate stranded travelers.
