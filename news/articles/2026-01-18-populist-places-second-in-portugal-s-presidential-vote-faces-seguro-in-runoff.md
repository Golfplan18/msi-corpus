Portugal’s presidential election went to a runoff after a hard-right populist leader placed second and will face center-left candidate António José Seguro in a Feb. 8 vote, according to near-complete results reported by The Associated Press.

With almost 98% of votes counted, André Ventura, leader of the Chega party that he founded less than seven years ago, captured 24% and finished second. Seguro led with almost 31%, setting up the two-way contest on Feb. 8.

The runoff winner will replace President Marcelo Rebelo de Sousa, who has served the limit of two five-year terms.

Ventura’s second-place result added another milestone to a broader shift in Europe’s politics, the AP said, as populist parties have gained ground in recent years. Chega’s rise also accelerated quickly: the AP said it became the second-largest party in Portugal’s parliament last year, just six years after it was founded.

The AP reported that the Sunday election included a record field with nine other candidates. None won the more than 50% needed for a first-round victory.

Ventura has made immigration a central campaign focus. In the AP account, Ventura used messaging that included billboards reading “Portugal is ours,” “This isn’t Bangladesh,” and “Immigrants shouldn’t be allowed to live on welfare.”

The AP reported that while Ventura’s campaign leaned on anti-immigrant sentiment, voters appeared more focused on a housing crisis and the cost of living.

Portugal’s political backdrop has also been turbulent. The AP said that last May the country held its third general election in three years, describing it as the worst stretch of instability for decades, with “steadying the ship” presented as a key challenge for the next president.

The incoming president is also expected to face policy decisions as the office mediates between political forces. The AP said a law permitting euthanasia and physician-assisted suicide was approved by parliament in 2022 but has been held up by constitutional objections, and that it will likely come to the president for approval.

In Portugal, the AP described the presidency as largely ceremonial, with no executive power. It said the head of state aims to stand above day-to-day politics by mediating disputes and defusing tensions, while also retaining tools including a veto that can be overturned.

The AP also said the president has the power—described in Portuguese political jargon as an “atomic bomb”—to dissolve parliament and call early elections. It added that political events in Portugal have little bearing on the broader direction of the European Union, noting the country’s small economy within the bloc and modest armed forces.

As the Feb. 8 runoff approaches, the AP said the winner will serve a five-year term at the president’s Lisbon residence along the Tagus, referred to as the riverside “pink palace.”
