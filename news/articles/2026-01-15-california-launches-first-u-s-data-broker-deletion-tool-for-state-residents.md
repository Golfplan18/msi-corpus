California residents can now request removal of their personal information from more than 500 data brokers through a new state-run platform that took effect January 1. The Delete Request and Opt-out Platform, known as DROP, is the first tool of its kind in the United States, the California Privacy Protection Agency said. The platform is available at [privacy.ca.gov/drop](https://privacy.ca.gov/drop).

Data brokers collect and sell personal records — including browsing history, location data, and health information — typically without consumers' knowledge or consent. DROP gives California residents a single portal to demand deletion from registered brokers, though an eight-month gap between enrollment and enforcement means removals will not begin until August 1.

## How to use DROP

The platform asks users to verify California residency before filing a deletion request. That verification requires a name, date of birth, address, and a phone number or email address to receive a confirmation code. Users may alternatively verify through a login.gov account backed by a passport, driver's license, or state ID.

Residents can add multiple name variations — a previous or maiden name, for instance — to improve the match rate against broker databases. The platform also accepts optional "unique identifier" fields, including mobile advertising IDs for iOS or Android devices, connected-television IDs, and vehicle identification numbers. Most people can skip those fields and add them later.

"The more information you provide, the more likely your personal data will be deleted," the DROP website states.

## Enforcement timeline

People who enroll now are placing themselves in a queue ahead of the compliance deadline. Data brokers registered in California will not be required to begin processing deletion requests until August 1, and after that they must honor requests on a 45-day cycle. Brokers that fail to comply face fines of $200 a day.

Residents who move, change phones, or buy a vehicle after filing can return to update their profiles and submit a fresh deletion request at any time.

## What data brokers hold

Data brokers sweep up personal records bought from businesses, scraped from websites, and captured through mobile tracking tools. Information they hold can include online purchase history, web browsing records, employment data, IP addresses, and more sensitive material such as Social Security numbers, driver's license details, precise geolocation, and health and sexual-orientation information. Deletion requests under DROP will also cover so-called "inferred preferences" — assumptions about a consumer derived from the collected data.

The California Privacy Protection Agency said buyers of this information range from landlords and insurance companies to "malicious actors" including fraudsters, hate groups, domestic abusers, and hostile foreign governments.

## Limits of the new tool

Privacy advocates welcomed DROP while noting its boundaries. Hayley Tsukayama, director of state affairs at the Electronic Frontier Foundation, said the platform should produce tangible results for many users.

"That's kind of my hope, because those are the sorts of things that are getting sold and that's kind of the most tangible way to grasp what a data broker is," Tsukayama said of expected reductions in spam texts and emails.

She identified two significant gaps, however. DROP covers only data brokers registered in California, and certain data categories are exempt — among them public records for vehicle and real estate ownership and credit-rating information. More broadly, Tsukayama contrasted the law with the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation, which places stricter limits on collection itself.

"The law itself doesn't address (data) collection that strongly," Tsukayama said.
