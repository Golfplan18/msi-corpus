Ugandan President Yoweri Museveni claimed his seventh consecutive term Saturday after official results gave him 71.65% of the vote in an election his main challenger rejected as fraudulent, conducted under a days-long internet blackout and marred by the failure of biometric voting machines in opposition strongholds.

Bobi Wine — the musician and politician whose legal name is Kyagulanyi Ssentamu — received 24.72% of the vote, according to the Electoral Commission. Wine rejected the outcome as "fake" and called on Ugandans to protest peacefully "until the rightful results are announced."

Security forces maintained what police described as "controlled access" around Wine's home after what the challenger said was a nighttime raid by armed personnel.

The result extends the four-decade grip on power of Museveni, 81, who has removed constitutional term limits and age restrictions to remain in office and faces no serious rivals within his own party. Uganda has not witnessed a peaceful transfer of presidential power since independence from British colonial rule six decades ago.

## Biometric machines fail in opposition areas

Biometric voter identification machines failed on election day Thursday, causing delays in urban areas including the capital, Kampala, that are opposition strongholds. Polling officials reverted to hard-copy voter registers after the failure — a setback for pro-democracy advocates who had long demanded the machines as a check on fraud.

Museveni said he agreed with the electoral commission's decision to revert to paper records. Wine alleged the breakdown was exploited for "massive ballot stuffing" and that his party's polling agents were abducted to give the ruling party an unfair advantage.

The failure of the machines is likely to form the basis of any legal challenge to the official results. Wine has not said whether he will pursue a court challenge. Courts in Uganda have previously declined to nullify Museveni's election victories, though they have recommended electoral reforms.

## Observers divided

The head of the African Union observer mission, former Nigerian President Goodluck Jonathan, told journalists Saturday that the mission did not find "any evidence of ballot stuffing" in the polling stations his team observed. He urged electoral authorities to test biometric machines in advance to prevent future failures.

Local observers were more critical. Livingstone Sewanyana, head of the Foundation for Human Rights Initiative, a civic group in Kampala, said the election climate was characterized by "fear and tension among the electorate, and some people just chose not to participate in the process."

Voter turnout stood at 52%, the lowest since Uganda's return to multi-party politics in 2006.

## Internet shutdown and security crackdown

The Uganda Communications Commission directed internet service providers to suspend access from Tuesday through late Saturday, citing an unspecified threat to national security. The directive lacked legal weight absent a formal state of emergency, but service providers complied. The shutdown disrupted a range of businesses, from sports betting shops to ride-hailing drivers.

Security forces were a constant presence throughout the campaign. Wine said authorities followed him and used tear gas against his supporters; he campaigned in a flak jacket and helmet due to what he described as security fears.

Wine said armed personnel stormed his home Friday night. Police spokesperson Kituuma Rusoke said Wine was "not under arrest" and was free to leave, but that there was "controlled access" to prevent the property from being used to incite violence.

## Museveni's consolidation of power

Museveni has governed Uganda since 1986, systematically removing the legal obstacles to his continued rule. The last constitutional constraints — term limits and age restrictions — have been amended away. Veteran opposition figure Kizza Besigye, a four-time presidential candidate, remains in prison on treason charges he says are politically motivated.

Yusuf Serunkuma, an academic and columnist for the local Observer newspaper, told the Associated Press that Wine "didn't stand a chance" against Museveni, who appoints the electoral commission.

"He has quite successfully emasculated the opposition," Serunkuma said of Museveni.

Even with Wine's challenge, Museveni faced what Serunkuma described as "one of the weakest oppositions" in recent times, in part because opposition figures are not unified while the president retains undisputed command of his party and the armed forces.
