Greenland's strategic importance stems from its location on the shortest routes that intercontinental ballistic missiles from China and Russia would take in a nuclear exchange, and from the early warning radar stationed there that gives the United States critical detection time. Defense specialists, however, dispute Trump's reasoning that U.S. ownership is necessary.

President Donald Trump said Wednesday that the United States must own Greenland to defend it, citing his "Golden Dome" missile defense system. The Arctic territory, currently under Danish sovereignty, sits on the flight paths that nuclear-armed missiles from China and Russia would take toward the United States in a hypothetical nuclear exchange. Trump's push for U.S. ownership has alarmed European allies, with the president saying the multibillion-dollar defense system must be operational before his term ends in 2029.

"Because of The Golden Dome, and Modern Day Weapons Systems, both Offensive and Defensive, the need to ACQUIRE is especially important," Trump said in a Truth Social post Saturday.

Trump addressed the global importance of the territory at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland on Wednesday. "If there is a war, much of the action will take place on that piece of ice," Trump said. "Think of it: those missiles would be flying right over the center."

## Arctic flight paths and nuclear strategy

Nuclear-armed missiles fired between superpowers tend to follow the shortest direct route on ballistic trajectories, meaning missiles launched from China or Russia toward the United States would traverse the Arctic region. Russian Topol-M missiles from the Tatishchevo silo complex southeast of Moscow would fly high over Greenland if targeted at U.S. ICBM forces stationed at Minot Air Force Base in North Dakota, Malmstrom Air Force Base in Montana, and Warren Air Force Base in Wyoming. Chinese Dong Feng-31 missiles, fired from silo fields the U.S. Defense Department has documented in China, could similarly overfly Greenland if targeted at the U.S. Eastern Seaboard.

## Pituffik Space Base and early warning

The Pituffik Space Base in Greenland hosts an array of early-warning radars that serve as the Pentagon's primary means of detecting incoming missile attacks over the Arctic. Located roughly halfway between Washington and Moscow and above the Arctic Circle, the facility operates an AN/FPS-132 radar that can detect and track ballistic missile launches from submarines and ground sites. The radar beams out nearly 3,450 miles in a 240-degree arc and can detect objects no larger than a small car at its maximum range, the U.S. Air Force says.

The facility was renamed in 2023 from Thule Air Base to Pituffik, using the remote location's Greenlandic name in recognition of the Indigenous community that was forcibly displaced when the U.S. constructed the outpost in 1951. The United States has operated at Pituffik for decades under a 1951 defense agreement with Denmark, which holds sovereignty over Greenland.

## Defense experts challenge Trump's logic

Trump contended that the U.S. cannot effectively defend Greenland on a leased basis. "You can't defend it on a lease," he said at Davos. Defense specialists struggle to reconcile this argument with the fact that the U.S. has operated Pituffik successfully for more than 70 years without owning the territory.

Etienne Marcuz, a French nuclear defense specialist and former nuclear defense worker for France's Defense Ministry, questioned the logic of Trump's acquisition argument. Marcuz noted that Trump has not suggested the United States needs to acquire the United Kingdom, despite Britain's Royal Air Force operating an early warning radar at Fylingdales in northern England that serves both the U.K. and U.S. governments and scans northward toward the polar region.

"Trump's argument that Greenland is vital for the Golden Dome—and therefore that it has to be invaded, well, acquired—is false for several reasons," Marcuz said. "One of them is that there is, for example, a radar in the United Kingdom, and to my knowledge there is no question of invading the U.K."

Marcuz also pointed to emerging technology that could diminish Greenland's importance. Space-based sensors already being tested and deployed could reduce the U.S. dependence on its Greenland-based radar station, he said, potentially undermining Trump's assertion that Greenland is uniquely essential to the Golden Dome system.

Pavel Podvig, a Geneva-based analyst specializing in Russia's nuclear arsenal, acknowledged Greenland's strategic value. "That gives the United States more time to think about what to do," Podvig said. "Greenland is a good location for that."

## Strained relations with Denmark

Before Trump escalated his campaign for Greenland, Denmark—as Greenland's sovereign state—would have readily accepted virtually any American military request for expanded facilities on the island, according to Marcuz. "Denmark was the most compliant ally of the United States," he said. "Now, it's very different. I don't know whether authorization would be granted, but in any case, before, the answer was 'Yes.'"

Trump announced Wednesday a "framework of a future deal" on Arctic security, though analysts said this announcement is unlikely to represent a final resolution to his push for U.S. control of Greenland.
