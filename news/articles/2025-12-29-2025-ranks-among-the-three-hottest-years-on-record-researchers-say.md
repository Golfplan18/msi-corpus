Climate researchers on Tuesday declared that 2025 ranked among the three hottest years ever recorded, saying continued fossil fuel emissions drove global temperatures to record heights even as a La Niña cooling pattern was present in the Pacific. The findings came from World Weather Attribution, a scientific consortium that analyzed extreme weather events worldwide throughout the year. Researchers also said the three-year global temperature average broke through the 1.5 degrees Celsius limit set by the 2015 Paris Agreement for the first time — a threshold scientists warn, if persistently exceeded, could trigger cascading and potentially irreversible environmental harm worldwide.

The report arrives weeks after United Nations climate talks in Brazil closed without any explicit commitment to phase out fossil fuels, underscoring a widening gap between scientific warnings and the pace of the international policy response.

## The findings

The WWA report, released Tuesday, said temperatures remained elevated through 2025 even with a La Niña pattern present — the periodic natural cooling of Pacific Ocean waters that influences weather globally. Researchers attributed the continued heat to greenhouse gas emissions produced by the ongoing burning of oil, gas, and coal.

"If we don't stop burning fossil fuels very, very, quickly, very soon, it will be very hard to keep that goal" of limiting warming, Friederike Otto, co-founder of World Weather Attribution and a climate scientist at Imperial College London, told the Associated Press. "The science is increasingly clear."

WWA scientists identified 157 extreme weather events in 2025 that met the group's severity criteria — events causing more than 100 deaths, affecting more than half of an area's population, or prompting a state of emergency. They closely analyzed 22 of those events.

Heat waves ranked as the deadliest category of extreme weather in 2025, according to the report. Researchers said some 2025 heat waves were 10 times more likely to occur than they would have been a decade ago because of climate change.

"The heat waves we have observed this year are quite common events in our climate today, but they would have been almost impossible to occur without human-induced climate change," Otto said. "It makes a huge difference."

## A year of compounding disasters

Extreme weather struck communities across multiple continents throughout the year. Prolonged drought contributed to wildfires that scorched Greece and Turkey. Torrential rains and flooding in Mexico killed dozens of people and left many more missing. Super Typhoon Fung-wong forced more than a million people to evacuate in the Philippines. Monsoon rains brought severe flooding and landslides across India.

The report singled out Hurricane Melissa as an illustration of how rapid storm intensification is outpacing the capacity of governments and communities to respond. The storm intensified so quickly it complicated forecasting and planning, the researchers said, then struck Jamaica, Cuba, and Haiti with enough severity that the small island nations struggled to manage the scale of damage and loss.

WWA researchers warned that increasingly frequent and severe events were pushing millions of people toward what the scientists called "limits of adaptation" — the point at which populations lack sufficient warning, time, and resources to respond effectively.

## Climate negotiations and policy divergence

UN climate talks held in Brazil in November ended without any explicit plan to move away from fossil fuels. Participating governments pledged more funding to help countries adapt to climate change, though negotiators said implementation would require additional time.

Otto said political conditions have not matched the scale of the scientific findings.

"The geopolitical weather is very cloudy this year with a lot of policymakers very clearly making policies for the interest of the fossil fuel industry rather than for the populations of their countries," she said. "And we have a huge amount of mis- and disinformation that people have to deal with."

National responses varied across major economies. China has rapidly expanded solar and wind capacity while continuing to invest in coal. Some European nations have accelerated clean-energy action in response to more frequent extreme weather, while others have cited concerns about economic growth. The Trump administration has steered U.S. policy away from clean energy in favor of measures supporting coal, oil, and gas.

Andrew Kruczkiewicz, a senior researcher at the Columbia University Climate School who was not involved in the WWA work, said communities are encountering disasters they have no prior experience managing, that extreme events are intensifying faster, and that the events are growing more complex — requiring earlier warnings and new approaches to response and recovery.

"On a global scale, progress is being made," Kruczkiewicz said, "but we must do more."
