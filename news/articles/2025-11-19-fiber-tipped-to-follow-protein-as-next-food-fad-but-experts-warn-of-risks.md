Fiber is poised to become the food industry's next major marketing focus, following years of protein-enriched products flooding U.S. supermarket shelves, according to nutrition scientists and food industry executives. PepsiCo CEO Ramon Laguarta told investors he expects fiber to follow protein as the nutrient consumers seek most. But registered dietitians and Cornell University researchers are cautioning that the emerging "fibermaxxing" trend — a push to meet or exceed daily recommended fiber intake — carries real risks if pursued too aggressively.

Most Americans already fall well short of the recommended daily fiber intake, and research on whether fiber added to processed foods delivers the same benefits as fiber from whole foods remains unsettled, scientists say — a distinction with significant consequences as the industry moves to capitalize on renewed consumer interest.

## The industry moves in

Hundreds of social media videos celebrate fiber's benefits, and market research firm Mintel reported a significant uptick in new U.S. products promoted with high or added fiber in 2025. The shift echoes the protein craze that produced protein-enhanced Pop-Tarts and Starbucks drinks.

"I think fiber will be the next protein," Laguarta said during a recent investor conference call. "Consumers are starting to understand that fiber is the benefit that they need."

Debbie Petitpain, a registered dietitian nutritionist and spokeswoman for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, said rising use of GLP-1 weight loss drugs may be one driver of the renewed focus. GLP-1 drugs slow digestion naturally, and fiber can help prevent the constipation that results. She said fiber has seen similar spikes of interest following high-fat diets such as Atkins or keto.

## What fiber does

Fiber is a carbohydrate found in plants that the human body cannot break down. It feeds gut bacteria and moves food through the digestive system. Studies show it lowers cholesterol levels, regulates blood sugar, and promotes weight loss, since high-fiber foods tend to make eaters feel more full. The American Heart Association says fiber may also protect against heart disease, diabetes, diverticulitis, and colon cancer.

There are two main types. Soluble fiber dissolves in water and forms a gel-like material that feeds gut bacteria; it is found in oats, peas, beans, apples, and carrots. Insoluble fiber does not dissolve in water and moves food through the digestive system; it is found in whole wheat flour, popcorn, wheat bran, nuts, and potatoes.

"Folks don't want to talk about it at a dinner party," Petitpain said of fiber's unglamorous reputation compared with protein.

## A significant shortfall

Under USDA dietary guidelines, adults should aim for 14 grams of fiber per 1,000 calories consumed — roughly 25 grams per day for women and 38 grams per day for men. Petitpain said Americans generally consume only about two-thirds of that amount.

Sander Kersten, director of the Division of Nutritional Sciences at Cornell University, said most people in Western countries could benefit from more fiber because their diets are low in vegetables, fruits, and whole grains.

For reference, one cup of raspberries contains 8 grams of fiber; a banana contains 3.2 grams; one-half cup of avocado contains 5 grams; and one cup of lima beans contains 13.2 grams, according to the USDA.

## The caution: whole foods vs. added fiber

Kersten said the long-term studies documenting fiber's health benefits focused on consumption of whole foods, not packaged products with fiber added to them.

"The way it is consumed as an additive and part of a diet that doesn't contain a lot of fiber may be different than a naturally fiber-rich diet," Kersten said. "You can eat a very processed, Western diet and consume foods that are enriched, but we don't know if it confers the same benefit."

Whole foods also provide benefits beyond fiber counts, Petitpain added. An apple, for example, contains 4.8 grams of fiber alongside water, vitamins, and minerals.

## Risks of moving too fast

As MSI previously reported on the cautions nutrition experts are raising around this trend, <a href="/articles/2025-11-19-nutrition-experts-urge-caution-as-fibermaxxing-trend-builds">fibermaxxing carries digestive risks that practitioners say are being underplayed in social media promotion</a>.

Kersten said there is no defined upper limit for fiber intake, but increasing consumption quickly can cause painful gas and bloating. Petitpain advised increasing fiber intake gradually and drinking plenty of water.

"You're feeding gut bacteria a food, and you can't break it down. You rely on them, and if you give them second, third and fourth servings, there's not enough of them to handle the extra load," Petitpain said.

Certain populations should exercise additional caution, Petitpain said. People sensitive to gluten or allergic to soy, shellfish, or psyllium husk should read labels carefully, since some fiber-enriched products contain those ingredients.

More broadly, Kersten questioned the pattern of concentrating on a single nutrient — whether protein or fiber.

"We don't eat nutrients, we eat foods. Ultimately, what you want to be striving for is a healthy diet, and you should choose foods that are considered to be an important part of a healthy diet," he said.
