Candles, incense, and fireplaces are fixtures of the holiday season, but a Johns Hopkins physician says the flames that conjure festive atmosphere also release chemicals that can degrade indoor air quality and trigger respiratory symptoms. Dr. Meredith McCormack, director of pulmonary and critical care medicine at Johns Hopkins University's medical school, recommends moderation and practical precautions rather than abandoning seasonal traditions. "Clean air is fragrance free," McCormack said. "If having seasonal scents is part of your tradition or evokes feelings of nostalgia, maybe think about it in moderation."

People in the Northern Hemisphere spend more time indoors during the winter holidays, and the American Lung Association says indoor air can be significantly more polluted than outdoor air because pollutants concentrate without proper ventilation or filtration — a risk that falls most heavily on children, older adults, and people with heart or lung conditions.

## What goes into the air

Active fireplaces and gas appliances release tiny airborne particles and nitrogen dioxide, a major component of smog, according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. Cleaning products, air fresheners, and candles also emit pollutants at varying concentrations.

McCormack, who has studied air quality and lung health for more than 20 years, said the risk depends on the source of the pollutant, the length and intensity of a person's exposure, and individual health. She noted that some pollutants have no smell, meaning unscented products can still affect indoor air quality.

## Who is most at risk

Polluted air affects everyone, but not equally. Children, older adults, minority populations, and people of low socioeconomic status are more likely to be affected by poor air quality, the EPA said, because of physiological vulnerabilities or higher cumulative exposure.

Children are particularly susceptible because of their lung size, McCormack said, which means they receive a greater dose of pollutants relative to their body size. People with heart or lung conditions, including asthma, face elevated hazard from indoor pollutants as well.

"The more risk factors you have, the more harmful air pollution or poor air quality indoors can be," McCormack said.

Signs of respiratory irritation include coughing, shortness of breath, headaches, a runny nose, and sneezing. Experts advise stopping use of pollutant-releasing products or immediately ventilating a room when symptoms appear.

## Practical steps to reduce exposure

McCormack recommends several measures to lower exposure without forgoing seasonal rituals: buy products with fewer ingredients; open windows when temperatures permit; use air purifiers equipped with HEPA filters; switch on kitchen exhaust fans before using a gas stovetop; and use back burners so the vent can more easily capture rising pollutants.

She also recommends setting boundaries with guests who smoke cigarettes or other tobacco products indoors.

On candle composition, experts note that all lit candles give off air pollutants regardless of whether they are made from soy-based wax or petroleum-based paraffin.

Rachael Lewis-Abbott, a member of the Indoor Air Quality Association — an organization for professionals who identify and address air quality problems — said most people do not notice what they are breathing until a serious problem such as a gas leak or mold develops.

"It is out of sight, out of mind," Lewis-Abbott said.

McCormack offered an analogy for incremental improvement. "Small improvements in air quality can have measurable health benefits," she said. "Similarly to if we exercise and eat a little better, we can be healthier."
