Valentino Garavani, the Italian fashion designer whose signature red became synonymous with elegance and whose authority as a couturier transcended decades of fashion cycles, died Tuesday at his residence in Rome. He was 93.

The Valentino Garavani and Giancarlo Giammetti Foundation announced his death on the opening day of Paris Fashion Week menswear, where the news cast a shadow over the event that bears his imprint.

Garavani's death marks the end of an era in fashion history — the passing of one of the last of a generation whose names could carry a global house and whose authority came from permanence rather than viral speed. His influence shaped not only the clientele of a great house but the very definition of couture elegance across generations.

## Bridge Between Rome and Paris

Valentino was a rare figure in fashion history: Italian by origin but fluent in the rituals that made Paris couture an institution. Trained in Paris, he founded his maison in Rome, positioning himself as a bridge between the two capitals of elegance.

His career moved between Rome and Paris, bringing Roman grandeur into a system that still treated fashion not only as commerce but as ceremony. Even as he aged, the designer kept turning up at his house's couture and ready-to-wear shows, radiating what observers described as quiet grandeur from his front-row seat before eventually retreating from public life.

"He was one of the last big couturiers who really embodied what was fashion in the 20th century," said Pierre Groppo, fashion editor-in-chief at Vanity Fair France.

## The End of a Generation

Fashion writer Luke Leitch framed the loss in terms of historical significance, calling Valentino "the last of the fashion leviathans of that generation." It marked the end of a generation of designers whose names could carry a global house and whose authority came from permanence rather than viral speed.

"There are some people who want to be Yves Saint Laurent, Chanel," said Guy-Claude Agboton, deputy editor of Ideat magazine. "There are also people who are spontaneously Valentino. It's a question of identity."

Fashion observer Arfan Ghani pointed to what Valentino represented to younger designers: a standard of restraint in an era that often rewards noise. "Because it was very classical materials," Ghani said. "It wasn't as loud as a lot of other of these brands are with branding."

## Aesthetic Authority

Ranti Bam, a Paris-based sculptor, described Valentino in the language of form rather than fashion. "As a sculptor I saw Valentino as an artist," Bam said. "He transcended fashion into sculpture. He didn't follow trends, he pursued form. That's why his work doesn't date, it endures."

His influence extended across decades through his designs for prominent clients. He dressed Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis and Elizabeth Taylor, among others, while establishing his signature color — known as Valentino red — as firmly in the public imagination as any color has ever been associated with a single house.

## Partnership and Legacy

Through his decades-long partnership with Giancarlo Giammetti, Valentino transformed the designer himself into part of the spectacle, as recognizable to the fashion world as the clients who sat in his front row.

Fashion observer Benedict Epinay described attending the designer's final show. "It was such a great moment," Epinay said. "I was lucky enough to attend the last show he gave. It was so moving because we knew at that time it was the last show."

The fashion house Valentino has continued under a new generation of leadership and design, still showcased in Paris. But for observers and industry figures present at the fashion week event, the designer's death marked a watershed moment in fashion history.
