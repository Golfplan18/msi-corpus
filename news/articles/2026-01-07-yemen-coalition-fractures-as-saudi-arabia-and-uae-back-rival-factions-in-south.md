Yemen's anti-Houthi coalition fractured into open conflict in early January 2026, as UAE-backed southern separatists seized oil-rich territory and Saudi Arabia responded with airstrikes on a Yemeni port city, exposing a deepening rift between two Gulf powers that had nominally been on the same side for a decade. The Southern Transitional Council, the most powerful armed group in southern Yemen, advanced into the Hadramout and Mahra governorates before Saudi-backed forces pushed it back. Yemen's internationally recognized government then expelled the STC's leader and charged him with treason; he subsequently fled the country.

The clashes mark the sharpest open breakdown yet between coalition partners assembled in 2015 to counter Iran-backed Houthi rebels, laying bare the competing territorial and revenue interests that have long strained the coalition's Saudi-UAE core.

Yemen's separatist Southern Transitional Council seized oil-rich territory in the country's south last month, triggering Saudi airstrikes on a Yemeni port city and setting off a chain of events that has left the decade-old anti-Houthi coalition in its most fractured state since it formed.

The STC — a UAE-backed group that controls most of southern Yemen, including key port cities and islands, and seeks an independent South Yemen — advanced into the Hadramout and Mahra governorates, areas previously held by Saudi-backed forces, breaking a fragile ceasefire among coalition members. Tensions sharpened further when a UAE arms shipment for the STC arrived at the port city of Mukalla. Saudi Arabia responded with airstrikes on Mukalla, and Saudi-backed forces pushed the STC back. The anti-Houthi coalition subsequently told the UAE to withdraw its forces from Yemen; the UAE said over the weekend that it had done so.

## STC leader expelled, charged with treason

Yemen's internationally recognized government — the Presidential Leadership Council, formed in 2022 to fight the Houthis — expelled STC leader Aidarous al-Zubaidi and charged him with treason after he reportedly declined Wednesday to personally attend de-escalation talks Saudi Arabia had convened in Riyadh, sending a delegation in his place.

The Saudi military said in a statement Thursday that al-Zubaidi had fled Yemen by boat to Somalia, where the UAE later flew him to Abu Dhabi. The STC said it maintained that al-Zubaidi had remained in Aden, Yemen's interim capital, to carry out his duties.

The STC said Wednesday it had lost contact with its Riyadh delegation. On Thursday, an STC member who heads the group's negotiation and reconciliation committee said he had met the Saudi ambassador to Yemen, describing the encounter as "fruitful."

## Saudi-backed forces retake ground

The Saudi-backed National Shield Forces regained most territory the STC had seized, deploying across Aden and oil-rich areas of Hadramout and al-Mahra. Salem al-Khanbashi, the governor of Hadramout, was appointed by the government to command the Saudi-led forces. PLC head Rashad al-Alimi said the NSF had achieved "record success" in taking back all military and security sites.

The STC's advance had itself been partly opportunistic. The Saudi-backed Hadramout Tribes Confederacy had seized the PetroMasila oil facility in November while pressing for a larger share of oil revenues and improved services for Hadramout residents. The STC apparently used that seizure as a pretext to advance and take the oil facilities for itself.

## A coalition built on mismatched interests

Yemen's civil war began in September 2014 when the Houthis — an Iran-backed movement — seized the capital, Sanaa, and much of northern Yemen. A Saudi-Emirati-led coalition backed by the United States intervened in 2015, with the stated goal of restoring Yemen's internationally recognized government. By 2022 the conflict had reached a stalemate: the Houthis held the country's most populous areas, including Sanaa, while the coalition controlled the south.

The coalition's members have always been a loose grouping with differing motivations and backers. Saudi Arabia has sought to restore the central government; the UAE has backed separatist and regional forces pursuing their own agendas. The December clashes made those diverging interests plain, exposing strained ties between the two Gulf states that sit at the coalition's core.
