The currency switch deepens Bulgaria's ties to the European Union nearly two decades after the country joined the bloc in 2007 as one of its poorest members — but the milestone arrives as a government resignation over anti-corruption protests has left Bulgaria without an approved budget, complicating the transition and fueling public anxiety stoked by nationalist and pro-Russian political forces.

Cash machines in Sofia dispensed brand-new euro banknotes for the first time on New Year's Day after Bulgaria formally joined the eurozone as the currency union's 21st member, marking a historic step in the former communist nation's decades-long integration into European institutions.

The lev, Bulgaria's outgoing national currency, will remain valid for cash payments through January, according to the Associated Press. Merchants must return change only in euros.

The milestone deepens Bulgaria's ties to the European Union nearly two decades after the country of nearly 6.7 million people joined the bloc in 2007 as one of its poorest members. EU membership requires an eventual commitment to the euro, though the process can take years — Croatia was the most recent prior country to make the switch, completing its adoption in 2023.

## Political turmoil shadows the transition

The currency changeover arrives against a backdrop of domestic instability. Bulgaria's conservative-led government resigned in December following nationwide anti-corruption protests, leaving the country without an approved budget for 2026, the Associated Press reported. The budget gap hampers pending reforms and Bulgaria's ability to draw on financial support funds from the 27-member EU bloc.

To qualify for eurozone membership, Bulgarian authorities were required to bring the country's inflation rate to 2.7%, which the government achieved to secure approval from EU leaders, according to the Associated Press. The subsequent resignation undercut the government's capacity to manage the transition period's fiscal demands.

## Skepticism stoked by nationalist and pro-Russian forces

Ordinary Bulgarians have expressed apprehension about the currency switch, with fears centered on potential price increases, the Associated Press reported. Nationalist and pro-Russian political groups have amplified those anxieties, arguing the adoption of the euro will deepen poverty and erode national identity.

Bulgaria's 1989 transition from a Soviet-style economy to democracy and free markets makes the country's integration into the European single-currency system a significant marker of post-communist realignment, the Associated Press reported.
