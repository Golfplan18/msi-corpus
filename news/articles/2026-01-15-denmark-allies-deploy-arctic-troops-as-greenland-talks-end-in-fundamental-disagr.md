Denmark's foreign minister and European allies announced small troop deployments to Greenland on Wednesday as U.S., Danish, and Greenlandic officials met in Washington and acknowledged a "fundamental disagreement" over President Donald Trump's ambition to take control of the Arctic territory. Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen said the two sides agreed to form a high-level working group to explore common ground, even as he conceded the talks had not moved the American position.

"We didn't manage to change the American position," Rasmussen said after meeting in Washington with his Greenlandic counterpart, Vice President JD Vance, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio. He said he had not expected to do so.

The parallel deployment of troop contingents from France, Germany, Norway, and Sweden — timed to coincide with the Washington meeting — signals that European allies intend to establish a visible security presence in Greenland even as Trump argues U.S. control of the island is a national security necessity. Whether the newly formed working group can bridge that divide remains uncertain.

## Working group formed amid low expectations

Denmark, Greenland, and the U.S. agreed to form a high-level working group "to explore if we can find a common way forward," Rasmussen said, with a first meeting expected "within a matter of weeks." He said the group should address U.S. security concerns while respecting Denmark's "red lines," without specifying what a compromise might look like.

"Whether that is doable, I don't know," Rasmussen said.

Danish Defense Minister Troels Lund Poulsen described the group's formation as "a step in the right direction," adding that it would at least allow the two sides to talk with each other rather than about each other.

Trump has argued repeatedly that the U.S. needs control of Greenland for national security, citing what he describes as Chinese and Russian designs on the island. Greenland holds vast untapped reserves of critical minerals.

## Troops deployed as a diplomatic signal

Just as the Washington talks were underway, the Danish Defense Ministry announced it was increasing its military presence in Greenland alongside NATO allies. France, Germany, Norway, and Sweden each sent small troop contingents in a move officials described as symbolic but deliberate.

Germany dispatched 13 troops, with its Defense Ministry saying the aim was to sound out "possibilities to ensure security with a view to Russian and Chinese threats in the Arctic" and describing the deployment as "a strong signal of our unity." The United Kingdom said one British officer was part of a reconnaissance group for an Arctic endurance exercise.

"The deployment serves both to send a political signal and military signal to America, but also indeed to recognize that Arctic security should be reinforced more," said Maria Martisiute, an analyst at the European Policy Center in Brussels. "And first and foremost, this should be done through allied effort, not by the U.S. coming and wanting to take it over. So it complicates the situation for the U.S."

Poulsen said Denmark intends to establish "a more permanent military presence with a larger Danish contribution" in the Arctic and invite allies to participate in exercises and training on a rotating basis.

## NATO's role in question

The European deployments were Danish-led and not coordinated through NATO, which the United States dominates. European allies said they are nonetheless seeking to keep NATO engaged. Germany stated the aim was to develop "a well-founded picture on the ground for further talks and planning within NATO."

Poulsen and Greenland's foreign minister plan to meet NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte in Brussels on Monday to discuss security in the Arctic region.

Martisiute said she was "really looking forward for an announcement of some kind of military activity or deployment under NATO's framework," warning that without one "there is indeed a risk that NATO is paralyzed — and that would not be good."
