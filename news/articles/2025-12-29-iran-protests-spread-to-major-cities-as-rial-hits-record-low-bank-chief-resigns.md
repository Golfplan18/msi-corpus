Iranian merchants shuttered shops and protesters filled streets in Tehran and at least three other cities Monday after the country's currency fell to a record low against the dollar, with state television confirming the resignation of Central Bank chief Mohammad Reza Farzin and police deploying tear gas against demonstrators in parts of the capital.

The demonstrations — the largest in Iran since 2022 — reflect mounting public anger over a currency that has lost more than two-thirds of its value since Farzin took office three years ago, an official inflation rate of 42.2 percent, and food prices that have risen 72 percent in a year.

Iranian merchants shuttered shops and protesters gathered in Tehran and at least three other cities Monday after the country's currency fell to a record low against the dollar, with state television confirming the resignation of Central Bank chief Mohammad Reza Farzin and police deploying tear gas against demonstrators in parts of the capital.

As MSI previously reported, the rial crashed to 1.42 million per dollar on Sunday and protests began at two large markets in central Tehran — the start of what became Monday's broader unrest. [That coverage tracked the first wave of demonstrations and Farzin's anticipated exit](/articles/2025-12-29-iran-rial-crash-triggers-mass-protests-central-bank-chief-resigns). By Monday the currency had recovered slightly to 1.38 million per dollar, according to the Associated Press, but the partial rebound did little to quiet protests that spread to Isfahan in central Iran, Shiraz in the south, and Mashhad in the northeast, witnesses told the AP.

The demonstrations were the largest in Iran since 2022, when the death of Mahsa Jina Amini, 22, under morality police custody ignited nationwide protests. Amini had been detained for allegedly wearing her hijab improperly.

Witnesses told the AP that merchants on Saadi Street in central Tehran and in the Shush neighborhood near the Grand Bazaar closed their stalls Monday and urged neighboring businesses to follow. The semi-official ILNA news agency reported that many businesses stopped selling, though some remained open. Vendors at the Grand Bazaar played a central role in Iran's 1979 Islamic Revolution.

The official IRNA news agency confirmed the protests.

## Economic toll

Iran's state statistics center reported that the country's official inflation rate reached 42.2 percent in December compared to the same month the prior year, up 1.8 percentage points from November. Food prices rose 72 percent year-over-year and health and medical items increased 50 percent over the same period, the center said. Critics have described the figures as signs of imminent hyperinflation.

The currency's decline has been severe. When Farzin took office in 2022, the rial traded at approximately 430,000 per dollar. At the time of the 2015 nuclear agreement that suspended international sanctions in exchange for limits on Iran's nuclear program, the currency stood at 32,000 per dollar.

That deal collapsed after President Donald Trump withdrew the United States from it unilaterally in 2018. State media reports that the government plans to raise taxes in the new Iranian year, which begins March 21, have added to public anxiety, the AP reported. A recent change in gasoline prices has further strained household budgets.

## Geopolitical backdrop

International pressure has compounded Iran's economic strains. The United Nations reimposed nuclear-related sanctions in September 2025, freezing Iranian assets abroad, halting arms transactions with Tehran, and imposing restrictions tied to Iran's ballistic missile program.

Many Iranians also remain anxious following a 12-day military conflict with Israel in June 2025, according to the AP, with concerns that a broader confrontation involving the United States could further destabilize markets.
