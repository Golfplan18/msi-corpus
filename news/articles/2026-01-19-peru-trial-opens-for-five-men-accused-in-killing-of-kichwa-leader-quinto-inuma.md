A Peruvian court opened a trial Tuesday for five men charged with the contract killing of Quinto Inuma Alvarado, a Kichwa tribal leader who was shot in an ambush on Nov. 29, 2023, after repeatedly opposing illegal logging and drug trafficking within his community's territory in Peru's northern Amazon region of San Martín. Prosecutors are seeking life sentences under contract-killing charges — the first time such charges have been brought in Peru for the murder of an Indigenous environmental defender.

The case is being closely watched by Indigenous groups, human rights advocates and international observers as a test of whether Peru can hold perpetrators accountable for violence against environmental defenders in the Amazon, where community leaders who defend forests and land rights often face threats with little protection and few cases ever reach court.

At least 35 Indigenous defenders have been killed in Peru over the past decade, according to Indigenous organizations and human rights groups including Global Witness, yet advocates say the vast majority of such cases never reach trial. A life-sentence conviction would be an unprecedented legal outcome in the country, and observers say it could signal across Latin America that killings of environmental defenders will no longer go unpunished.

## Ambush on the river

Inuma, 50, was from Santa Rosillo de Yanayacu in San Martín. He was traveling by boat along a river route used to reach his community when he was shot during an ambush and fell into the river, said Cristina Gavancho, a lawyer with the Lima-based Instituto de Defensa Legal, which has accompanied Indigenous organizations and victims' families since the killing. Another community member was wounded in the attack and survived.

Prosecutors allege that the perpetrators, believed to have been illegal loggers, targeted Inuma because of his role defending Indigenous land and reporting illegal activities to authorities. Prosecutors say they have built a case that includes forensic gunshot-residue tests and witness testimony placing the accused at the scene around the time of the killing. Investigators also link the suspects to individuals Inuma had repeatedly reported to authorities for illegal logging and drug trafficking.

Five of the six suspects originally charged will face trial. A sixth suspect was killed during an attempted arrest after he attacked police officers with a machete, Gavancho said.

Inuma's son, Kevin Arnol Inuma, 30, said his father had known the risks he was taking.

"He used to tell us that one day they might kill him and that we should be prepared," Kevin Inuma told the Associated Press. "My father was deeply committed to his territory and his community. Being a real environmental defender requires a lot of sacrifice — walking through the forest, in sun and rain, and exposing yourself to danger."

## Protection that was never implemented

The killing brought attention to the failure of Peru's system for protecting environmental and Indigenous defenders. Inuma had been granted a security detail under a state protection mechanism created in 2021, but those measures were never implemented.

"Quinto Inuma had all the paperwork that was supposed to protect his life, and he was still killed," said Matías Pérez Ojea del Arco, advocacy coordinator for Peru at Forest Peoples Programme. "These protection mechanisms stay on paper. Paper does not stop bullets."

Gavancho said the state has acknowledged those failures before the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights and United Nations rapporteurs, and that the protection "was never carried out due to lack of budget." The Inter-American Commission granted precautionary measures to Inuma's community in early 2024.

Kevin Inuma said his father's death forced his family to leave their community and adapt to life in the city, where they lost their home, crops and way of life. "Living in the city feels like being imprisoned," he said. "It is not our territory, and it has been very hard for our family."

## A rare test of accountability

Gavancho said convictions in this case could have implications beyond Peru, showing that thorough investigations are possible and that lack of resources should no longer be used to justify impunity in killings linked to illegal logging, drug trafficking and mining across the Amazon.

"This case is significant because it is the opportunity that the Peruvian state has to establish an exemplary sanction," she said.

Mary Lawlor, the United Nations Special Rapporteur on the situation of human rights defenders, told the AP that the investigation itself already represents a rare step forward.

"So often where people are killed for defending human rights the crime is never investigated, and the perpetrators are never prosecuted," Lawlor said. "The fact that the state's investigation has identified both the alleged perpetrators and alleged intellectual authors behind Quinto's killing is, sadly, ground breaking."

Lawlor said she would monitor the trial and expressed hope that it would mark a new chapter in the prosecution of attacks and threats against human rights defenders in Peru.

While high-profile killings of environmental defenders in countries including Brazil, Honduras and the Philippines have led to arrests or prosecutions, advocates say they have seldom resulted in outcomes seen as setting lasting precedents.

Kevin Inuma said the trial cannot undo what his family has lost.

"Even if there is justice, it won't bring my father back," he said. "We will never see him again."
