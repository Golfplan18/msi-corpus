U.S. President Donald Trump's push to bring Greenland under American control centers on the world's largest island's unmatched position above the Arctic Circle — a location that has shaped North Atlantic defense strategy since World War II and now sits at the center of intensifying competition among the United States, Russia and China.

Greenland, a self-governing territory of Denmark, is home to about 56,000 mostly Inuit people and guards shipping lanes and military corridors that are becoming more accessible as climate change thins the polar ice. Both Denmark and Greenland's own government have rejected Trump's overtures, saying the island's people will decide their own future.

The dispute unfolds as Russia has rebuilt military infrastructure across the Arctic since 2014 and China has declared itself a "near-Arctic state," raising the strategic stakes of a region long treated as a zone of international cooperation. Defense analysts say the United States already enjoys nearly all the military access it seeks through existing alliance arrangements — a point that has not tempered Trump's stated ambitions for the island.

## Strategic location

Greenland sits off the northeastern coast of Canada, with more than two-thirds of its territory within the Arctic Circle. That position made it crucial to North American defense during World War II, when the United States occupied the island to prevent it from falling to Nazi Germany and to protect critical North Atlantic shipping lanes.

Today, Greenland guards part of what defense planners call the GIUK Gap — the stretch of ocean between Greenland, Iceland and the United Kingdom — where NATO monitors Russian naval movements in the North Atlantic.

The U.S. Department of Defense operates Pituffik Space Base in northwestern Greenland, built after the United States and Denmark signed the Defense of Greenland Treaty in 1951. The base supports missile warning, missile defense and space surveillance operations for the United States and NATO.

## Expert assessment

Thomas Crosbie, an associate professor of military operations at the Royal Danish Defense College, said a U.S. takeover would not improve upon what Washington already has through its alliance relationship.

"The United States will gain no advantage if its flag is flying in Nuuk (Greenland's capital) versus the Greenlandic flag," Crosbie told the Associated Press. "There's no benefits to them because they already enjoy all of the advantages they want. If there's any specific security access that they want to improve American security, they'll be given it as a matter of course, as a trusted ally. So this has nothing to do with improving national security for the United States."

## Russia and China's Arctic ambitions

Russia has opened military bases in the Arctic and worked to reconstruct airfields since 2014, rebuilding Soviet-era infrastructure across the polar region. Russian President Vladimir Putin said Moscow was concerned about NATO's activities in the Arctic and would respond by strengthening its armed forces there, while also saying Moscow was holding the door open to broader international cooperation in the region.

China declared itself a "near-Arctic state" in 2018 in an effort to gain more influence in the region and announced plans to build a "Polar Silk Road" as part of its global Belt and Road Initiative. Then-Secretary of State Mike Pompeo rejected China's claim, asking: "Do we want the Arctic Ocean to transform into a new South China Sea, fraught with militarization and competing territorial claims?"

Climate change is accelerating the competition. Thinning Arctic ice is opening the prospect of a northwest passage for international trade and making the region's mineral resources more accessible.

## Denmark's military response

Denmark has moved to strengthen its own Arctic presence. The Danish government announced a roughly 14.6 billion-kroner ($2.3 billion) agreement with the governments of Greenland and the Faroe Islands to improve surveillance capabilities and maintain sovereignty in the region. The plan includes three new Arctic naval vessels, two additional long-range surveillance drones and satellite capacity.

Denmark's parliament approved a bill last June to allow U.S. military bases on Danish soil, widening a 2023 agreement that had granted U.S. troops access to Danish air bases. Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen wrote that Denmark would be able to terminate the agreement if the United States tries to annex Greenland.

Denmark's Joint Arctic Command, headquartered in Nuuk, is tasked with surveillance, sovereignty assertion and military defense of Greenland and the Faroe Islands. The Sirius Dog Sled Patrol, an elite Danish naval unit, conducts long-range reconnaissance and enforces Danish sovereignty across Greenland's Arctic wilderness.

## Mineral wealth

Greenland's appeal extends beyond its geography. The island holds rare earth minerals that are key components of cellphones, computers, batteries and other high-technology goods — resources that have drawn U.S. and other Western interest as they seek to reduce China's dominance of the critical-minerals market. Development of Greenland's mineral resources has been hampered by the island's harsh climate and strict environmental controls.
