Portugal’s presidential election results released with nearly all votes counted Sunday put far-right populist leader André Ventura in second place. Ventura, head of the Chega (Basta) party, won about 24% of the vote, trailing António José Seguro, a center-left Socialist Party candidate who led with about 31%, according to results with almost 98% counted.

The top two finishers will meet again in a runoff on Feb. 8. The result will decide who replaces the current president, Marcelo Rebelo de Sousa, after he completes two five-year terms.

Ventura’s performance marked another step for parties on the far right in Europe, the AP reported. Chega, founded by Ventura less than seven years ago, became Portugal’s second-largest party in parliament last year, shortly after the party’s rise.

The election featured a record field of candidates, with 10 people running for president. None won the more than 50% share required to take the presidency outright in the first round, AP said.

Chega’s push for broader attention has been driven in part by Ventura’s immigration-focused campaign. The AP reported that Ventura targeted what he called “excessive immigration,” portraying foreign workers as increasingly visible in Portugal.

During the campaign, Ventura used billboards with messages including “Portugal es nuestro” and slogans such as “esto no es Bangladesh” and “no se debería permitir a los inmigrantes vivir de la asistencia social,” according to the report. The AP said that sentiments like those on the posters were previously “unthinkable” in Portugal.

The AP also said the emergence of Chega has pulled support from Portugal’s two biggest long-running parties that have alternated in power for the past half-century: the center-right Social Democratic Party and the center-left Socialist Party.

Looking ahead, the AP described the presidency as largely ceremonial, with little executive power. The president’s role is mainly to stay above the political fight, mediate disputes, and reduce tensions, while still holding tools such as the ability to veto legislation passed by parliament, which the report said can be overturned.

The AP said the president also has a “atomic bomb” power in Portuguese political jargon: the authority to dissolve parliament and call early elections. It added that events in Portugal generally have limited effect on the broader direction of the European Union, noting the country’s smaller economy and modest armed forces.

The next president will serve in Lisbon, at the president’s seat described as the “Palacio Rosa” on the river, for a five-year term. The runoff on Feb. 8 is set to determine who takes the post.
