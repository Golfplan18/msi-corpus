A federal vaccine advisory committee voted Friday to eliminate the longstanding recommendation that all newborns receive a hepatitis B vaccine within 24 hours of birth, instead making the decision optional for most infants. Under the new guidance from the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, only babies whose mothers test positive for hepatitis B or have unknown infection status would receive the vaccine at birth; for other babies, parents and doctors would decide whether vaccination begins at 2 months. Medical organizations and public health experts immediately condemned the decision, saying it will leave children vulnerable to a serious liver disease that can cause chronic infection, liver cancer and death.

The shift reflects a broader effort by the Trump administration's appointed health officials to reduce federal vaccine recommendations, despite warnings from pediatricians and public health organizations that more children will contract preventable diseases as a result.

Hepatitis B is a serious liver infection that for most people lasts less than six months. But for some—especially infants and children—it can become a long-lasting problem that leads to liver failure, liver cancer and scarring called cirrhosis.

The virus can be transmitted from an infected mother to a baby during birth. Infants can also contract it from relatively casual contact with someone who has chronic disease, such as touching a towel or toothbrush, since the virus can survive on surfaces for more than seven days at room temperature.

The risk is particularly acute for infants. Up to 90 percent of infants who contract hepatitis B develop chronic infections, meaning their immune systems do not fully clear the virus. Around 2.4 million people in the United States are estimated to have hepatitis B, and as many as half are unaware they are infected, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

## A Vaccination Program With a Proven Record

For decades, federal health guidance has recommended newborn hepatitis B vaccination. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, a panel of government-appointed experts, recommended an initial dose at birth beginning in 1991. The guidance was modified over the years and currently suggests a dose within 24 hours of birth for all medically stable infants weighing at least 4.4 pounds, followed by shots at about one month and six months.

Why a dose right at birth? Health officials relied on screening expectant mothers to identify babies that might have been exposed to the virus. But many cases were missed, partly because some women weren't tested or test results were incorrect. The newborn vaccination became standard practice to protect infants before exposure could occur.

Over approximately 30 years, the strategy proved effective. Hepatitis B cases among children fell from about 18,000 per year to about 2,200.

A collaboration of public health researchers, the Vaccine Integrity Project, released its analysis of more than 400 studies and reports spanning 40 years this week. The group concluded the newborn dose is safe and is an important reason U.S. pediatric hepatitis B infections have fallen.

## A Committee Transformed

The committee that oversees vaccination guidance has changed dramatically. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., a leading vaccine skeptic, fired all 17 members of the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices earlier this year and replaced them with a group that includes several anti-vaccine voices.

The new panel raised concerns about giving a vaccine to a baby so early in life. Committee members also worried that in many cases, doctors and nurses don't have full conversations with parents about the pros and cons of giving the vaccine to newborns.

## Doctors and Medical Groups Respond

The vote to change the recommendation drew sharp criticism from the medical establishment.

"It's an illegitimate process," said Dr. Paul Offit, a vaccine researcher at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and former government adviser. "The people that are in a position to make decisions about public health don't have the expertise to make those decisions. I think what you have is an ACIP that is shaped in Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s image, which is to say anti-science, anti-vaccine."

Dr. Offit added that the committee "has just condemned hundreds of children to a shorter life."

Dr. James Campbell, vice chair of the American Academy of Pediatrics' infectious diseases committee, called this week's meetings a "brazen attempt to sow fear and distrust in vaccinations that have saved countless lives."

He recalled treating a 15-year-old in Baltimore in 1999 who was not vaccinated as a newborn and not offered vaccination later because she wasn't considered at high risk. "She died from hepatitis B after suffering liver failure and undergoing two failed liver transplants," Campbell said, describing the experience as "heart-wrenching."

"Because of the birth dose and because of the current recommendations that we have since then," he said, "I have never had to go through that heart-wrenching experience again."

Forty-five medical and public health organizations, including the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, the American Medical Association and the American Public Health Association, issued a joint statement saying they are "deeply alarmed" by the vaccine committee's actions.

"ACIP's actions will harm children, their families and the medical professionals who care for them," the organizations said. "That is why we are joining together to speak up."

A report released this week estimated that delaying the hepatitis B vaccine to 2 months could result in at least 1,400 hepatitis B infections in children and 480 deaths. The report has not yet been peer-reviewed or published in a medical journal.

## States and Institutions Respond

Several state and local officials said they will not adopt the committee's new guidance. The governor of Massachusetts and health departments in Maryland, New York City and Santa Clara County, California, all announced they plan to continue recommending hepatitis B vaccination for all newborns within 24 hours of birth. Milwaukee's health department said parents should continue consulting with their doctors and "follow trusted sources like the American Academy of Pediatrics."

The American Academy of Pediatrics said it will continue recommending routine hepatitis B vaccination for all newborns, with a first dose within 24 hours of birth, a second dose at one to two months and a third dose at six months.
