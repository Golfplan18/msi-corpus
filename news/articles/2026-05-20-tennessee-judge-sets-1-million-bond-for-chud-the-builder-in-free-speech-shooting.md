CLARKSVILLE, Tenn. — A Montgomery County judge on Thursday set a $1 million bond for Dalton Eatherly, the 28-year-old livestreamer known as “Chud the Builder,” who faces an attempted murder charge for shooting Joshua Fox, a Black man, in the parking lot of the county courthouse on May 13. The shooting and Eatherly’s online persona have drawn national scrutiny, as his videos — which often feature racially charged taunts and confrontations — are marketed as “First Amendment audits,” a genre in which content creators test the boundaries of free speech by filming public officials and citizens.

At the hearing, Judge H. Reid Poland III forbade anyone in the courtroom from using electronic devices and ordered several spectators removed, including conservative activist Jake Lang, who was taken out in handcuffs. The judge’s strict control of the room signaled the intensity surrounding a case that has become a flashpoint in the ongoing argument over how far speech protections extend when online provocation spills into real-world violence.

Eatherly’s attorney, Jacob Fendley, refused to discuss the charges when reached by the Associated Press but pleaded for an end to the harassment of his office. “Even though I’m defending him, I find his online content objectionable,” Fendley said. His statement underscores the tension at the heart of the case: a defendant whose free speech claims sit uncomfortably alongside a record of hate-filled productions.

Civil rights advocates contend that Eatherly’s self-portrayal as a First Amendment auditor is a cover for race-baiting — videotaping and taunting Black people under the guise of recording public employees. Critics say the profit-driven harassment model normalized by some online creators invites the very escalation that the Clarksville shooting represents. The debate echoes earlier cases in which courts have grappled with the line between protected expression and targeted intimidation.

Eatherly remains held pending trial on the attempted murder charge. The $1 million bond reflects the serious nature of the allegation, while the courtroom’s removal of a conservative activist hints at the broader political currents the case has pulled into its wake.
