## Analysts say closure would remove only US post near Strait of Malacca

The Trump administration's decision to close the U.S. Consulate in Medan, Indonesia, may save relatively little money but would weaken American influence in one of the Indo-Pacific's most strategically important regions, according to former U.S. diplomats and Asia analysts quoted in a UPI commentary. The move comes at a time when Washington says competition with China is its foremost foreign policy priority, while shrinking the American diplomatic footprint outside Indonesia's capital.

The Medan consulate is small compared with the U.S. Embassy in Jakarta, but it serves roughly 60 million Indonesians across 10 provinces on the island of Sumatra. Its location gives Washington a permanent presence near the Strait of Malacca, one of the world's busiest maritime corridors.

Patrick Cronin, chair for Asia-Pacific Security at the Hudson Institute, told UPI the administration should reconsider closing a strategically located consulate near the Strait of Malacca. "Recent tensions around the Strait of Hormuz remind us how indispensable the Strait of Malacca remains to global commerce," Cronin said.

The analysis appeared in a UPI commentary by James Borton, a non-resident senior fellow at Johns Hopkins SAIS Foreign Policy Institute, who wrote that the proposed closure comes as the United States seeks to deepen its Comprehensive Strategic Partnership with Indonesia — Southeast Asia's largest economy, the world's largest Muslim-majority democracy and an increasingly influential G20 member. Borton wrote that those ambitions sit uneasily alongside a shrinking American diplomatic footprint in one of Indonesia's most important regions.

Sumatra is one of Indonesia's economic engines, producing energy, palm oil, coffee, rubber and other commodities while anchoring shipping and trade routes across the Malacca Strait and the Indian Ocean. North Sumatra recorded more than $3.5 billion in exports in 2025, and South Sumatra recorded $6.3 billion. American companies in energy, agribusiness, manufacturing and logistics have commercial interests across the island, including relationships with provincial governments, suppliers, ports, industrial estates and local business communities.

Murray Hiebert, head of research at BowerGroupAsia, said closing Medan would reinforce perceptions that the United States is reducing its presence in Southeast Asia. "It will reinforce the perception that the United States is shrinking its footprint in Southeast Asia," Hiebert told UPI. "Closing Medan also removes the only permanent U.S. diplomatic presence near the Strait of Malacca."

Unlike countries where political authority is concentrated in one capital, Indonesia's decentralized system gives provincial governments considerable influence over investment, environmental management and economic development. Borton wrote that relationships built through a regional consulate cannot easily be replicated through periodic visits from embassy officials based in Jakarta.

Ted Osius, former U.S. Ambassador to Vietnam and former president and CEO of the U.S.-ASEAN Business Council, said the cost of operating the Medan post is minimal in strategic terms. "The annual cost of operating the U.S. Consulate in Medan, staffed by just two American diplomats serving an island of more than 60 million people, is roughly equivalent to one minute of Pentagon spending during the conflict with Iran," Osius told UPI. "The modest savings from closing the post would come at a disproportionate strategic cost."

Indonesia occupies a distinctive place in Washington's Indo-Pacific strategy — neither a treaty ally nor a strategic adversary, but an independent regional power that has pursued its long-standing "free and active" foreign policy. Jakarta has resisted choosing sides between Washington and Beijing, preferring to cultivate strong ties with both.

China has expanded its presence across Indonesia over the past decade through infrastructure projects, mining investments, industrial cooperation and Belt and Road financing. Chinese companies dominate Indonesia's rapidly growing nickel-processing industry, and the Jakarta-Bandung high-speed railway has become Beijing's flagship infrastructure project in Southeast Asia. Borton wrote that Chinese engagement extends well beyond Jakarta, with provincial governments working directly with Chinese companies, universities and local officials.

Robert Blake, a former U.S. ambassador and senior managing director at McLarty Associates, said the Medan post's value exceeds its size. "We should not be closing the U.S. Consulate in Medan," Blake told UPI. "Despite its relatively small operating cost, the consulate punches well above its weight. It enables U.S. diplomats to engage with Sumatra's 60 million people, promotes American business in a region rich in energy, palm oil and coffee, and supports Indonesia's efforts to protect Sumatra's unique biodiversity — a globally significant natural heritage and one of the world's most important carbon sinks."

The consulate's work has included support for conservation partnerships, scientific cooperation, educational exchanges and civil society initiatives. Sumatra contains some of the world's richest tropical forests, including the Leuser Ecosystem, one of the few places where orangutans, elephants, rhinos and tigers still co-exist in the wild. As climate change intersects with national security and economic policy, Borton wrote, environmental diplomacy has become another arena in which countries build long-term influence.

Supporters of consolidation argue that modern communications and periodic travel can substitute for permanent regional offices during a period of fiscal restraint. The analysts quoted in the commentary said diplomacy remains fundamentally relational, with trust developing through sustained interaction and familiarity with local institutions and networks.

Borton wrote that whether the closure ultimately proceeds is less important than what it signals. Throughout the Indo-Pacific, Washington contends that strategic competition with China will be won not only through military strength but also through sustained economic, diplomatic and societal engagement — a strategy that depends on people, institutions and local relationships built over decades, not only on high-level summits or defense agreements.
