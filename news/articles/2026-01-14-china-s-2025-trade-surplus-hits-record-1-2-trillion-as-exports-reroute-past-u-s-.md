HONG KONG — China's trade surplus surged 20 percent to a record of nearly $1.2 trillion in 2025, Chinese customs data showed Wednesday, as manufacturers redirected shipments to Africa, Southeast Asia, Europe and Latin America to offset a sharp fall in exports to the United States under higher tariffs imposed by President Donald Trump.

The surplus — the first to exceed $1 trillion for a full calendar year, up from $992 billion in 2024 — came despite a 20 percent decline in exports to the United States. Total exports climbed 5.5 percent to $3.77 trillion while imports flatlined at $2.58 trillion.

"We continue to expect exports to act as a big growth driver in 2026," said Jacqueline Rong, chief China economist at BNP Paribas.

## Geographic Diversification

While shipments to the United States fell sharply after Trump returned to office and escalated his trade war with the world's second-largest economy, Chinese exporters found expanding markets elsewhere. Exports to Africa surged 26 percent in 2025. Those to Southeast Asian countries jumped 13 percent; to the European Union, 8 percent; and to Latin America, 7 percent.

Mechanical and electrical items — China's largest export category — rose 8.4 percent from a year earlier. Auto exports surged 21 percent to more than 7 million units, driven by electric vehicles and plug-in hybrids, according to the China Association of Automobile Manufacturers. Exports of furniture, clothing, shoes and other labor-intensive products fell over the same period.

Strong global demand for computer chips and other devices supported that growth, analysts said.

## December Data

China's export momentum strengthened at year-end. In December, exports climbed 6.6 percent from a year earlier in dollar terms, better than economists' estimates and accelerating from November's 5.9 percent year-on-year rise. Imports in December were up 5.7 percent year-on-year, compared with 1.9 percent in November.

## Domestic Demand Gap

Strong exports have helped keep China's economy growing at an annual rate close to its official target of about 5 percent, but analysts said that reliance creates risks as the global trade environment stiffens.

Wang Jun, vice minister of China's customs administration, told reporters in Beijing that the country faces a "severe and complex" external trade environment in 2026, while adding that China's "foreign trade fundamentals remain solid."

HSBC economists Erin Xin and Taylor Wang wrote Wednesday that "even if exports sustain neutral or positive growth this year, more of China's growth drivers will need to come from domestic demand."

A prolonged property downturn — triggered when Chinese authorities cracked down on excessive borrowing, producing defaults by many developers — has weighed on consumer confidence and household spending. Government trade-in subsidies for home appliances and vehicles offered over the past year helped lift domestic passenger car sales 6 percent in 2025, but sales softened toward year-end as subsidies were scaled back or phased out in some areas.

Rong of BNP Paribas said she expects domestic demand growth to "stay tepid," adding that "the policy boost to domestic demand looks weaker than last year — in particular the fiscal subsidy program for consumer goods."

The head of the International Monetary Fund called in December for China to fix its economic imbalances and accelerate a shift away from export reliance by boosting domestic consumption and investment.

## Outlook

Gary Ng, a senior economist at French investment bank Natixis, forecast that China's exports will grow about 3 percent in 2026, less than the 5.5 percent pace in 2025. With slow import growth, he expects the trade surplus to remain above $1 trillion.

Wang Jun of customs said the government was confident the country's trade foundations could withstand the pressure.
