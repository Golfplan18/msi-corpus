The U.S. Supreme Court ruled 6-3 on June 25 that the Trump administration may end Temporary Protected Status for immigrants from Haiti and Syria, overturning lower court injunctions that had blocked the terminations.

The decision carries implications beyond those two countries. As of March 2025, approximately 1.3 million people from 17 countries held TPS, according to the National Immigration Forum, an immigrant advocacy group. The ruling establishes that executive decisions to terminate the program are largely beyond judicial review, potentially affecting holders from all designated countries.

Congress created TPS in 1990 under President George H.W. Bush to protect people who cannot safely return to countries experiencing armed conflict, environmental disaster, or other dangers. The program, which was designed to fill a gap in asylum law that covers only people with a well-founded fear of persecution on specific grounds, offers renewable deportation protection and work authorization but no pathway to permanent residency.

Justice Samuel Alito wrote the majority opinion, holding that TPS renewal and termination decisions fall within executive discretion. The ruling directly affects approximately 350,000 Haitian TPS holders and roughly 6,000 Syrian TPS holders.

The Trump administration moved to terminate TPS designations for 13 countries, including Afghanistan and Cameroon, according to NBC News. Lower courts had blocked those efforts. In February, Federal Judge Ana Reyes issued an 85-page decision finding that racial animus had motivated the administration's TPS terminations, according to Heba Gowayed, an associate professor of sociology at CUNY who wrote about the ruling in The Guardian. Reyes cited a social media post by Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem in which Noem recommended a travel ban "on every damn country that's been flooding our nation with killers, leeches, and entitlement junkies."

Even as the administration argued it was safe for Haitians to return, the State Department maintained a "Level 4: Do Not Travel" advisory for Haiti due to "crime, terrorism, kidnapping, unrest and limited healthcare," Gowayed reported, citing the lower court record.

In the majority opinion, Alito wrote that President Donald Trump's past statements about Haitian immigrants — including claims about immigrants eating dogs and remarks that immigrants were "poisoning the blood" of the nation — were not "overtly racial" but rather "expressed policy views that could rest on race-neutral justifications."

Haiti's TPS designation has been in place since the 2010 earthquake. El Salvador's has lasted 25 years and Somalia's more than 35 years. Gowayed wrote that TPS holders contribute more than $29 billion to the U.S. economy and nearly $8 billion in taxes annually while remaining ineligible for most public assistance programs.

The Supreme Court issued a second 6-3 decision the same day allowing the administration to block asylum seekers at the U.S.-Mexico border, according to Gowayed's account.

"Today, Trump's loyalists in the supreme court have joined forces with him to deny immigrants' internationally recognized human rights and advance an authoritarian, white-supremacist agenda at home," said Rep. Delia Ramirez, an Illinois Democrat, according to The Guardian.

The House has passed a bipartisan bill to extend protections for Haitian TPS holders. The Vera Institute of Justice called on the Senate to pass the legislation and urged Congress to create a pathway to permanent residency for all TPS recipients.

"People's lives should never be left up to political whim," Gowayed wrote.
