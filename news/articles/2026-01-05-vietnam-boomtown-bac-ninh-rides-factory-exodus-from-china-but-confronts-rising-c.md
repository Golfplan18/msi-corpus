BAC NINH, Vietnam — A city once celebrated for rice paddies and the love duets of its centuries-old Quan Ho folk songs has become one of Vietnam's busiest factory zones, remade by successive waves of foreign investment that U.S. tariff pressure on China has accelerated. Rising labor costs, worker shortages, and infrastructure gaps are now testing the limits of that rapid transformation.

Vietnam recorded a $123.5 billion goods surplus with the United States in 2024 — third only to China and Mexico — cementing its standing as one of the clearest regional beneficiaries of Washington's friction with Beijing. But the country's economy is roughly 40 times smaller than China's, and its leaders are pushing to climb into higher-value manufacturing before rivals like Indonesia and the Philippines close the gap.

## Factories follow tariffs

Bac Ninh's transformation has unfolded in stages. Samsung built its first phone factory there around 2008, turning the province into its largest offshore manufacturing base and establishing Vietnam's early credibility as an electronics hub.

Chinese companies have since poured in, drawn by Vietnam's electronics supply chain, its labor force, and supportive local governments, according to the Associated Press. Chinese-speaking intermediaries handle paperwork and logistics. Tmall-branded storefronts and Chinese-language schools have appeared in Bac Ninh's downtown, and signs in Chinese advertise services for investors.

But the economics of the strategy are getting harder. A worker at a telecoms equipment company that relocated from Shenzhen — identified only by his surname, Peng, because he was not authorized to speak to the media — said finding workers has grown difficult and expensive.

"It is becoming difficult to recruit workers," Peng said. He said labor costs had jumped 10%–15% since 2024. "And we expect them to keep rising."

Jacob Rothman, co-founder and chief executive of China-based Velong Enterprises, which makes grill tools and kitchen gadgets and has shifted some production to Southeast Asian countries including Vietnam, said supply chains in China reflect decades of government support, large-scale investment, and a population that smaller neighbors cannot match.

"You can't recreate that overnight," Rothman said.

Brian Bourke, global chief commercial officer at U.S.-based SEKO Logistics, said factories making footwear, furniture, and technology are still relocating to Vietnam, but the country lags China in infrastructure and logistics capabilities.

## A $123 billion surplus — and scrutiny to match

Few countries have benefited more from Trump's trade policies than Vietnam. Its $123.5 billion surplus with the United States in 2024 drew Trump's attention, and he threatened a 46% tariff on Vietnamese goods before settling on a 20% rate.

The two countries have been working toward a deal to keep most tariffs at that level; Vietnam has offered broad preferential access for U.S. products, the White House said in October. The country continued to run a surplus of $121.6 billion in January through November 2025, according to the Associated Press.

Scrutiny of Vietnam's role as a potential hub for tariff-dodging transshipments has prompted some manufacturers to hedge their bets. Bourke said one of SEKO Logistics' customers shifted some furniture production to India to avoid concentrating all operations in Vietnam.

Frederic Neumann, chief Asia economist at HSBC, said the October trade truce between President Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping — which lowered average tariffs on Chinese exports to the United States to about 47% — eased some concerns. But persistent uncertainty means companies are spreading factories across multiple countries rather than concentrating moves in any single destination.

Manufacturing inefficiencies in Southeast Asia add only about 10% in cost compared with China, Neumann said, keeping the calculus in favor of relocating even with lower tariffs on Chinese goods. Large corporations can shift production easily; smaller firms with expensive equipment face a harder calculation.

"(The) race to move outside of China is still happening, and it's accelerating," Rothman said.

## Building capacity amid competition

Cumulative foreign direct investment in Vietnam topped $28.5 billion as of September 2025, up 15% from the prior year, according to the Associated Press. To keep pace with demand, the government has been building: a highway to the Chinese border has cut travel time by more than an hour, and a planned railway will link Hanoi to its largest seaport at Haiphong and to the border town of Lao Cai.

On December 19, Bac Ninh broke ground on an expansion of an industrial zone for high-tech manufacturing, covering electronics, pharmaceuticals, and clean energy. Weeks before Vietnam's National Party Congress, scheduled for January — where leaders will determine the country's political leadership and economic direction — the government launched 234 major projects valued at more than $129 billion.

Countries like Indonesia and the Philippines are promoting themselves as alternative manufacturing bases. A new Philippine law allows foreign investors to lease private land for up to 99 years to attract long-term commercial and industrial investment.

## Climbing the value chain

Vietnam's automation rates remain low: about a third of factories still rely on non-automated equipment and only about 10% use robots on production lines. The government is offering incentives, including tax breaks on imported machinery and discounted rents, to push suppliers toward modernization.

Vietnam has also been seeking to reduce dependence on the U.S. market by expanding exports to the Middle East, Latin America, Africa, and India, directing overseas trade offices to share market intelligence and promote Vietnamese-made products.

The country's declared goal is to achieve high-income status by 2045 — following the export-led development models of South Korea and Taiwan — by moving from low-cost assembly into electronics and clean energy manufacturing.

Prime Minister Pham Minh Chinh, announcing the December project launches, framed the national stakes: Vietnam must "reach far into the ocean, delve deep underground and soar high into space."
