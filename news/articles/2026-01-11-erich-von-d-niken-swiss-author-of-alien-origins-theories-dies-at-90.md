Erich von Däniken, the Swiss author whose 1968 bestseller *Chariots of the Gods* argued that alien astronauts visited ancient civilizations and guided construction of the Egyptian pyramids and other monumental structures, died Saturday in a hospital in central Switzerland. He was 90.

His representatives announced his death on his website Sunday, January 11.

Von Däniken's books sold nearly 70 million copies in more than 30 languages, according to Swiss public broadcaster SRF, making him one of Switzerland's most widely read authors. Scientists and archaeologists consistently rejected his central claims, but his work seeded decades of popular entertainment built around ancient mysteries, from paranormal magazines to prime-time television.

## 'Chariots of the Gods'

Published in 1968, *Chariots of the Gods* contended that the Mayans and ancient Egyptians lacked the sophistication to construct their monumental architecture without outside assistance — specifically, von Däniken claimed, from extraterrestrial visitors who had come to Earth in antiquity. The book appeared at a moment when conventional science was carrying humanity toward its first Moon landing, feeding a public appetite for unexplained phenomena and alternative histories.

More than two dozen additional books followed, according to the Associated Press, creating what it described as "a literary niche in which fact and fantasy were mixed together against all historical and scientific evidence." A second book, *Gods from Outer Space*, was ready for publication by the time von Däniken left prison following his first book's commercial success.

In 1991, von Däniken became the first recipient of the Ig Nobel Prize for literature, an award given, according to the Associated Press, for raising public awareness of science through questionable experiments or claims.

His critics on occasion did more than argue: a British television documentary confronted him with evidence that artifacts he had presented as ancient were nearly new. He acknowledged discrepancies but held to the substance of his theories. "I have admitted (my mistakes), but not one of the foundations of my theories has yet been brought down," he wrote in his 1985 book *Neue Erinnerungen an die Zukunft* (New Memories of the Future).

## A contentious life

Von Däniken was born in 1935 in Schaffhausen, a town in northern Switzerland, the son of a clothing manufacturer. He attended Catholic boarding school and is said to have rebelled against his father's strict Catholicism and his clerical instructors by developing his own alternatives to the biblical account of human origins.

After leaving school in 1954, he worked as a waiter and barkeeper, during which time he faced repeated fraud accusations and served short stints in prison. In 1964, he was appointed manager of a hotel in the resort town of Davos and began writing *Chariots of the Gods*. The book's rapid commercial success was quickly followed by accusations of tax dodging and financial impropriety, and he again spent time behind bars.

By the time he was released, the book was earning him a fortune. Throughout the 1970s, he conducted field trips to Egypt, India, and Latin America — whose ancient cultures held particular fascination for him — lectured widely, and set up societies to promote his theories. He later adapted his work to video and multimedia formats to reach broader audiences.

His popularity waned in English-speaking markets by the 1980s, though the Associated Press reported that his books and films influenced a wave of semi-serious archaeological documentaries and popular television programs, including *The X-Files*.

His last major venture, a theme park called Mystery Park built around his books and located near Interlaken, Switzerland, failed after a few years due to lack of interest. The site remains standing.

## Survivors

Von Däniken is survived by his wife of 65 years, Elisabeth Skaja; Cornelia; and two grandchildren.
