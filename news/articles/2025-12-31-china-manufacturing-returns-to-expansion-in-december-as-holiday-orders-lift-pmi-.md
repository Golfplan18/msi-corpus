The marginal improvement was driven partly by a seasonal rush to complete orders before year-end holidays and partly by an extended truce in U.S.-China trade tensions, but analysts cautioned that the structural drags weighing on the world's second-largest economy — a prolonged property slump and widespread industrial overcapacity — are set to persist through 2026.

Chinese factory activity expanded in December for the first time in eight months, with the official purchasing managers index for manufacturing rising to 50.1, China's National Bureau of Statistics reported Wednesday. A private-sector survey by RatingDog independently confirmed the December reading at 50.1 — just above the threshold that separates expansion from contraction on a scale up to 100.

As MSI [previously reported](/articles/2025-12-31-china-factory-activity-expands-for-first-time-in-eight-months), the December data ends a run of contraction that stretched back to April. Both the official and private surveys exceeded analyst expectations.

## What drove the rebound

Orders picked up ahead of year-end and New Year holidays, the National Bureau of Statistics said. Construction firms also rushed to complete projects before the break. China's Lunar New Year falls in mid-February.

Trade pressures played a role as well. An extended truce in tensions with the United States eased some pressure on manufacturers, according to the Associated Press report.

High-tech manufacturing led sector gains, with its official PMI reaching 52.5 in December — up 2.4 percentage points from November. Equipment manufacturing and the consumer goods industry each posted a reading of 50.4. PMI measures for food, textiles, clothing, and electronics were above 53.

Despite the headline improvement, new export sales fell slightly in December, and hiring weakened, RatingDog reported.

"Overall, the manufacturing sector regained growth at the end of 2025," said Yao Yu, RatingDog's founder. "However, the improvement was marginal, with the impact of promotions and new products appearing impulse-driven and their sustainability requiring observation."

## Uneven across firm sizes

The December expansion was concentrated among large manufacturers. Factory activity for small and mid-sized enterprises — which account for the majority of employment in China — remained in contractionary territory, the National Bureau of Statistics said. Conditions for retailers and restaurants also deteriorated as consumers continued to pull back on spending.

Higher costs for raw materials, especially metals, put pressure on company profit margins. To help offset those costs, exporters raised prices for the first time in three months, RatingDog reported.

## Outlook clouded by structural pressures

In remarks to a New Year's gathering carried Wednesday by state media, President Xi Jinping vowed to promote "high-quality development" and carry out "more positive macroeconomic policies" while ensuring social harmony and stability.

China's economy is forecast to grow at a pace just below the official target of approximately 5% for 2025, supported by strong performance in high-tech industries and exports.

Julian Evans-Pritchard of Capital Economics cautioned that the upturn may prove temporary. "The big picture is that the structural headwinds from the property downturn and industrial overcapacity are set to persist in 2026 and there appears to be limited appetite among policymakers for a big increase in demand-side stimulus," he said.

The broader challenges facing China's economy include a years-long slump in the property sector and excess capacity across industries including automaking, which has fueled damaging price wars.
