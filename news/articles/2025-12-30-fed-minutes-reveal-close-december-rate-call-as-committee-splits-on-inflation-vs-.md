Federal Reserve policymakers voted 9 to 3 to cut their benchmark interest rate by a quarter point at their Dec. 9–10 meeting, but minutes released Tuesday show the decision was far from settled: even some officials who voted for the reduction had considered backing no change at all.

The three-way split in the vote — two dissenting members favored no cut, one sought a larger half-point reduction — was an unusual level of public disagreement for a committee that typically works by consensus, and the minutes underscore deep divisions over whether a softening job market or persistent inflation poses the greater threat to the economy.

The divisions extend to 2026: seven members of the 19-person committee projected no rate cuts next year, eight forecast two or more reductions, and four supported exactly one — a range so wide it leaves the central bank without a coordinated path forward as key economic data remain distorted by the six-week government shutdown.

Federal Reserve policymakers were deeply divided when they cut their benchmark interest rate at their Dec. 9–10 meeting, with minutes released Tuesday showing that even some officials who voted for the quarter-point reduction had considered holding rates steady.

As MSI previously reported, the December vote laid bare a fundamental split on the Federal Open Market Committee over the economy's most pressing threat — a softening job market or inflation that remains above target. <a href="/articles/2025-12-30-fed-december-rate-cut-a-close-call-as-officials-split-on-inflation-versus-jobs">That reporting covered the decision itself</a>; Tuesday's minutes detail how close and contested the deliberations were inside the room.

The committee approved the cut 9 to 3 — an unusual level of dissent for a body that typically moves by consensus — with two members voting to leave rates unchanged and one pushing for a larger half-point reduction.

## The dissenters

The two officials who voted against any cut were Jeffrey Schmid, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, and Austan Goolsbee, president of the Chicago Fed.

The third dissent came from the opposite direction. Fed governor Stephen Miran, appointed by President Donald Trump in September, said the quarter-point reduction was too small and argued for a half-point cut.

The minutes do not attribute arguments to specific individuals, but the vote tallies are public record.

## A committee flying with outdated instruments

The minutes show the committee confronted an unusually acute data problem. The six-week government shutdown delayed key statistics on jobs, inflation, and growth, leaving officials to rely on outdated figures when they gathered Dec. 9–10, the minutes said.

The available labor-market data were sobering. The unemployment rate stood at 4.6% in December, a four-year high, according to Federal Reserve data. Government figures showed employers had cut about 40,000 jobs in October and November.

Fed Chair Jerome Powell said at a Dec. 10 news conference that monthly job gains from April through September had averaged about 40,000 — a figure he said could be revised lower by as much as 60,000 upon updated data, which would imply employers had actually shed an average of around 20,000 jobs a month during that stretch.

"It's a labor market that seems to have significant downside risks," Powell told reporters. "People care about that. That's their jobs."

## Inflation still above target

Inflation complicated the calculus in the other direction. Headline annual inflation cooled to 2.7% in November, down from 3% in September, according to government data — but economists said those figures may have been distorted by the shutdown, which forced the government to estimate many price changes rather than measure them directly.

The Fed's preferred core inflation measure, which strips out food and energy, ran at 2.83% year-over-year in December, according to Federal Reserve data, holding above the central bank's 2% target.

When the Fed reduces its key rate, borrowing costs for homes, cars, and credit cards can fall over time, though market forces also shape those rates.

## No consensus on 2026

The divisions extended to the Fed's quarterly economic projections, released alongside the December rate decision. Seven of the 19 committee members foresaw no rate cuts in 2026, while eight projected two or more reductions and four supported exactly one.

That spread left the central bank without a coordinated path into the new year. The minutes noted that some officials who backed the December cut did so only tentatively, saying they wanted to see more economic data before committing to further reductions.

Whether the next move is a cut, a hold, or a reversal will depend heavily on data the shutdown delayed — and on whether the labor market continues to weaken.
