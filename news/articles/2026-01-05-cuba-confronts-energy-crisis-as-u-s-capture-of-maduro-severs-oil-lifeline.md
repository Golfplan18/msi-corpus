HAVANA — Cuban officials lowered flags before dawn Monday to mourn 32 security officers they say were killed in the U.S. weekend strike in Venezuela, as residents across the island confronted the prospect that President Nicolás Maduro's capture could sever the oil supplies keeping Cuba's economy alive.

The two governments had grown so intertwined that Cuban soldiers and security agents routinely served as Maduro's bodyguards, while Venezuelan petroleum has sustained the island through years of chronic blackouts and food shortages. Cuban authorities said the 32 officers were killed "after fierce resistance in direct combat against the attackers, or as a result of the bombing of the facilities."

Venezuela had been shipping Cuba an average of 35,000 barrels of oil per day in the three months before the strike — roughly a quarter of the island's total demand — and energy experts warn that losing that supply without a replacement would be catastrophic for a country already teetering on economic collapse.

## A dependency decades in the making

Many observers say Cuba, an island of about 10 million people, had exerted a remarkable degree of influence over Venezuela, an oil-rich nation with three times as many people. That relationship ran in both directions: Venezuelan oil kept Cuban households and industries from going dark, while Cuban security forces helped keep Maduro in power.

"Blackouts have been significant, and that is with Venezuela still sending some oil," said Ricardo Torres, a Cuban economist at American University in Washington. "Imagine a future now in the short term losing that. It's a catastrophe."

Jorge Piñón, a Cuban energy expert at the University of Texas at Austin Energy Institute, said the critical question now is whether the U.S. will permit Venezuela to continue supplying Cuba with oil under whatever government follows Maduro.

"The question to which we don't have an answer, which is critical: Is the U.S. going to allow Venezuela to continue supplying Cuba with oil?" Piñón said.

## Alternate suppliers seen as long shots

Cuba's options for replacing Venezuelan oil are narrow. Mexico once supplied the island with 22,000 barrels of oil per day, but that figure dropped to 7,000 barrels after U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio visited Mexico City in early September, according to Piñón.

"I don't see Mexico jumping in right now," Piñón said. "The U.S. government would go bonkers."

Russia currently sends Cuba about 2 million barrels of oil per year — far short of what Venezuela had been providing. Piñón said Russia has the capacity to increase that supply but questioned whether it has the political will to do so.

"Russia has the capability to fill the gap. Do they have the political commitment, or the political desire to do so? I don't know," Piñón said.

Torres said he doubts Russia would risk its negotiations with the United States over Ukraine to deepen its support for Cuba.

"Meddling with Cuba could jeopardize your negotiation with the U.S. around Ukraine. Why would you do it? Ukraine is far more important," he said.

Torres suggested that Cuba's best path to attracting Chinese support would be to open its doors to private-sector activity and reduce the size of the public sector — a significant shift for the island's government.

## Trump signals pressure campaign on Cuba

Trump warned Saturday that Cuba's already ailing economy will suffer further from Maduro's removal.

"It's going down," Trump said of Cuba. "It's going down for the count."

The Trump administration has stated openly that toppling Maduro will help advance a longer-standing goal of dealing a blow to the Cuban government. Cuban authorities responded Saturday by calling on the international community to stand up to what they described as "state terrorism."

On the streets of Havana, residents expressed fear and defiance in equal measure. Berta Luz Sierra Molina, 75, could barely speak. "I can't talk. I have no words," she said, sobbing and placing a hand over her face. Regina Méndez, 63, said she was too old to join the military but was not willing to concede.

"Give me a rifle, and I'll go fight," Méndez said.

## Venezuela's oil industry faces its own uncertain path

Trump told NBC News on Monday that the U.S. government could reimburse oil companies that invest in Venezuela to maintain and increase production, and suggested rebuilding the country's neglected oil infrastructure could happen in less than 18 months.

"It'll be a lot of money," Trump said. "A tremendous amount of money will have to be spent and the oil companies will spend it, and then they'll get reimbursed by us or through revenue."

Venezuela currently produces an average of about 1.1 million barrels of oil per day, according to the Associated Press — down from 3.5 million barrels per day in 1999, before a government takeover of the majority of oil interests and a combination of corruption, mismanagement, and U.S. economic sanctions drove output sharply lower. Whether and how quickly the industry can be rebuilt remains deeply uncertain given the country's unstable political situation and the billions of dollars that would be required.

Piñón noted that Cuba lacks the foreign currency to purchase oil on international markets, leaving the island with few options it can pursue on its own.

"The only ally that they have left out there with oil is Russia," he said.
