The European Union is preparing sweeping trade countermeasures against the United States in response to President Donald Trump's threats over Greenland, according to reporting from Brussels. The measures, referred to as the EU's "trade bazooka," could include steep tariffs on American goods, restrictions on investment, and bans from public contracts—potentially closing the bloc's 450-million-customer market to U.S. companies. French President Emmanuel Macron is among European leaders touting the deployment of what officials describe as the bloc's most aggressive economic tool.

The potential conflict threatens one of the world's largest trade relationships. In 2024, EU-U.S. trade in goods and services totaled 1.7 trillion euros ($2 trillion), making the two economic blocs deeply interdependent—and an actual trade war could drive up prices for consumers and businesses on both sides of the Atlantic.

## What the EU's 'trade bazooka' is

The European Commission established the Anti-Coercion Instrument in 2021 after China restricted trade to Lithuania following that country's diplomatic ties with Taiwan, which Beijing claims as its own. The instrument gives the EU a toolkit of economic measures to deploy against countries the bloc deems to be applying coercive pressure.

The measures are comprehensive: they can curtail the export and import of goods and services, bar countries or companies from European Union public tenders, or limit foreign direct investment. In its most severe form, the bazooka would essentially close off access to the EU's 450-million-customer market to targeted countries.

"The primary objective of the ACI is deterrence," the European Commission stated in guidance issued before the Greenland dispute. "The instrument will, therefore, be most successful if there is no need to use it."

## How activation works

Using the trade bazooka would not happen quickly. The European Union would need at least six months to activate the Anti-Coercion Instrument against another nation. That timeline gives both sides room for negotiation and political maneuvering before the most severe restrictions took effect.

Most EU member states remain skeptical about actually deploying such a drastic tool, according to reporting from Brussels. French President Macron has been more willing to discuss the option, but consensus among European capitals would be required to put the bazooka into use.

## The scale of US-EU trade

The economic stakes underscore why both sides would face pressure to negotiate. In 2024, trade in goods and services between the EU and United States totaled 1.7 trillion euros ($2 trillion), according to Eurostat—an average of 4.6 billion euros per day in two-way flows.

Europe's largest exports to the United States include pharmaceuticals, motor vehicles, aircraft, chemicals, medical instruments, wine, and spirits. The United States sends to Europe professional and scientific services such as payment systems and cloud infrastructure, along with oil and gas, pharmaceuticals, medical equipment, aerospace products, and vehicles. A sustained trade conflict would disrupt all of these flows and likely increase costs for consumers and businesses on both continents.

By preparing these countermeasures, the EU is signaling that it may pursue a prolonged economic confrontation with the Trump administration if Greenland remains a point of contention.
