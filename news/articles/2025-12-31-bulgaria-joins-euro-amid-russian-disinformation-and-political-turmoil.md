The adoption deepens Bulgaria's ties to the European Union, but arrives amid polling showing most Bulgarians skeptical of the single currency, a government brought down by anti-corruption protests, and analyst warnings that Russia-aligned parties have deliberately cultivated public opposition to weaken support for Western institutions.

SOFIA, Bulgaria — Bulgaria became the 21st country to join the euro currency union on New Year's Day, completing a years-long integration process that supporters called one of the country's greatest achievements since its 1989 transition from a Soviet-style economy.

As MSI previously reported on [Bulgaria's eurozone accession and the political uncertainty surrounding it](/articles/2025-12-31-bulgaria-joins-eurozone-as-21st-member-amid-political-uncertainty), the historic milestone arrives at a moment of institutional fragility. Analysts say a disinformation campaign coordinated by pro-Russian political actors has been a central driver of public opposition to the changeover.

Anti-euro disinformation spread by pro-Russian politicians and social media aims "to reduce support for the European Union, NATO and Ukraine," said Dimitar Keranov, program coordinator for engaging Central Europe at the German Marshall Fund in Berlin. Social media platforms have circulated false claims that euro adoption could lead to the confiscation of bank accounts, according to the Associated Press.

## Russian-backed parties amplify economic anxiety

Anti-euro rallies in May and September were organized by the pro-Russian Vazrazhdane party, though both demonstrations remained smaller than the nationwide anti-corruption protests that forced the government to resign.

Keranov said Bulgaria's European integration "is not in Moscow's interest at all, so if it can somehow polarize society and weaken support for the European Union that's what it tries to achieve." Euro adoption, he said, is itself a countermeasure: "The further Bulgaria advances in its European integration, the harder it becomes for Russia to influence the country."

## Political instability clouds the milestone

The government successfully brought Bulgarian inflation down to 2.7% earlier this year to meet EU membership criteria, then resigned after less than a year in office under pressure from nationwide anti-corruption protests. The country heads into 2026 without a regular budget, with a new election — the eighth in five years — expected next spring.

Petar Ganev, an analyst at the Sofia-based Institute for Market Economics, warned that the timing undercuts the economic signal euro adoption was meant to send.

"Instead of capitalizing on euro adoption as a strong and positive signal to the international community—investors, debt holders, and those investing in Bulgarian assets and economic activity—we risk sending the opposite message," Ganev said in an interview with the Associated Press.

Ganev said eurozone membership should be seen as an opportunity to address corruption and rule of law, though it alone cannot resolve Bulgaria's recurring cycle of elections and political fragmentation.

## Public opinion divided

Two EU Eurobarometer polls found majorities of Bulgarians skeptical of the changeover. A March poll of 1,017 people showed 53% opposed joining the eurozone and 45% in favor, with a margin of error of about plus or minus 3.1 percentage points. A separate poll conducted between October 9 and November 3 showed about half of Bulgarians opposed the single currency while 42% were in favor.

Inflation has since rebounded to 3.7% — above the 2.7% level the government achieved to satisfy EU entry requirements — stoking fears that merchants will use the currency changeover as cover to raise prices further.

European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde has said that countries joining the eurozone have experienced a slight, transient price rise of 0.2% to 0.4% right after the transition. Price increases at cafes and hair salons, she noted, can be more apparent than real, as businesses may delay updating menus and price lists ahead of the change rather than raise prices because of it.

## Voices from Sofia

Nevelin Petrov, 64, said he welcomed the euro. "Bulgaria is a full member of the European Union, and its rightful place is alongside the other developed and democratic European nations," he said. "I am convinced that the adoption of the euro will contribute to the long-term prosperity of our country."

Darina Vitova, who runs a pedicure salon in Sofia, said the transition was moving too fast, though she welcomed it "in principle."

"The standard of living and incomes in our country are far from those in the richest European countries, while prices here are rising and life for the average person will become more difficult," she said.

## Practical transition

Bulgaria, with a population of 6.4 million, is among the poorest members of the 27-country European Union, with an average monthly wage of 1,300 euros ($1,530). Because the lev has been pegged to the euro since 1999 by law at a fixed rate of one lev per 51 euro cents, local economists expect the formal monetary transition to bring modest rather than dramatic economic changes.

Both currencies will circulate together throughout January for cash payments, with customers receiving euros as change. The lev and euro dual-circulation period closes a 26-year managed-exchange arrangement that had already insulated Bulgarian households from most currency risk.
