Israel's recognition of Somaliland as an independent nation — the first by any country — has drawn militant threats, governmental condemnation, and competing great-power maneuvers into the Horn of Africa and the Middle East, the Associated Press reported Monday.

Somaliland's foreign minister, Abdirahman Dahir Adan, said the territory aims to join the Abraham Accords and described the new relationship with Israel as "a mutually beneficial friendship." But the recognition has alarmed Somalia, the 57-nation Organization of Islamic Cooperation, the African Union, and al-Qaida affiliate al-Shabab, which issued explicit threats against the breakaway territory.

The recognition has focused international attention on Somaliland's position along one of the world's busiest maritime corridors, roughly 100 miles (160 kilometers) from Yemen, where Iran-backed Houthi rebels have been attacking commercial shipping and firing at Israel. It has also reignited a disputed proposal — denied by Somaliland's foreign minister but reported to the AP by U.S. and Israeli officials last year — that Israel had approached Somaliland about resettling Palestinians displaced from Gaza.

## Strategic position

Somaliland broke away from Somalia in 1991 as that country collapsed into conflict and has since maintained relative stability while seeking international recognition.

Its coastline along the Gulf of Aden positions it close to the Houthi threat in Yemen and the Red Sea shipping lanes through which about $1 trillion of goods pass annually, according to the AP. The Houthis have been targeting commercial vessels and firing missiles and drones at Israel in response to the war in Gaza.

"If you are trying to watch, deter or disrupt Houthi maritime activity, a small footprint (in Somaliland) can provide disproportionate utility," said Andreas Krieg, a military analyst at King's College London.

The Houthis threatened Somaliland shortly after Israel's recognition was announced.

## Somaliland's stated goals

Adan said the recognition would open "cooperation with Israel in trade, investment and technology" and expressed hope it would prompt other countries to recognize Somaliland's sovereignty. "There are no limits as to what areas we can work with," he said.

Adan said he was hopeful that Israel's recognition would reduce pressure from Turkey and China, two of Somalia's top benefactors. "Before Israel's recognition, we were worried so much that other powers like Turkey and China would squeeze us," he said.

He dismissed criticism from Mogadishu, calling Somalia a "failed state."

Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Saar visited Somaliland the previous week. Somaliland's president is expected to visit Israel. "This is a natural connection between democratic countries — both in challenging regions," Saar said.

Israel and Somaliland said their ties would include defense cooperation, though officials declined to provide details.

## Militant threats

Al-Shabab, an al-Qaida affiliate that operates from Somalia and rarely carries out attacks in Somaliland, issued a direct warning after the recognition.

"Members of the movement reject Israel's attempt to claim or use parts of our land. We will not accept this, and we will fight against it," al-Shabab spokesperson Sheikh Ali Mohamud Rageal said in an audio message posted on one of the group's sites.

Analysts told the AP that Somaliland's ties with Israel could serve as a rallying cry for Islamic extremists, threatening the territory's reputation as an island of stability in a volatile region.

## Somalia and international condemnation

Somali President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud said Israel's recognition threatens his country's sovereignty. He warned that Somalis would not accept their nation being used by a foreign power accused of harming Palestinian civilians, and that establishing foreign military bases would further destabilize the region.

Somali territory "cannot be divided by a piece of paper written by Israel and signed by (Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin) Netanyahu," Mohamud said in a televised address.

The OIC and the African Union both condemned Israel's recognition. On Monday, Somalia annulled its security and defense agreements with the United Arab Emirates, citing the need to safeguard "unity, territorial integrity, and constitutional order."

## Great-power realignments

The recognition has unsettled a region where Gulf states and other major powers hold competing interests.

The UAE, a key regional ally of Israel, has long invested in Somaliland's port of Berbera. Last week, Saudi Arabia accused the UAE of using Somaliland as a transit point to smuggle the leader of a separatist group out of southern Yemen.

Asher Lubotzky, an analyst at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies, said Somaliland represents one example of an emerging alignment between Israel and the UAE, both of which have sought to align with U.S. foreign policy while countering extremist groups.

Turkey, Somalia's largest investor, and China, which has long viewed Somaliland with suspicion over its ties with Taiwan, were also put on alert, the AP reported. A planned visit to Somalia by Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi did not take place last week; the Chinese embassy cited "scheduling reasons."

Landlocked Ethiopia, Africa's second-most populous country, has remained silent on Israel's recognition. Ethiopia sees Somaliland as a key route to the sea.

Mahad Wasuge, director of the Somali Public Agenda think tank, said the recognition has pushed the region into uncertain territory. "It could increase violence or bring proxy wars, particularly if the Israelis want to have a presence in the port of Berbera to counter threats in the Red Sea," he said.

## Palestinian resettlement question

Adan said Somaliland has had no discussions with Israel about taking in Palestinians displaced from Gaza. However, U.S. and Israeli officials told the AP last year that Israel had approached Somaliland about such a proposal.
