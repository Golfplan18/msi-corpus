**BORYSPIL, Ukraine** — Emergency repair crews in Ukraine's Kyiv region worked through bitter cold Wednesday to restore electricity after relentless Russian barrages on energy infrastructure plunged communities into what Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko described as the longest and broadest blackouts since Russia's full-scale invasion began nearly four years ago.

The outages, compounded by the coldest winter in years, have left some Kyiv homes without electricity for days, forcing families to improvise heat and light while repair crews face the added obstacle of grid collapse each time power briefly returns.

In Boryspil, a town of about 60,000 people in the Kyiv region, workers dismantled and rebuilt burned-out electrical systems in temperatures of minus 15 degrees Celsius (5 degrees Fahrenheit), from early morning until midnight, said Yurii Bryzh, who leads the Boryspil regional department of private electricity provider DTEK.

Crews have managed to restore supply for about four hours a day, but a recurring problem undercuts the effort. "When the power comes back on, people turn on all the electrical equipment that is available in the house," Bryzh told the Associated Press, describing how residents rush to wash, cook and recharge phones. "That collapses the system again," he said.

Kyiv temperatures stood at minus 12 degrees Celsius (10 degrees Fahrenheit) on Wednesday, with the city enduring below-freezing conditions for more than two weeks. Mayor Klitschko said Monday night's strikes caused the biggest electrical outage the capital has faced since Russia's invasion. Some homes have been dark for days. At night, streets are unlit and towering apartment blocks show no light in the windows.

## How residents are coping

Residents described improvised measures to stay warm and sustain basic routines.

Scientists Mykhailo, 39, and Hanna, 43, who gave only their first names for security reasons, said the temperature in the bedroom of their five-year-old daughter Maria had fallen to minus 15 degrees Celsius. The couple has a gas stove for cooking but sleeps together under every blanket in the house. During the day they bring Maria to work with them because the office has a generator; her kindergarten has no heat.

Zinaida Hlyha, 76, heats water on her gas stove and tucks the filled bottles into bed. She declined to frame the hardship as complaint. "Of course it's hard, but if you imagine what our guys in the trenches are going through now, you have to endure," she said. "What can you do? This is war."

Tetiana Tatarenko, whose two sons are fighting at the front, said her fear deepened after a Shahed drone struck the apartment building next door. "It's as if life in the house has stopped, that's the feeling," she said.

Her neighbor, 89-year-old physicist Raisa Derhachova, lives alone. She sometimes plays the piano in what she called "this terrifying cold." "Of course, it's hard to survive this," she said. "We survived World War II, and now this terrible war is upon us."

## The technical toll

The infrastructure challenge compounds the civilian hardship. Russian strikes are targeting power plants and large substations, and replacement equipment such as transformers can take months to procure, said Dennis Sakva, an energy sector analyst at Dragon Capital, a Ukrainian investment firm.

"There are two types of heroes in Ukraine," Sakva said. "They are the military and energy workers."
