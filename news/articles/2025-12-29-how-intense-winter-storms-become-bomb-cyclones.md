## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- - The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration defines bombogenesis as a storm event where central pressure drops at least 24 millibars within 24 hours.
- - Rapidly intensifying storms meeting the bombogenesis threshold produce heavy rain, blizzard conditions, intense winds, downed trees, and widespread power outages.
- - Andrew Orrison, a meteorologist with the National Weather Service, stated that bomb cyclone warnings in forecasts signal significant active weather conditions.
- - Bomb cyclones develop predominantly in fall and winter when frigid Arctic air collides with warmer air masses farther from the poles.
- - Alaska, the Pacific Northwest, and the Great Lakes region are the North American areas most susceptible to bomb cyclone formation.

## When and where bomb cyclones form

Bomb cyclones can occur in any season but develop predominantly in fall and winter, when frigid Arctic air moves south and collides with warmer air masses farther from the poles.

"It's really the clash of those air masses that really kind of helps to generate the areas of low pressure in the first place," Orrison said.

In North America, the regions most susceptible to bomb cyclones include Alaska, the Pacific Northwest, and the Great Lakes area. The phenomenon is rare at lower latitudes.

"It's not common to get bomb cyclones at lower latitudes. So generally speaking, you wouldn't see a bomb cyclone across, let's say, the southern United States," Orrison said.
