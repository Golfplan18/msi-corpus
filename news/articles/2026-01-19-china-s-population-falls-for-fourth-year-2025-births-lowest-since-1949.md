China's population shrank for the fourth consecutive year in 2025, government statistics released Monday showed, with 7.92 million babies born — a 17% drop from the prior year and the lowest birth rate recorded since the Communist government took power in 1949. The total population stood at 1.404 billion, a decline of 3 million from 2024.

The figures underscore deepening demographic pressure on the world's second-most populous nation, which counted 323 million people over the age of 60 — 23% of its population — while its working-age base continues to contract, threatening China's ambitions to transition into a high-tech consumer economy.

## Birth numbers at a historic low

The 2025 birth rate of 5.63 per 1,000 people was the lowest since records began under the People's Republic, established in 1949. The 7.92 million births represented a decline of 1.62 million, or 17%, from the prior year. A slight uptick recorded in 2024 did not mark a lasting reversal; births had declined for seven consecutive years through 2023.

China was surpassed by India as the world's most populous nation in 2023.

Experts estimate China's fertility rate — the average number of children a woman is expected to have over her lifetime — is now around 1, according to the Associated Press. The government last officially reported a rate of 1.3 in 2020. Both figures fall well below the 2.1 rate considered necessary to maintain a population's current size.

## The economic stakes

The demographic shift is straining China's effort to move from labor-intensive industries into a consumer-driven, high-tech economy. With 323 million people over 60 comprising nearly a quarter of the population, the country faces a shrinking pool of workers to support its older citizens.

"The bigger concern is whether economic growth can stay afloat with a shrinking population," said Gary Ng, senior economist for Asia Pacific at the French investment bank Natixis. He said China will eventually need to reform its pension system and broaden its tax base to cover higher government expenditures.

China reported 5% annual economic growth for 2025, according to official data also released Monday. Some analysts expect growth to slow in coming years.

## Policy responses

Chinese authorities have deployed a range of measures to encourage more births, with limited results. In July, the government announced cash subsidies of 3,600 yuan — approximately $500 — per child to families. Starting January 1, China began applying a 13% value-added tax to condoms after removing contraceptives from a tax-exemption list in 2025. Kindergartens, day care centers, and matchmaking services have been added to the tax-exemption list.

Researchers say the policy tools fall short of addressing the structural barriers families cite most often.

"It's these big structural issues which are much harder to tackle, whether it's housing, and work and getting a job and getting started in life and expectations around education," said Stuart Gietel-Basten, director of the Center for Aging Science at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. "It's gonna be difficult to make a major change in those number of births until those are addressed."

Gietel-Basten said young women in particular want workplace policies ensuring they are not penalized for taking time off to have children — changes that would require action by private companies. "It shouldn't be this massive penalty," he said.

## Historical context

For decades, China barred families from having more than one child, sanctioning those who exceeded the limit. The government raised the permitted number of children to two in 2015, then to three in 2021 amid demographic pressure. The shift in policy has not reversed the trend.

Analysts also noted that 2025 was the Year of the Snake in the Chinese zodiac, considered one of the least favored years for having a child under traditional beliefs — a potential contributing factor to the year's low birth numbers.
