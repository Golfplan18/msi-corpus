Chen Zhi, founder of Cambodia's Prince Holding Group and a suspect in one of Southeast Asia's largest alleged online fraud networks, was taken into custody by Chinese authorities Thursday after Cambodia handed him over to Beijing. China's Ministry of Public Security called the transfer a "major achievement" in bilateral law enforcement cooperation.

Chen faces a U.S. federal indictment unsealed in October alleging he defrauded victims in the United States and around the world of billions of dollars; Chinese authorities said they would soon issue arrest warrants for other key figures in his alleged network.

BANGKOK — Chen Zhi, the founder of Cambodia's Prince Holding Group and the subject of a U.S. federal fraud indictment, was taken into Chinese custody Thursday after Cambodian authorities handed him over to Beijing. China's Ministry of Public Security described the transfer as a "major achievement" in bilateral law enforcement cooperation and said it would soon issue arrest warrants for other key figures in Chen's alleged criminal network.

Video shared by the ministry's criminal investigations division showed Chinese police marching Chen from a China Southern Airlines flight with a cloth cover on his head and his hands chained before removing the covering once he had stepped onto the tarmac.

Chen has publicly denied any allegations of wrongdoing.

## A public figure with government ties

Publicly, Chen occupied an unusual position of status in Cambodian society. He held the title of personal adviser to Prime Minister Hun Manet and his father, former Prime Minister Hun Sen, and bore the honorary Cambodian distinction of "neak oknha," a designation reserved for a select few in the country.

His profile as a philanthropist reinforced that standing. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Chen donated $3 million to Cambodia to help purchase vaccines, drawing a personal letter of thanks from then-Prime Minister Hun Sen.

Mark Taylor, who formerly worked on human trafficking issues in Cambodia for the nonprofit Winrock International, said Chen had built up "at least a facade of legitimate businesses that are well connected to the government."

## Rise of Prince Group

Chen grew up in Lianjiang county in China's Fujian province and previously managed an internet cafe before his rise in Cambodia. He founded Prince Group in 2015, starting with what he said was a loan from an uncle in the real estate business.

The group is credited with transforming Sihanoukville from a quiet coastal town into a larger community that came to be known as a gambling and scam hub, according to Radio Free Asia.

Chinese court records tell a more damaging story. Court documents in China show Prince Group is mentioned in at least a dozen judgments, mostly concerning money laundering and online gambling — which is outlawed in mainland China and permitted only in Macau. Prosecutors in China's Sichuan province sentenced 45 people in a case involving what they said was an illegal online gambling operation linked to Prince Group that targeted Chinese nationals as early as 2016.

## U.S. charges and unresolved jurisdiction

In October, U.S. federal prosecutors unsealed an indictment against Chen charging him with defrauding American and international victims of billions of dollars. U.S. prosecutors said Chen boasted of pulling in $30 million a day, personally managed scam compounds, and kept records tracking profits from so-called "pig butchering" schemes — a form of investment fraud in which victims are cultivated over an extended period before being defrauded of their funds. At the time of the indictment, Chen held passports from China, Cambodia, Vanuatu, Cyprus and St. Lucia, according to the filing.

Experts in transnational crime had described Chen as one of the largest operators of online scamming in Cambodia.

It was not clear, as of Thursday, whether U.S. authorities knew about Chen's transfer to Chinese custody or had any role in the proceedings. The U.S. indictment remained outstanding.
