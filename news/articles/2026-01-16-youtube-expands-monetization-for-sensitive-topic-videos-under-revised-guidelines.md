YouTube on Tuesday expanded its advertiser-friendly content policy to allow full ad revenue on videos covering domestic abuse, self-harm, suicide, adult sexual abuse, abortion and sexual harassment, provided the content contains no graphic descriptions or imagery, the Google-owned company announced.

The update, outlined in a video posted to YouTube's Creator Insider channel, extends full monetization eligibility to personal accounts, preventative content and journalistic coverage of those topics — areas where YouTube said its prior guidelines had been too restrictive and were incorrectly demonetizing nongraphic dramatized content.

## What the changes cover

Under the revised guidelines, creators who address the newly eligible topics in a nongraphic way — including those producing dramatized content or first-person narratives — are now eligible to earn full ad revenue. Previously, YouTube said, the degree of graphic or descriptive detail in videos was not considered when determining advertiser friendliness, a gap the company said produced overly broad restrictions.

Ads will remain restricted on videos covering child abuse, child sex trafficking and eating disorders.

"We took a closer look and found our guidelines in this area had become too restrictive and ended up demonetizing uploads like dramatized content," said Conor Kavanagh, YouTube's head of monetization policy experience, in the video announcing the changes. Kavanagh said the company's goal was to "ensure the creators who are telling sensitive stories or producing dramatized content have the opportunity to earn ad revenue while respecting advertiser choice and industry sentiment."

The updated advertiser-friendly content guidelines were also published Tuesday with specific definitions and examples for each category.

## Creator workarounds and policy history

The change follows years of documented creator workarounds. Some video makers attempted to evade demonetization by substituting symbols and numbers for letters or using alternative vocabulary for restricted subjects. The most prevalent cross-platform example, according to YouTube, has been the term "unalive" used as a substitute for suicide-related language.

Tuesday's update is the second significant monetization policy revision in recent months. In July, YouTube eased restrictions on profanity, making videos that use strong language in the first seven seconds eligible for full ad revenue. YouTube said it updates its policies in response to creator feedback.
