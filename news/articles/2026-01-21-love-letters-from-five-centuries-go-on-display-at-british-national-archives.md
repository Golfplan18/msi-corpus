A public exhibition opening Saturday at the British National Archives showcases love letters spanning five centuries, featuring intimate correspondence from royalty, politicians, celebrities, spies and ordinary people. The exhibition, titled "Love Letters," runs through April 12 with free admission.

The display ranges from medieval heartbreak songs and 20th-century classified ads seeking same-sex romance to wartime letters between sweethearts. Curator Victoria Iglikowski-Broad said the exhibition aims to "open up the potential of what a love letter can be," with expressions of love found in "all sorts of places, and surprising places."

## Royal Romance and Tragedy

Royal romance and tragedy thread through the collection. One centerpiece is a poignant letter to Queen Elizabeth I from Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, written days before his death in 1588. With "his last lettar" written on the outside—spelling at the time was idiosyncratic—the missive was found at the queen's bedside when she died almost 15 years later.

A letter written in 1541 by Catherine Howard, fifth wife of King Henry VIII, to her secret lover Thomas Culpeper carries what archives historian Neil Johnston described as a tone of "restrained panic." Catherine signed off with "yours as long as life endures"—a promise that turned out not to be long. The king discovered the affair and both Catherine and Culpeper were executed for treason.

The exhibition preserves a letter by Queen Henrietta Maria to King Charles I addressed to "my dear heart." Such intimate letters between monarchs are rare, since Britain's royal family guards its private papers closely. The letter was found among possessions left behind by Charles after his defeat in the English Civil War. Charles was tried, convicted and executed in 1649. The letter ended up in Parliament's archives, which were transferred to the National Archives last year.

"We don't have very many intimate letters between monarchs like this," Johnston said. "This is a little gem within the disaster of the English Civil War."

## Love Across the Social Spectrum

The exhibition balances grand historical figures with the voices of ordinary people. Jane Austen's handwritten will from 1817 shows her leaving almost everything to her beloved sister Cassandra. An 1851 petition from Daniel Rush, an unemployed 71-year-old weaver, begs authorities not to separate him and his wife by sending them to different workhouses.

Both appear alongside the Instrument of Abdication through which King Edward VIII gave up the throne in 1936 so he could marry Wallis Simpson, a twice-divorced American whom he called "the woman I love."

"There is a lot of connection in these seemingly very different items," Iglikowski-Broad said. "In common they have just this human feeling of love … that the sacrifice is actually worth it for love."

## Love Lost and Danger

A never-before-displayed 1944 letter from British intelligence officer John Cairncross to his former girlfriend Gloria Barraclough reflects on what might have been. "Would we have broken off, I wondered, if we had known what was coming?" he wrote. Years later, Cairncross was unmasked as a Soviet spy.

Lord Alfred Douglas's letter asking Queen Victoria to pardon his lover Oscar Wilde tells another story of love and legal peril. Wilde had been sentenced to two years in prison for gross indecency after Douglas's father revealed their relationship.

The exhibition also features a 1956 letter in which the father of London gangster twins Reggie and Ronnie Kray implores a court to go easy on the brothers because "all their concern in life is to do good to everybody." The letter stands as testimony to how love—familial, romantic or passionate—transcends the boundaries between famous and forgotten.
