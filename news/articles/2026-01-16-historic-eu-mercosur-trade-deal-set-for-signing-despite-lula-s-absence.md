The European Union and four South American nations are poised to finalize a historic free trade agreement on Saturday in Paraguay, capping more than 25 years of negotiations. Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva will not attend the ceremony, instead sending Foreign Minister Mauro Vieira to represent Brazil.

The pact creates one of the world's largest free-trade zones, uniting some 720 million people and an estimated $22 trillion in gross domestic product, and grants South American agricultural producers unprecedented access to European markets at preferential rates.

The European Union and four South American nations celebrated Friday as Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen marked the final step toward a free trade agreement set to be signed the following day in Paraguay. The pact, negotiated over more than 25 years, will create one of the world's largest free-trade zones, uniting approximately 720 million people and an estimated $22 trillion in gross domestic product.

## The Deal Takes Shape

Lula underscored the significance of the moment during remarks at Itamaraty Palace in Rio de Janeiro. "It was more than 25 years of suffering and attempts to get a deal," he said. "Tomorrow in Asuncion, we will make history by creating one of the world's largest free trade areas, bringing together some 720 million people and a GDP of over $22 trillion."

The agreement covers Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, and Uruguay—members of Mercosur since its formation decades ago. Bolivia, the bloc's newest member, did not participate in negotiations but may join the accord in coming years. Argentina's Javier Milei, Uruguay's Yamandú Orsi, and Paraguay's Santiago Peña are expected to attend Saturday's signing. Foreign Minister Mauro Vieira will represent Brazil in the ceremony.

Von der Leyen, standing alongside Lula, offered warm praise for the Brazilian leader's efforts. "The political leadership, the personal commitment and passion that you have shown in the last weeks and months, dear Lula, are truly second to none," she said.

## Agricultural Access and Industrial Ambitions

The agreement grants South American nations, renowned for fertile land and skilled farmers, increased preferential access to Europe's vast agricultural market. Brazil's government investment agency Apex estimates that EU-bound agricultural exports—including instant coffee, poultry, and orange juice—will generate approximately $7 billion in coming years.

Yet Lula made clear that Mercosur aims beyond commodity sales. "We want to produce and sell industrial goods with higher added value," he said, signaling the bloc's desire to move further up the manufacturing chain.

## Why Lula Stayed Home

Lula's decision to skip Saturday's ceremony caught the attention of analysts studying the deal's political dynamics. Experts attributed the move to possible frustration that the agreement was not finalized in December, when Brazil held Mercosur's rotating presidency.

Flavia Loss, an international relations professor at the Foundation School of Sociology and Politics in São Paulo, interpreted the absence as a deliberate signal. "I see Lula's absence as signaling: 'The deal is important but we're not going to change everything for them,'" Loss said. She suggested that Brazil and Mercosur are asserting their position and demanding equal footing with the EU.

## Mercosur in Global Trade Realignment

European Council President António Costa stressed that the agreement extends well beyond commodity trade. "It's a trade deal, but it's also an investment agreement," Costa said at a press conference in Copacabana. "Because our policy is not, unlike the Chinese approach, to simply arrive and extract the minerals so that the added value is generated elsewhere. There is no longer that post-colonial logic of going around extracting natural resources."

The accord lands at a moment when global trade patterns are shifting. President Donald Trump has moved aggressively to withdraw the United States from multilateral trade arrangements, while China has pursued extensive trade and lending strategies to build influence across the region.

Von der Leyen cast the agreement as a statement about the broader architecture of global commerce. "This is the power of partnership and openness. This is the power of friendship and understanding between peoples and regions across oceans," she said. "And this is how we create real prosperity—prosperity that is shared. Because we agree that international trade is not a zero-sum game."

Experts note that the agreement represents a victory for the EU and Mercosur as both major powers vie for influence in the resource-rich region. Roberto Goulart Menezes, an international relations professor at the University of Brasilia, described the deal as evidence of Mercosur's enduring relevance. The agreement demonstrates that the EU is betting on diversification of partners and multilateralism, he said, a posture at odds with Trump's approach to international commerce.

"For Mercosur, it illustrates that the bloc is relevant, despite accusations of being insignificant and on its last legs," Goulart said.
