The escalation highlights a persistent hazard across the Philippine archipelago, where thousands of impoverished residents have long settled inside officially prohibited zones around Mayon, drawn by fertile land and economic activity despite the mountain's documented record of deadly eruptions.

MANILA, Philippines — Philippine authorities raised the alert level around Mayon Volcano to 3 on a 5-step scale Tuesday and ordered the evacuation of more than 2,800 villagers from the mountain's designated permanent danger zone after detecting intermittent rockfalls and pyroclastic flows from its peak crater, officials said Wednesday.

An additional 600 residents living outside the permanent danger zone evacuated voluntarily to government-run emergency shelters in the northeastern province of Albay, Claudio Yucot, regional director of the Office of Civil Defense, said.

Troops, police, and disaster-mitigation personnel helped move the 2,800-plus villagers — drawn from 729 households within a 6-kilometer (3.7-mile) radius of the crater — to safety, Albay provincial officials said. That radius constitutes a permanent danger zone that authorities have long demarcated with concrete warning signs.

Teresito Bacolcol, the country's chief volcanologist, told the Associated Press that the mountain is already in eruption, though a restrained one. "This is already an eruption, a quiet one, with lava accumulating up the peak and swelling the dome, which cracked in some parts and resulted in rockfalls, some as big as cars," Bacolcol said.

He said it is too early to determine whether the activity would escalate into a major violent eruption, noting the absence of key warning signs: a spike in volcanic earthquakes and elevated sulfur dioxide emissions. Alert level 5 — two steps above the current classification — would indicate a major explosive eruption with violent ejections of ash and debris is underway.

## The volcano and the danger zone

Mayon, which stands 2,462 meters (8,007 feet) and is prized by tourists for its near-perfect cone shape, is the most active of the Philippines' 24 restive volcanoes. It has erupted 54 times since records began in 1616.

Entry to the permanent danger zone on its foothills is prohibited, but thousands of villagers have flouted the ban for generations, making the zone their home or maintaining farms there. Lucrative businesses — sand and gravel quarrying and sightseeing tours — have also operated openly despite the restrictions and the mountain's documented eruption history, according to the Associated Press.

## A pattern of precarious settlement

The situation around Mayon reflects conditions across the Philippine archipelago, where many impoverished residents live on or near active hazards: volcano slopes, landslide-prone mountainsides, vulnerable coastlines, earthquake fault lines, and flood-prone lowlands. About 20 typhoons and storms strike the Philippines each year. The country sits along the Pacific "Ring of Fire," the arc of fault lines around the Pacific Ocean basin where volcanic eruptions and earthquakes are frequent.

The human cost of living within Mayon's reach is written into the landscape. In the town of Cagsawa, about 13 kilometers (8 miles) from the volcano, the belfry of a 16th-century Franciscan stone church protrudes from the ground — all that remains of a baroque church buried by volcanic mudflow in an 1814 eruption that killed approximately 1,200 people, including many who had sought refuge inside the church.
