The designations target a movement whose ideology has simultaneously fueled elected governments, armed insurgencies and rival bans across the Middle East, deepening a fault line between regional powers that support the Brotherhood and those that have outlawed it.

The Trump administration on Tuesday designated the Lebanese, Jordanian and Egyptian chapters of the Muslim Brotherhood as terrorist organizations, escalating U.S. engagement with a regional dispute over the transnational Sunni Islamist movement that has shaped political Islam across the Middle East for nearly a century.

The State Department named al-Jamaa al-Islamiya — the Lebanese branch — a foreign terrorist organization, the most severe designation available under U.S. law and one that makes providing material support to the group a criminal offense, according to the Associated Press. The Treasury Department separately listed the Jordanian and Egyptian branches as specially designated global terrorists, citing their support for Hamas.

The U.S. said the three chapters engage in or facilitate violence and destabilization campaigns that harm their own regions, American citizens and U.S. interests.

## A movement founded in 1928

The Muslim Brotherhood was founded in Egypt in 1928 by Hassan al-Banna, a schoolteacher who believed Islamic teachings should form the basis of governance. In its early decades the group focused largely on social services before turning to militancy, with an armed wing that fought British colonialists and Israel. Al-Banna was assassinated in Cairo in 1929, two months after the Egyptian prime minister he was implicated in killing.

The group witnessed a significant revival in the 1970s under President Anwar Sadat, who tolerated the Brotherhood as a counterweight to leftist opponents. The group formally foreswore violence during that period.

By 2005, during the 30-year rule of Hosni Mubarak, the technically banned but tolerated Brotherhood had become Egypt's strongest political opposition, winning a fifth of seats in parliament. It rose to power after the 2011 popular uprising that toppled Mubarak, but fueled fears that it aimed to monopolize power. After protests over President Mohammed Morsi's divisive rule, the Egyptian army ousted the group in 2013 and has suppressed it heavily since. The Brotherhood's supreme guide, Mohammed Badie, remains in Egyptian custody under several life sentences, the last of which was upheld in July 2021.

## Hamas and the Lebanese armed wing

Hamas, the Palestinian militant group whose October 7, 2023, attack on southern Israel triggered the war in Gaza, traces its origins directly to the Brotherhood. Formed in December 1987 in Gaza at the outset of the first Palestinian intifada, Hamas defined itself in its founding charter as a Palestinian branch of the Brotherhood. It called for armed resistance and the establishment of an Islamic state across historic Palestine.

The Lebanese chapter, al-Jamaa al-Islamiya, is a Sunni political party that also maintains an armed wing. After the outbreak of the Israel-Hamas war, that wing joined forces with the Shiite militant group Hezbollah in launching rockets across the border into Israel. Mohammed Takkoush, the al-Jamaa al-Islamiya leader, told the Associated Press at the time that his group and Hezbollah "supported different sides in regional conflicts and Syria and Yemen but put their differences aside to fight Israel."

## Regional divisions

The Muslim Brotherhood's ideology has exposed deep fault lines among Sunni regional powers. Turkey and Qatar have been sympathetic to the Brotherhood, while Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Egypt view the group as a threat and have moved against it.

Jordan announced a sweeping ban on the Brotherhood earlier in 2025, including the potential closure of the country's largest opposition party, after accusing the group of planning attacks. The monarchy had banned the Brotherhood a decade ago but officially licensed a splinter group and continued tolerating the Islamic Action Front while restricting some of its activities.

The Brotherhood's leaders maintain that the organization renounced violence decades ago and seeks Islamic governance through elections and other peaceful means. Critics, including multiple governments across the region, contend that the group's continued links to armed offshoots undermine those claims.
