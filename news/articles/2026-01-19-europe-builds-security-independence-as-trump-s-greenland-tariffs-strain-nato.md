European Union leaders used terms including "intimidation," "threats," and "blackmail" to describe President Donald Trump's warning to impose new tariffs on nations opposing American control of Greenland, as European governments accelerated a coordinated push toward military and economic independence from the United States one year into Trump's second term, the Associated Press reported Sunday.

Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen has warned that should Trump attempt to annex Greenland, a semiautonomous Danish territory, "then everything stops … including our NATO."

The warnings follow a year in which the United States reduced arms supplies to Ukraine, U.S. officials told European allies they must manage their own security, and Trump threatened to seize territory belonging to a NATO partner — developments that have propelled Europe toward a new multibillion-euro arms fund, eased defense-spending rules, and symbolic troop deployments to Greenland itself.

BRUSSELS — "We are at the very early stage of a rather deep political-military crisis," Maria Martisiute, an analyst at the European Policy Centre, told the Associated Press on Sunday. "There is a greater realization, even though political leaders will not like to admit it, that America has abandoned NATO."

Martisiute's assessment captures a shift that European governments spent much of 2025 trying to avoid acknowledging publicly. One year after President Donald Trump returned to the White House, European faith in the transatlantic alliance has eroded sharply, driven by curtailed U.S. arms supplies to Ukraine, explicit American demands that Europe bear its own security burden, and Trump's repeated threats to seize Greenland from Denmark, a NATO ally.

## A year of delivered warnings

When Trump took office in January 2025, European NATO allies were waiting to learn his plans for Ukraine, then entering its fourth year of war with Russia. Within weeks, U.S. arms supplies and funds to Ukraine began to dry up, the AP reported.

In February 2025, U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth addressed NATO allies at alliance headquarters, telling European countries and Canada that the United States had priorities elsewhere and that Europe must handle security in its own backyard, the AP reported. Hegseth said Ukraine would not join the alliance and that Russian-seized Ukrainian territory would not be returned.

Later that month, Vice President JD Vance traveled to Munich, where he met with the leader of a far-right party during Germany's federal election campaign. Vance argued that Europe's primary threat was internal rather than external and said that free speech was "in retreat" across the continent.

Friedrich Merz, who won the German election that followed, responded that "in view of the increasing threat situation," Germany and Europe "must now very quickly make very big efforts, very quickly," to strengthen their defense capabilities. Merz has since become chancellor.

## Europe's security response

Over the past year, the EU created a multibillion-euro fund to buy arms and ammunition, with an emphasis on sourcing them from European companies and weaning member states off U.S. suppliers, the AP reported. Debt rules were eased for defense spending. Money was directed into Ukraine's defense industry. In December 2025, European leaders agreed to pay for most of Ukraine's military and economic needs for the following two years as Kyiv approached bankruptcy.

A new U.S. national security strategy further strained the relationship. The AP reported that the document described European allies as weak, offered tacit support to far-right political parties, and criticized European speech and migration policies.

European Council President Antonio Costa warned the U.S. against interfering in European affairs. Merz said the strategy reinforced the need for Europe to become "much more independent" from the United States.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said work has begun on a European security strategy aimed at responding to "the geopolitical changes in our world and to give an appropriate answer to that."

## Troops sent to Greenland

Last week, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Norway, Sweden, and the Netherlands sent small numbers of troops to Greenland — a deployment the AP described as highly symbolic in the message of resolve it sent to Washington. French President Emmanuel Macron said the deployments were about standing "at the side of a sovereign state to protect its territory."

"Europe is being shaken from some of its certainties," Macron told French military chiefs. "It sometimes has allies that we thought were predictable, fearless, always by our side, who are now causing us to doubt a lot, or are even turning against those who expected it the least."

Eight European countries targeted by Trump's tariff threats issued a joint statement saying they "stand firmly behind" the principles of sovereignty and territorial integrity. "Tariff threats undermine transatlantic relations and risk a dangerous downward spiral," the statement said.

## The alliance's first-term near-collapse recalled

Former NATO Secretary-General Jens Stoltenberg, who served during both of Trump's terms, wrote in a recent memoir that the first term had pushed the alliance to the brink. "I feared that NATO was about to stop functioning," Stoltenberg wrote, recounting the moment Trump threatened to walk out of a 2018 NATO summit.

On Ukraine, Trump floated a 28-point plan to end the war that would have acquiesced to many Russian demands, according to the AP. The plan was reworked and talks have continued, but without Russian President Vladimir Putin's participation. Trump mostly blames Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy for the stalemate, the AP reported.
