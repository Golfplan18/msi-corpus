SpaceX's Starlink satellite internet service began offering free access to users in Iran as of Tuesday, activists told the Associated Press, giving demonstrators a stronger tool to bypass a government-imposed communications blackout as a death toll from nationwide protests surpassed 2,500.

SpaceX also pushed a firmware update to help Starlink receivers in Iran evade government jamming of satellite signals, according to those activists. The company did not officially announce either step and did not respond to a request for comment.

The moves come as protests underway since Dec. 28 over Iran's faltering economy and the collapse of its currency have killed more than 2,500 people — primarily protesters but also security personnel — according to the U.S.-based Human Rights Activists News Agency. Iran imposed a complete shutdown of telecommunications and internet access for its 85 million residents on Jan. 8, in what activists described as the most severe communications suppression the Islamic Republic has ever imposed.

## Satellite footage altered outside world's picture of the protests

Mehdi Yahyanejad, an Iranian whose nonprofit Net Freedom Pioneers has helped smuggle Starlink units into Iran, said the satellite service has already changed international understanding of the protests' scale.

"Starlink has been crucial," Yahyanejad said in an interview from Los Angeles, pointing to video that emerged last Sunday showing rows of bodies at a forensic medical center near Tehran.

"That showed a few hundred bodies on the ground, that came out because of Starlink," he said. "I think that those videos from the center pretty much changed everyone's understanding of what's happening because they saw it with their own eyes."

Iran began allowing outgoing international mobile calls on Tuesday, but calls from outside the country into Iran remain blocked, the AP reported.

## Cat-and-mouse with Iranian security services

Starlink is banned in Iran by telecommunications regulations, and activists say users risk espionage charges that can carry the death penalty. Despite that, more than 50,000 Starlink units are estimated to have been smuggled into Iran since 2022, when the first units entered the country during protests over mandatory headscarf laws, according to Ahmad Ahmadian, executive director of Holistic Resilience, a Los Angeles-based organization that was responsible for getting some of the first units inside.

After the experience of a June communications blackout during a 12-day war with Israel proved less effective at cutting information flows than the government hoped, security services adopted more extreme tactics, Ahmadian said — including jamming Starlink's radio signals and GPS systems, relying on informers, and raiding apartments where satellite dishes have been spotted. Iranians have responded by disguising receivers as solar panels and using virtual private networks to conceal their IP addresses.

After Holistic Resilience passed reports of the jamming efforts to SpaceX, Ahmadian said, the company pushed its firmware update to work around them.

"The government is using every tool in its toolbox," Ahmadian said. He added that jamming attempts had only been effective in certain urban areas, suggesting security services lack the resources to block Starlink more broadly.

## Trump cited Starlink help for protesters

President Donald Trump told reporters aboard Air Force One two days before the firmware update that he planned to ask Elon Musk to provide Starlink help for Iranian protesters. Trump's press secretary later confirmed the call took place, though the AP reported it is not clear whether that request prompted SpaceX to act.

Musk has made Starlink free during several natural disasters, and Ukraine has relied heavily on the service since Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022. Musk drew criticism in the Ukraine context after he refused to extend Starlink coverage to support a planned Ukrainian counterattack in Russian-occupied Crimea. Ahmadian said that decision was a wake-up call but that he could see no comparable reason for Musk to act similarly in Iran.

## Risks of relying on a single company's network

Julia Voo, who heads the International Institute for Strategic Studies' Cyber Power and Future Conflict Program in Singapore, said activists' dependence on a single company's satellite network "creates a single point of failure," though she acknowledged no comparable alternatives currently exist.

Starlink has secured operating licenses in more than 120 countries, including some governed by authoritarian rulers who have persecuted journalists and protesters. China has been exploring ways to hunt and destroy Starlink satellites, Voo said, and governments around the world are watching closely as the service demonstrates an ability to pierce government-imposed terrestrial blackouts.

"It's just going to result in more efforts to broaden controls over various ways of communication, for those in Iran and everywhere else watching," she said.
