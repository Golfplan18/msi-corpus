## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- The U.S. State Department has launched a review that could result in the closure of some or all of Mexico’s 53 consulates in the United States, without publicly explaining the reason.
- The Los Angeles consulate, the busiest, serves thousands of people each week with passport renewals, birth registrations, and legal referrals for relatives caught in the Trump administration’s immigration crackdown.
- Mexican officials, including former ambassador Arturo Sarukhan, warn that closures would sever a lifeline for the estimated 37 million Mexican nationals and Mexican Americans who depend on consular services, and would violate the 1963 Vienna Convention on Consular Relations.
- The review is unfolding as far-right commentators and Trump-aligned media have accused the consulates of election interference and encouraging migration; conservative author Peter Schweizer’s group has filed public records requests seeking documents on alleged “electioneering.”
- The State Department also suspended new visas for Mexican nationals this month, citing Mexico’s refusal to accept U.S. deportation flights of third-country nationals, heightening bilateral tensions.

The State Department review, first reported in early May, has not been explained publicly. Spokespeople did not make themselves available for an interview and did not respond to requests for comment about the review’s scope, its timeline, or what criteria would be used to decide which consulates to close. The lack of clarity has deepened anxiety among the millions of people who rely on Mexico’s 53 consulates — the largest consular network operated by any country in the United States — for routine documentation and increasingly for legal help in the face of the administration’s aggressive immigration enforcement.

Carlos Gonzalez, a 58-year-old construction worker from Jalisco, visited the Los Angeles consulate this week to renew his passport and obtain a birth certificate for his U.S.-born daughter. “If they close this place, I don’t know where I’d go,” he said. “Everything official, you need to do it here.” Azucena Avilés, a community organizer who works with Mexican families in Southern California, said the consulate has become a crucial clearinghouse for referrals to immigration attorneys as detentions have risen.
