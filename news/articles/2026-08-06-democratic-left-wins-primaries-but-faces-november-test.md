## Statewide victory expands the movement’s coalition beyond urban strongholds

Tuesday’s primaries in Kansas, Michigan, Missouri, Virginia and Washington featured high Democratic turnout and contests between moderates and candidates on the party’s left, according to Ben Davis’ analysis published by The Guardian.

Davis identified El-Sayed’s Michigan Senate victory as the most important result. Donavan McKinney and Will Lawrence also won congressional primaries, while Chris Gilmer-Hill won a state legislative contest, the analysis said.

The results showed continued growth for progressives and socialists inside the Democratic Party, but Davis said the victories were narrower than polling and media coverage had led some observers to expect. The analysis described the Democratic electorate as more progressive and more willing to challenge party leadership, while cautioning that the shift was neither uniform nor complete.

Davis estimated that an additional 10% to 15% of Democratic primary voters are now consistently willing to support left candidates beyond the existing left-wing base. He also wrote that many Democratic voters remain moderate, cross-pressured or primarily motivated by individual candidates.

The analysis placed the Michigan result in the context of the movement’s earlier electoral strategy. The left first built support through city council and state legislative races, then expanded into mayoral and congressional contests in deep-blue areas, Davis wrote.

El-Sayed’s victory represented a broader test because Michigan is a swing state and the primary coalition reached beyond the movement’s urban base, according to the analysis. Davis wrote that the left had not previously won a genuinely competitive statewide Democratic primary of that kind.

The result also challenged expectations that the left’s gains would arrive through a single decisive shift. Davis described the effort to build a durable left-wing majority inside the Democratic Party as a gradual process, rather than one marked by a single tipping point.

The next test will come in November, when Democratic nominees face Republicans in general elections. Davis wrote that primary contests against moderate candidates, party institutions and well-funded outside groups such as Aipac and the crypto lobby do not replicate the conditions of a swing-state general election.

El-Sayed must show that his primary coalition can expand enough to win outside deep-blue territory, Davis wrote. The same question could apply to Francesca Hong if she advances in Wisconsin.

Davis linked that question to Democratic efforts to win the Senate and retain governorships, as well as to the longer-term prospects of left candidates in a 2028 presidential primary. The analysis said the movement’s electoral project depends on building coalitions capable of winning and governing with durable majorities.

Davis wrote that Tuesday’s results activated young voters and brought Arab and Muslim voters back into the Democratic fold. He also cautioned that every primary remains dependent on candidates, organization, spending and local conditions, leaving the movement’s performance in November unresolved.
