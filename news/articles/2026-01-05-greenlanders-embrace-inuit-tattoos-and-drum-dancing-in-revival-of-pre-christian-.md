Sanimuinaq, who bears traditional Inuit facial tattoos, is part of a movement reshaping Greenlandic identity. Across the Arctic island, Greenlanders are reclaiming pre-Christian practices — drum dancing, shamanic healing and ancestral tattoo traditions — that European missionaries suppressed over three centuries of colonial rule.

About 90 percent of Greenland's approximately 57,000 residents identify as Inuit, and the vast majority belong to the Lutheran Church, which a Danish missionary introduced to the island more than 300 years ago. The Inuit, whose traditional religion is animist, believe that animals, birds, stones and even rain and snow possess spirits deserving of respect.

The revival is gaining force as Greenland pursues greater self-determination from Denmark and faces heightened international attention — including repeated statements by U.S. President Donald Trump that Washington should acquire the territory. For some Greenlanders, the political spotlight has created space to address colonial-era abuses and speak more openly about the traditions that survived them.

"We don't have to walk silenced anymore," Sanimuinaq told the Associated Press. "That's the change we see — that the voice we get out in the world has been forbidden even within our country. Now that we're opening, we have more freedom."

## A tradition suppressed, now returning

Asta Mønsted, a professor at the University of California, Berkeley, who researches Inuit oral history and its connections to Greenland's archaeology, said missionaries viewed traditional Inuit practices as incompatible with Christianity.

"Drum songs and drum duels were central to Inuit spiritual and social life, but the missionaries viewed them as pagan practices and superstitions that needed to be replaced with Christian hymns and prayers," Mønsted said. "Drums were confiscated or destroyed in order to break the connection to the pre-Christian beliefs."

In some parts of Greenland, she noted, knowledge of drum songs and drum-making survived outside the church's reach.

Missionaries were equally hostile to traditional tattoos. "Tattoos were also linked to Inuit cosmology and rites, but missionaries labeled them as pagan and especially viewed the facial tattoos as a defilement of God's creation," Mønsted said. "They promoted the European ideal, where the human body should remain unmarked."

## The meaning of Tunniit

The traditional Inuit tattoos, known as Tunniit, were applied by poking soot from soapstone lamps onto the skin with a needle or by dragging a soot-covered sinew thread beneath the skin. Women traditionally received tattoos at menstruation and childbirth, viewing them as protection against illness and malevolent spirits, Mønsted said.

Resistance deterred many Greenlanders across generations. Therecie Sanimuinaq Pedersen recalled how her grandmother covered her facial tattoos in soot rather than face social alienation from her community.

Therecie, the mother of shaman Aviaja, got her own facial tattoos only after her daughter did in recent years — reclaiming the same patterns her grandmother had worn.

"The tattoos I have goes from mother to daughter for thousands of years," Therecie said in Greenlandic, as translated by her daughter. "I have the same as my grandmother — that's my heritage."

These days, when she encounters other Greenlanders displaying Inuit tattoos on the streets of Nuuk, she said the recognition is immediate. "When I see them, it's like we have a connection," she said. "Without knowing them, and them knowing me, we say hi. Some come, give a hug, and say thank you."

## Drums, dispute and the colonial record

The traditional Inuit drum, or qilaat, served multiple functions before colonization — entertainment and socializing, a tool for shamans during ceremonial seances, and a form of community jurisprudence. In drum duels, opponents argued disputes before gathered community members through song, insult and exaggerated movement. Collective laughter often determined the winner without any formal ruling, Mønsted said, though a losing party could face exile into the Arctic wilderness — a consequence she described as potentially tantamount to a death sentence.

Greenland remained a Danish colony until 1953, when it became a province, and received home rule in 1979. Denmark retains control over the island's foreign and defense affairs.

Denmark has been accused of committing abuses against Greenland's Inuit people, including removing children from their families in the 1950s in the name of social integration and fitting women with intrauterine contraceptive devices in the 1960s and 1970s, allegedly to limit population growth.

## Music, faith and reclamation

Naja Parnuuna, an award-winning singer-songwriter, uses music to encourage Greenlanders to embrace their Inuit heritage. Growing up, she said, it felt "cooler to be a Dane" and she was "ashamed to be Greenlandic and follow Inuit traditions."

"I feel like we've been looked down for so long, and we really haven't had a voice for a long time," Parnuuna told the AP.

Her father, Markus Olsen, is a former Lutheran pastor who was dismissed from his church position in 2022 after he allowed drum dancing during a National Day service at the Nuuk Cathedral. He said he believes the qilaat needs to be restored to a valued place in religious services. Olsen, who wears a collar bearing both a small qilaat and a crucifix, has cited the Latin American Liberation Theology movement and figures including the Rev. Martin Luther King Jr. as inspirations.

For Aviaja Sanimuinaq, the reclamation requires no conflict between traditions.

"The sacredness of Christianity is still sacred in my eyes," she said. "But so is Buddhism, so is Hinduism, and so is my work. That's where I stand — that the arising of our culture, and us as a people, is also to get the equality within our culture, to acknowledge that our culture is legit; that it has to have a space here."
