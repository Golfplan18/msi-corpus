Trump's repeated threats against a NATO ally's territory have exposed a structural gap in the alliance's founding treaty — Article 5's collective-defense pledge was written to protect members from outside aggressors, not from one another — leaving the alliance without a clear mechanism to respond if the United States moved against Denmark.

BRUSSELS — President Donald Trump on Sunday reaffirmed his intention to seize Greenland, a semi-autonomous island that is part of the Danish realm, as the White House declined to rule out military force and Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen warned that a U.S. attack on a NATO ally would end the alliance.

"One way or the other, we're going to have Greenland," Trump said. He argued that control of the Arctic island was necessary to prevent Russia or China from taking it over, and said that making a deal would be "easier."

Frederiksen drew a hard line in response. "If the United States chooses to attack another NATO country militarily, then everything stops … including our NATO," she said.

Senior U.S. and Danish officials were scheduled to meet Wednesday, according to the Associated Press.

## A treaty built for outside threats

The episode has surfaced a structural gap in NATO's founding charter. The alliance's collective-defense guarantee, enshrined in Article 5 of the 1949 Washington Treaty, pledges members to treat an attack on any one of them as an attack on all. The provision has been invoked once in the alliance's history — in 2001, when NATO activated it to support the United States in the aftermath of the Sept. 11 attacks.

But Article 5 was designed to protect members from external adversaries, not from one another. In any conflict between the United States and Denmark, there would be no unanimity among the alliance's 32 members to invoke it, rendering the clause effectively inoperable. Beyond diplomacy, NATO has no established mechanism for managing open conflict among its own membership.

A U.S. move against Greenland would set a dangerous precedent. Tensions have periodically flared between NATO members — most persistently between neighbors Greece and Turkey — but annexation of one ally's territory by another would challenge the foundational premise of the alliance. A U.S. attack on Denmark is almost certain to divide NATO, according to the AP's reporting; the 2003 Iraq war, which the United States led with British and Spanish support while France and Germany led opposition, offers the closest historical parallel.

Denmark retains one formal recourse short of armed conflict: Article 4 of the Washington Treaty allows any member to call for official consultations when it believes its sovereignty or territorial integrity is threatened. Article 4 talks carry no obligation to produce action.

## Alliance leadership deflects

NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, a former Dutch prime minister who chairs the North Atlantic Council and speaks for all 32 member states, sidestepped direct questions about Greenland at a briefing Monday.

"All allies agree on the importance of the Arctic and Arctic security because we know that with sea lanes opening up there is a risk that the Russians and the Chinese will be more active," Rutte told reporters.

Asked whether NATO was in crisis over Greenland, Rutte replied: "No, not at all."

Ensuring continued U.S. commitment to the alliance is described by the AP as one of Rutte's principal tasks. The alliance's military headquarters, located in Mons, Belgium, is commanded by Air Force Lt. Gen. Alexus Grynkewich, who serves as Supreme Allied Commander Europe. Trump is Grynkewich's commander in chief.

## Alliance stakes

Trump has previously fueled uncertainty about U.S. support for Article 5 by expressing doubt about whether he would defend allies he regards as insufficiently committed to defense spending. His Greenland claims add a different dimension: not whether the United States would defend allies, but whether it might move against one.

NATO was formed by 12 nations in 1949 to counter the Soviet Union. Its ranks have since grown to 32 members, most recently when Sweden joined in 2024 amid concern over Russia's increasing military aggression. The alliance officially identifies Russia and international terrorism as its foremost threats.

The United States remains NATO's most powerful member by a substantial margin, spending far more on defense than any other ally. NATO's deterrence posture is built on a strong American troop presence in Europe and U.S. nuclear weapons stationed there — assets the alliance cannot readily replicate if Washington's commitment wavers.
