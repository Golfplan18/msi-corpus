The emergency marked Chile's most serious crisis in recent years, following massive wildfires two years earlier that killed more than 130 people. The mobile clinic represented an extraordinary effort to provide first aid to pets caught in the flames, with veterinarians and volunteers working to treat burned animals and reunite lost pets with their owners.

In January, as devastating wildfires consumed most of the south-central Chilean town of Lirquén, veterinarians from Chile's investigative police set up a mobile clinic to treat animals rescued from the flames. The fires left at least 20 dead and nearly 300 injured nationwide, with the Trinitarios fire—the most destructive of 30 wildfires burning across the country—consuming more than 140 square kilometers in the Bío Bío region.

## Inside the Mobile Clinic

Inside a small van parked in the fire-ravaged town of Lirquén, veterinarians treated burned paws, dehydrated bodies, and eyes scorched by toxic smoke. Angiella Scalpello, a veterinarian from the canine squad of Chile's investigative police, described the clinic's mission: "Our main goal is to help animals that have been rescued, found in the rubble, in the ash, or that managed to escape with their owners but suffered injuries due to this massive fire."

The mobile clinic became a critical staging ground during January's catastrophic wildfires. Almost every animal that arrived bore the signature wounds of the flames: burned whiskers and paws, severe dehydration, or conjunctivitis caused by days spent in ash and toxic fumes. Many had sheltered in rubble or beneath debris while waiting to be found.

## The Scale of Disaster

The Trinitarios fire, the most destructive of the 30 wildfires burning across Chile at that time, consumed more than 140 square kilometers in the Bío Bío region. The town of Lirquén, with approximately 20,000 residents, experienced the worst of it—roughly 80% of the city burned. Nationwide, the fires left at least 20 dead and nearly 300 injured. This disaster followed Chile's deadliest wildfires two years earlier, which had killed more than 130 people.

## The Rescue Work Continues

Juan Vivanco, another veterinarian in the mobile unit, noted the realities of the rescue effort. "We usually find cats that were sheltering in small corners and that survived both the fire and the days that followed," he said. "We've also found several pets already deceased—it happens regularly."

Inside the clinic, animals received IV fluids, vital signs checks, and wound care. The most severe cases were transported to veterinary hospitals. Volunteer Vanessa Morales described the emotional weight of rescuing the burned animals. "We've transported four or five puppies, in addition to three kittens. The last kitten we brought in yesterday really touched us, because it had all four paws and its tail burned," she said.

## Residents Seeking Help and Reunion

Kevin Carrasco brought his senior poodle, Chica, to the clinic after evacuating their destroyed home. "I noticed her eyes were a little sore, with some discharge, so I looked for help, and luckily there was a veterinarian here who gave me some drops for her and some gauze to clean them, because she had conjunctivitis," he said.

Not all who came to the clinic were pet owners seeking treatment. Yasna Hidalgo arrived searching for her 85-year-old grandmother's two dogs, separated during the evacuation. "She had to leave in a hurry, and the house was completely destroyed," Hidalgo said.

For 21-year-old student María Paz, the losses extended far beyond missing pets. "Everything burned, we lost everything. All 200 houses in our town burned down," she recalled. "People died, families died, neighbors died, and many animals died, including my little kitten."

As rescue operations continued, the mobile clinic remained a source of hope for residents desperate to save their animal companions and for those searching for beloved pets lost in the chaos of evacuation.
