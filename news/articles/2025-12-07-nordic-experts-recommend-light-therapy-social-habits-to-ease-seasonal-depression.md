Health researchers in Norway, Sweden, and Finland say light therapy devices, dawn simulators, and sustained social contact can meaningfully reduce seasonal affective disorder, which is estimated to affect millions of people worldwide each year. Experts from three Nordic countries — speaking to the Associated Press ahead of the Dec. 21 winter solstice — outlined evidence-based strategies for countering the depression, fatigue, and weight gain that accompany the region's months-long winter darkness.

Seasonal affective disorder, characterized by recurring episodes of depression beginning in fall and easing in spring, is not inevitable, the researchers said — and a growing body of research points to practical, low-cost interventions that can help.

As MSI previously reported, [Nordic experts have outlined light therapy and social habits](/articles/2025-12-07-nordic-experts-advise-light-therapy-social-habits-to-counter-winter-blues) as the frontline tools for countering winter blues.

## Light therapy: the primary clinical tool

Christian Benedict, a pharmacology professor at Uppsala University in Sweden, said a morning routine with a dedicated light therapy device can benefit both people diagnosed with SAD and those experiencing milder winter symptoms.

"It's not like it's a fate, an annual or a seasonal fate, and you cannot do anything about it," Benedict said. "There are possibilities to affect it."

Research supports using a device rated at approximately 10,000 lux — a measure of brightness — for 30 minutes each morning. Such devices emit roughly 20 times the brightness of standard indoor lighting and run from $70 to $400, though some products marketed for SAD are not bright enough to be effective. Insurance may cover part of the cost for patients with a formal diagnosis.

Benedict said the therapy helps reset the circadian rhythm and raises serotonin levels in the brain.

## Sleep disruption and winter weight gain

Dr. Timo Partonen, a research professor at the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare, said limited winter daylight prevents the body's internal clock from synchronizing properly, disrupting sleep. People may sleep longer in winter but still wake unrefreshed, he said.

Partonen recommended pairing a dawn simulator — a gradually brightening alarm clock — with a dedicated light therapy device, using both before noon each day.

Disrupted circadian rhythms also drive wintertime weight gain. Partonen said people typically gain 2 to 5 kilograms (4 to 11 pounds) during winter, fueled by evening cravings for carbohydrates. Maintaining an exercise routine can help offset both mood and metabolic effects — and inviting a friend along can reinforce both goals, he said. Partonen noted that symptoms rarely improve in isolation, making social contact a clinical as well as cultural priority.

## Social habits and the science of blue light

Ida Solhaug, an associate professor in psychology at the University of Tromsø — also known as the Arctic University of Norway, the world's northernmost university — said embracing winter rather than dreading it is a core survival strategy built into Nordic culture.

"It's part of the culture," Solhaug said.

She recommended indoor practices like hygge, the Danish emphasis on warmth and coziness, alongside outdoor activities such as fika, the traditional Swedish coffee break taken outside, even on cloudy days. Solhaug said she takes a cold plunge into the waters off Tromsø, an island 350 kilometers (217 miles) north of the Arctic Circle, at least once a week. "Challenge yourself to look for light in the darkness," she said.

The blue light science supports her outdoor prescription. Researcher Kathryn Roecklein at the University of Pittsburgh found that people with SAD are less sensitive to blue light — the wavelength sunlight is richest in — than people without the disorder, particularly during winter. Specialized cells in the eye convert that wavelength into neural signals affecting mood and alertness; reduced sensitivity may be a contributing cause of seasonal depression, according to her research.

In severe cases, the experts said, clinical support and antidepressant medications may be necessary.

## Finland's president weighs in

Finland's President Alexander Stubb offered his own prescription in a recent AP interview. "Take an ice bath and then followed up by a sauna and do one more ice bath, one more sauna, then a shower and go out there. You'll manage," Stubb said.
