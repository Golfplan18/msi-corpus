## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- The Supreme Court has sent a Native American voting rights case back to a lower court for reconsideration after a recent ruling that weakened the Voting Rights Act.
- The justices instructed lower courts to re-examine a prior decision that ruled against the tribes, which had undercut lawsuits from voters and advocacy groups.
- The 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals had previously ruled that only the federal government could sue to enforce the Voting Rights Act, conflicting with decades of case law.
- Lenny Powell, an attorney for the Native American Rights Fund, called the Supreme Court's action "the right call" and vowed to continue fighting for Native voters' ability to effect change.

The Supreme Court acted in a Voting Rights Act case brought by Native American tribes on Monday, ordering lower courts to reconsider a ruling that weakened the Civil Rights-era law.

The justices directed lower courts to take another look at the lower-court decision that went against the tribes and undercut a key enforcement mechanism: lawsuits from voters and advocacy groups.

Those groups have been key to enforcing the law, bringing most of the lawsuits filed under the provision of the Voting Rights Act known as Section 2.

However, in a North Dakota case brought by two Native American tribes, the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that only the federal government can sue to enforce the law.

The Supreme Court blocked that decision in July, allowing the tribes’ preferred maps to temporarily stay in place.

Lenny Powell, an attorney for the Native American Rights Fund, said tossing out the appeals court ruling was the right call. He vowed to “keep fighting to ensure that Native voters have the ability to vote and effect change in their communities.”

The appeals court’s finding has nevertheless been cited elsewhere, with Mississippi making a similar argument in another appeal over its state legislative map. The court also sent that case back for reconsideration on Monday.

That decision jeopardizes three new majority-Black state legislative districts, though the effects likely won’t be felt until 2027, said Damon Hewitt, president and executive director of the Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law.

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented from both decisions, saying she would have left the Mississippi ruling in place and fully reversed the decision in the North Dakota case.

The conservative majority, meanwhile, has already diluted enforcement power with their April decision that struck down a majority Black congressional district in Louisiana and made future cases much harder to win.

In that case, the high court’s conservative majority ruled that map relied too heavily on race with a district aimed at giving Black voters a chance to elect a candidate of their choice. The decision effectively limited Voting Rights claims to maps that are intentionally designed to discriminate, a very high standard.
