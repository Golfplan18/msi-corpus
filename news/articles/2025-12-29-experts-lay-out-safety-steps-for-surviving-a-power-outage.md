Power outages caused by winter storms, heat waves and wildfires can turn dangerous—and sometimes fatal—depending on how long they last and the temperature outside, safety experts said. The American Red Cross and the American Public Power Association offered guidance Sunday on what households need to be ready before the lights go out.

The advice covers three phases: building an emergency kit before an outage strikes, managing food safety and temperature during one, and planning longer-term backup power solutions between events.

"When thinking about preparedness, I always think about the old proverb of, 'An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure,'" said Michael Coe, vice president for physical and cybersecurity programs with the American Public Power Association.

## Before the outage

Preparation depends on household circumstances, said Denise Everhart, disaster executive with the American Red Cross. Families with children, pets or electrically powered medical equipment face different challenges than those without.

Customers who rely on medical devices should notify their utility provider, Coe said. Many utilities post online disclosure forms, and backup battery arrangements may be available.

Everhart said a well-stocked emergency kit should include:

- Two weeks of food, water, pet supplies and medicine
- A week's worth of cash, in case ATMs and credit card machines go down
- Flashlights, glow sticks or battery lanterns
- Backup phone batteries and charger cords
- Blankets in cold weather, or extra water in hot weather
- Written phone numbers for family and friends in case the phone dies
- A solar-powered or hand-crank radio for emergency alerts if cell towers stop working

Food choices should match what the family will actually eat, Everhart said.

"Is your 6-year-old really going to eat a cold can of chili?" she said.

Matthew Gonzales, executive director of the Consumer Energy Alliance Southwest Region, said filling a bathtub or bucket with water before a storm allows toilet flushing to continue. Households on well water or electric pump systems typically have only the water remaining in their tank once power fails.

Signing up for emergency alerts from utilities, local police and other emergency services gives residents early notice of boil-water advisories or evacuation orders, Coe said.

## During an outage

Keeping the refrigerator door closed preserves food temperature. Coe said a refrigerator can maintain safe temperatures for up to four hours, and a freezer for up to 48 hours, as long as the door stays shut. Perishable food should be eaten first. Anything that smells, looks or tastes unusual should be discarded.

Space heaters and candles should never be left unattended because they can cause house fires, and generators should never be operated indoors because they produce carbon monoxide, Everhart said. If conditions become too hot or too cold to remain safely at home, leaving is the right move, she said.

Before evacuating, residents should check on neighbors if possible, Gonzales said.

## Planning for next time

The period between outages is the best time to prepare for the next one. Households should consider saving for a portable generator in the 5,000- to 10,000-watt range, which can power a microwave, refrigerator or space heater and typically costs $500 to $1,000. Solar battery storage and reverse electric vehicle charging offer additional options for keeping power on when the broader grid goes down.

Other longer-term steps include learning how to open the garage door manually, keeping key documents in a single accessible place, mapping a backup evacuation route and establishing a way to reach family members if internet or cell service fails.

"Have a kit, have a plan, have a way to get notified, and a way to notify people. That's really preparedness in a nutshell," Everhart said.
