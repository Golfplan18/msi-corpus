Many habits Americans already resolve to adopt each January — eating better, spending less, clearing clutter — also reduce energy use, food waste, and carbon emissions, advocates and energy experts told the Associated Press.

The overlap between personal-finance goals and environmental impact is broader than most households realize, and nearly all of the changes require little or no upfront spending.

From thermostat adjustments to no-buy clothing challenges, low-cost behavioral shifts offer households a simultaneous path toward budget relief and a smaller environmental footprint heading into 2026.

## Cutting home energy use

Unplugging electronics when not in use and adjusting the thermostat while away from home can cut both utility bills and household energy consumption, according to Matthew Gonzales, vice chairman of the National Hispanic Energy Council, which advocates for affordable energy for communities of color.

The U.S. Department of Energy has said that adjusting a home thermostat 7 to 10 degrees Fahrenheit — about 4 to 6 degrees Celsius — for eight hours a day can save up to 10% annually on heating and cooling costs.

Washing laundry on cold and air-drying when possible can also reduce energy use and utility costs, Gonzales said. Heating water is the biggest energy draw in laundry, and clothes dryers rank among the most energy-intensive household appliances, he added. Other low-cost measures include switching to LED bulbs, sealing drafty windows, and replacing dirty air filters so heating and cooling systems run more efficiently.

"Don't let perfect be the enemy of good enough," Gonzales said.

Savings vary depending on location, home age, and local energy prices, but Gonzales said households in high-cost states can see meaningful reductions from small changes over time.

## Cutting food waste

Food waste costs U.S. consumers $728 per year, according to the Environmental Protection Agency, and generates annual greenhouse gas emissions equivalent to those of 42 coal-fired power plants.

Cookbook author Anne-Marie Bonneau recommended starting with what is already in the kitchen before shopping.

"If you have fat and an onion and a couple of random vegetables — or even one random vegetable or some leftover cooked proteins or grains — you've got soup in the making," Bonneau said.

She also suggested preserving food before it spoils — including by fermenting vegetables, which she said requires only cabbage, salt, and a glass jar for a basic sauerkraut — and freezing seasonal sauces for later use.

Cutting back on ultra-processed foods such as sugary cereals and prepackaged meals reduces plastic packaging contact along with environmental impact, Bonneau said.

"You'll improve your diet, you'll reduce your waste and your food will come in less contact with plastic," she said.

## Decluttering and buying less

Katrina Caspelich, communications director for Remake, an advocacy group for human rights and climate justice in fashion, said spending even an hour organizing a living space can reduce impulse spending by helping people clarify what they actually use.

Americans generated about 17 million tons of textile waste in 2018, most of it clothing, according to the EPA.

Remake estimates that pausing new clothing purchases for 90 days can save roughly 3,900 liters — about 1,030 gallons — of water, avoid about 300 kilograms of carbon dioxide emissions, keep around 9 kilograms of clothing out of landfills, and save an average of nearly $300.

Unsubscribing from retailer email lists and sale alerts can help prevent those purchases from occurring, Caspelich said.

"Most impulse purchases start with a notification, not a need," she said. Waiting 24 hours before buying something new often converts a perceived must-have into a pass, she added.

For those put off by the strictness of a complete spending pause, Caspelich recommended reframing the effort as a flexible experiment.

"The key is flexibility," she said. "Framing it as an experiment rather than a strict rule makes it feel empowering instead of restrictive."
