Israeli government officials on Monday inaugurated Yatziv, a new settlement in the occupied West Bank near the Palestinian town of Beit Sahour, marking the culmination of a two-decade effort to convert a sparse hilltop outpost into an officially recognized settlement. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, who leads Israel's settlement policy, told the Associated Press at the ceremony that settlers would "be here forever" and would "never establish a Palestinian state here." The rapid transformation—from a few mobile homes to a fully recognized settlement in one month—reflects the current political landscape, with Smotrich and other settler-aligned ministers holding key positions in the Israeli government and maintaining close ties with the Trump administration.

The settlement represents another step in the Israeli settler movement's expansion across the West Bank, which Palestinians say makes it increasingly difficult to establish a viable independent state. Settler violence rose by 27 percent in 2025, according to Israel's military, as Palestinians and international observers describe the settlements as obstacles to peace.

## Settlement in a month

The transformation from sparse hilltop outpost to officially recognized settlement took just 30 days. Israeli government officials celebrated Monday as settlers inaugurated Yatziv, the latest addition to what Palestinians describe as a tightening enclosure of their territory.

The acceleration reflects the political moment. Settler-aligned ministers hold key government positions, and the Trump administration has signaled openness to Israeli settlement policy. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, who leads Israel's settlement expansions, framed the achievement in those terms. "We are standing stable here in Israel," he told the Associated Press at the ceremony. "We're going to be here forever. We will never establish a Palestinian state here."

## Twenty years of persistence

Settlers had pursued this particular hilltop near the Palestinian town of Beit Sahour for two decades before the recent acceleration. The site took its latest name, Yatziv — meaning "stable" in Hebrew — only after Smotrich announced approval of this settlement along with 18 others on December 21.

That approval capped years of maneuvering. Yaron Rosenthal, chair of the settlement council, attributed the sudden pace to convergent political factors. "We understood that there was an opportunity," he told the AP. "But we didn't know it would happen so quickly. Now there is the right political constellation for this to happen."

The transformation also followed a November stabbing attack in which Palestinian attackers killed an Israeli at a nearby junction. Rosenthal said this incident created justification for moving the settlement forward.

## The hospital that was never built

The land's history illustrates the path from contested site to settlement. In 2006, the Israeli government was in talks with the United States about building a Palestinian children's hospital on the land, funded by the U.S. Agency for International Development. Settlers opposed the project and organized demonstrations urging Israel to block it. The hospital was never built.

Instead, the Netanyahu government converted the site into a military base in 2009. Settlers soon established a cultural center there with lectures and exhibits.

Ehud Olmert, the Israeli prime minister at the time the hospital was under discussion, told the AP that the military-base designation was the pivotal moment. "Once it is military installation, it is easier than to change its status into a new outpost, a new settlement and so on," he said. Olmert noted that Netanyahu, who has served as prime minister nearly uninterrupted since then, was "committed to entirely different political directions from the ones that I had. They didn't think about cooperation with the Palestinians."

## Palestinian view of the land

Elias Isseid, mayor of Beit Sahour, offers a different account of the land's ownership. "These lands have been owned by families from Beit Sahour since ancient times," he said. The new settlement, he said, "poses a great danger to our children, our families."

Yatziv is the latest in a series of settlements ringing Beit Sahour, all connected by a highway that runs to Jerusalem without entering Palestinian villages. Palestinians say this pattern of settlement expansion leaves less viable land for a future Palestinian state.

Nadia Matar, a settler activist, described the long commitment required to reach this moment. "Shdema was nearly lost to us," she said, using the earlier name for the site. "What prevented that outcome was perseverance."

## Rising violence

Israel's military documented a 27 percent increase in settler violence in 2025, even as the settlement movement celebrates expanding territorial control. Hagit Ofran, a director at Peace Now, an anti-settlement watchdog group, said the site's conversion from proposed hospital to military base to settlement exemplifies how legal structures facilitate territorial consolidation.

Most of the world considers Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank illegal under international law, though Israel disputes this characterization. Palestinians seek the West Bank, captured by Israel in 1967, as part of a future state.
