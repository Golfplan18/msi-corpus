Consumer interest in sustainable toilet paper has grown sharply since 2019, but environmental benefits vary significantly across products and claims on packaging can be hard to evaluate, experts told the Associated Press. Recycled-content products carry the strongest sustainability credentials, researchers say, while bamboo alternatives present a more complicated picture and bidets offer a path to near-zero paper use.

Independent certification labels, an annual product-grading report from the Natural Resources Defense Council, and peer-reviewed research point to recycled-content toilet paper as the most reliable choice for environmentally conscious consumers, while a recent study finds bamboo toilet paper manufactured in China can carry a higher environmental footprint than U.S.-made products using imported forest fibers.

Toilet paper is used for a few seconds before being flushed and forgotten, but its production typically relies on trees, energy-intensive manufacturing, and chemicals that can harm the environment. A growing market of sustainable alternatives offers consumers real options for reducing that footprint — provided they can sort credible claims from marketing.

More than 1 billion gallons of water and 1.6 million trees could be saved annually if every American replaced one conventional roll with a roll made from recycled content, according to the Environmental Paper Network, a coalition of nonprofits.

"Paper is one of the easiest materials on the planet to recycle," said Gary Bull, professor emeritus of forest economics at the University of British Columbia. Toilet paper made from postconsumer recycled fibers avoids freshly cut trees and increasingly uses chlorine-free bleaching techniques that reduce pollution from manufacturing.

## Reading the labels

The most rigorous method for evaluating a product's environmental footprint is a life cycle assessment, which measures impacts from a seedling through manufacturing and disposal, Bull said. Because that analysis is not practical for most shoppers, third-party certification labels and independent grading reports provide accessible alternatives.

Labels from the Forest Stewardship Council or the Sustainable Forestry Initiative indicate a company is making scientifically proven efforts to be sustainable, Bull said. Both groups' standards include conserving water, wildlife, and biodiversity, along with compliance with forestry laws.

The Natural Resources Defense Council publishes an annual grading report, "The Issue with Tissue," rating products from A+ to F. The 2025 report gave its highest letter grade to five products: Aria, Green Forest, Natural Value, Trader Joe's, and Whole Foods 365 100% Recycled, all made entirely from recycled materials.

Ashley Jordan, an NRDC corporate campaign advocate, said she has observed dozens of sustainable toilet paper brands emerge since the report launched in 2019.

## Bamboo's limits

Bamboo is often marketed as more sustainable than wood-pulp toilet paper because it grows faster than trees. But researchers recommend consumers focus on recycled-content products rather than bamboo alternatives.

Ronalds Gonzalez, an associate professor at North Carolina State University who studies fibers used in the hygiene industry, said pollution from bamboo manufacturing can undercut its environmental advantages. Gonzalez recently co-authored a study finding that bamboo toilet paper manufactured in China and sold in the U.S. had a higher environmental impact than toilet paper made in the U.S. with imported forest fibers, largely because Chinese manufacturers rely on coal-generated electricity.

The study found the bamboo toilet paper's environmental impact could be reduced when produced in regions that use renewable energy, Gonzalez said.

## Industry response

Mark Pitts, executive director of tissue at the American Forest & Paper Association — whose members include large manufacturers Kimberly-Clark Corporation and Georgia-Pacific — said sustainability is a core industry focus. He told the Associated Press that members have reported reductions in greenhouse gas emissions along their supply chains and have increased use of recycled material while following responsible forestry practices.

## Bidets as an alternative

Bidets allow people to rinse after using the bathroom, reducing or eliminating toilet paper use. Common in Europe, they can be separate fixtures or devices attached to existing toilets.

Kory Russel, assistant professor of landscape architecture and environmental studies at the University of Oregon, said bidets are sustainable because "you're not using any sort of logging, it's water that's already coming to your household and it's very little water."

Basic attachable bidets cost around $30, while toilet seats with heated water and air-drying features can exceed $600. Some models require professional installation.

## Price outlook

Sustainable toilet paper typically costs more per square foot than conventional products. But Russel said prices are likely to drop as consumer demand grows and manufacturers expand production.

"If more people are buying sustainable toilet paper and demanding it, there should be economies of scale and prices should fall to match that of conventional toilet paper," he said.

Kory Russel also noted that consumer purchasing decisions send a signal to corporations. "When people purchase a sustainable product it sends a message to corporations to make more eco-friendly products available," he said.
