## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- New Mexico prosecutors asked a judge in a second phase of a bench trial to order Meta to change apps and algorithms to protect children.
- A first-phase jury verdict found Meta liable for harming children’s mental health and concealing what it knew about child sexual exploitation.
- Prosecutors are seeking safeguards aimed at reducing compulsive features, improving age verification, and preventing child sexual exploitation through default privacy settings and oversight.
- Meta says the remedies sought would violate free-speech rights and could force the company to eliminate service in New Mexico.
- Trial testimony also included statements from the judge about concerns that a court order could overreach the court’s role.

New Mexico state prosecutors opened the second phase of a landmark bench trial Monday, seeking court-ordered changes to Meta Platforms’ social media apps and the algorithms behind them to safeguard children. The case, in Santa Fe, examines whether platforms that include Instagram pose a public nuisance, and it follows the end of a first phase that resulted in civil penalties against Meta.

The trial’s first phase ended with a jury awarding $375 million in civil penalties and finding that Meta knowingly harmed children’s mental health and concealed what it knew about child sexual exploitation on its platforms. In the trial’s reconvened second phase, state attorneys urged the judge to impose “fundamental changes” designed to address both alleged addiction and child-safety failures.

New Mexico’s prosecution team said it wants a redesign of recommendation systems that, prosecutors argued, prioritize constant engagement. Prosecutors also pointed to features tied to compulsive use, including “infinite scroll” and push notifications, and they asked for changes that would affect default product settings such as how “likes” and sharing counts appear to users.

The prosecution also called for steps intended to improve age verification, as well as default privacy changes and closer oversight aimed at preventing child sexual exploitation. In addition, New Mexico sought requirements that child accounts have an associated parent or guardian and that a court-supervised child safety monitor track safety improvements over time.

David Ackerman, the New Mexico prosecution attorney, outlined a proposed $3.7 billion abatement plan in opening statements, which he described as recognizing the scope of the public nuisance. Ackerman said the plan includes items for public education, support for schools, help for law enforcement, and support for mental health providers, adding that children across New Mexico were “begging for help,” as the state pressed its case for sweeping remedies.

The judge presiding over the bench trial, Bryan Biedscheid, told the court Monday he was concerned about the scope of any possible court order. He said he was “probably not the easiest sell on an idea where I would become a one-person legislature, judge and executive branch enforcer,” highlighting worries about the authority the court would be asked to exercise in selecting and enforcing changes.

Meta has disputed the nuisance theory and argued that state remedies would be impractical and constitutionally problematic. In opening statements, Meta attorney Alex Parkinson argued there is no public right to social media under nuisance laws, asking rhetorically, “Are bars a public nuisance because drinking alcohol is undeniably associated with car fatalities?” Parkinson said users harmed by specific actions should be able to pursue personal injury cases in other lawsuits.

Parkinson also argued that the state’s proposed mandates could interfere with parental rights and stifle free expression, and he disputed the feasibility of tailoring changes only for New Mexico users. He told the judge that geo-fencing the company’s services “into that version of the apps” was “not feasible, technologically,” according to his remarks in the second-phase proceedings.

The case is the first to reach trial among lawsuits filed by more than 40 state attorneys general alleging that Meta contributes to a youth mental health crisis, with most other cases pursuing remedies in U.S. federal court. New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez said Monday that the jury verdict punctured what he described as an aura of invincibility surrounding liability for content posted on platforms under Section 230, while also calling for public education efforts and new public service warnings inside Meta apps.

Outside the courtroom, the trial’s arguments also drew commentary from Eric Goldman, co-director of the High Tech Law Institute at Santa Clara University School of Law. Goldman said having a trial on the nuisance theory itself was “a remarkable outcome,” and he said the theory is “not well accepted as applied to the internet,” while also warning that a court order requiring age authentication would face legal uncertainty.

The proceedings could have influence beyond New Mexico because the state’s requested remedies target algorithmic recommendation design, age verification, default privacy settings, and other safeguards that prosecutors argue are central to the harms alleged in the case. Meta has said it plans to appeal the first-phase jury verdict and has warned it could eliminate service in New Mexico if forced to comply with the state’s requested changes.
