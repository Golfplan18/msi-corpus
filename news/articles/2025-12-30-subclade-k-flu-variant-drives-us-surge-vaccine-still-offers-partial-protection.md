The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported high or very high flu activity in more than half of U.S. states as of Dec. 30, driven by subclade K — a mutated version of the H3N2 influenza strain that produced early outbreaks in the United Kingdom, Japan and Canada. The agency estimated at least 7.5 million illnesses, 81,000 hospitalizations and 3,100 deaths from flu so far this season, based on data through Dec. 20, before major holiday gatherings. At least eight children have died.

Health officials say vaccination remains worthwhile despite the variant's partial immune escape: a preliminary analysis from the United Kingdom found the current flu shot still reduces the risk of hospitalization from subclade K, even though it is not a perfect match for the strain.

MSI reported earlier Tuesday on [subclade K's rapid spread through the United States](/articles/2025-12-30-flu-subclade-k-drives-rapid-rise-in-us-cases-cdc-reports); the following explains what is known about the variant, the vaccine's effectiveness and what people at high risk should do now.

## What is subclade K?

The flu virus constantly mutates and comes in multiple forms. Subclade K is a mutated version of the H3N2 subtype — one of two Type A influenza strains. Flu seasons dominated by H3N2 tend to be harsh, with more infections overall and higher rates of serious illness, especially among adults 65 and older.

Subclade K's mutations are not large enough to constitute an entirely new influenza type, said Andrew Pekosz, a virus expert at Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health. But they are significant enough to evade some of the protection offered by this year's vaccine, he said.

The variant drove early outbreaks in the United Kingdom, Japan and Canada before spreading in the United States, where flu typically begins its winter rise in December.

## How severe is this season so far?

The toll through Dec. 20 — before major holiday gatherings — included at least 7.5 million illnesses, 81,000 hospitalizations and 3,100 deaths, according to the CDC. The data predates travel that typically accelerates transmission.

Some states have been particularly hard hit. New York's health department said the week ending Dec. 20 marked the most flu cases the state recorded in a single week since 2004: 71,000.

The CDC said it is too early to know whether this season will match the severity of last winter. Pekosz said it will take additional time to determine whether subclade K spreads more easily than prior strains, is more dangerous, or both — questions with different implications for how the season unfolds.

## Does the vaccine still work?

A preliminary analysis from the United Kingdom found the current flu shot offers at least partial protection against subclade K, lowering the risk of hospitalization, the CDC said. The vaccine also appears to be a good match against H1N1 and Type B flu strains that may circulate alongside subclade K this season, Pekosz said.

About 42% of adults and children had received a flu vaccination as of the reporting date, according to the CDC — leaving the majority of the population without even partial protection heading into the peak of the season.

## Who should get vaccinated — and when?

The CDC and major medical societies recommend flu vaccination for nearly everyone age 6 months and older. Those recommendations have not changed despite recent misinformation about vaccines, the CDC said.

Flu poses particular danger to adults 65 and older, pregnant women, young children and people of any age with chronic health conditions including asthma, diabetes, heart disease and weakened immune systems.

It is not too late to be vaccinated, health experts said. Shots are available for all ages. The nasal spray FluMist is available for people ages 2 to 49, and for the first time this season some people may be eligible to self-administer it at home.

## What if someone gets sick?

The CDC notes that prescription antiviral medicines can treat flu and are generally recommended for people at high risk of complications. Those medicines typically need to be started within one to two days of symptoms appearing to be most effective.
