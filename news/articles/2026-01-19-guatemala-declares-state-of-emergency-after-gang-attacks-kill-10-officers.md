Suspected gang members killed 10 Guatemalan police officers in a wave of coordinated attacks across the capital Sunday and Monday, prompting President Bernardo Arévalo to declare a 30-day state of emergency that Guatemala's Congress approved 149-to-1. The violence followed simultaneous riots Saturday in which inmates seized control of three prisons and took 43 guards hostage, demanding privileges for gang members and their leaders.

The attacks mark an unprecedented escalation by Central American street gangs against Guatemala's state security forces, drawing a near-unanimous emergency response from the government and condemnation from the U.S. Embassy, which called the perpetrators terrorists with "no place in our hemisphere."

Suspected gang members killed 10 Guatemalan police officers over the weekend in a series of coordinated attacks across the capital, prompting President Bernardo Arévalo to declare a 30-day state of emergency that Guatemala's Congress approved Monday on a vote of 149 to 1. Schools were suspended nationwide, streets emptied, and officials announced that a tenth officer had died late Monday.

The declaration, published in the government gazette, cited "coordinated actions by self-named maras or gangs against state security forces, including armed attacks against civilian authorities." The emergency restricts freedom of assembly and movement and authorizes police to arrest suspected gang members without a judicial order. Security forces may also prohibit vehicle traffic in specific areas or subject vehicles to searches.

## The violence

The crisis began Saturday when inmates seized control of three prisons in what authorities described as apparently coordinated riots, taking 43 guards hostage. Gangs demanded privileges for their members and leaders, according to officials.

Police liberated one of the three prisons Sunday morning. Shortly afterward, suspected gang members launched attacks on police posts across the capital. By late Monday, officials said a tenth officer had died from wounds sustained in those attacks.

Police honored the fallen in a ceremony Monday at the Interior Ministry, where flag-draped coffins were displayed.

"Today it pains me to give each one of the families this flag, symbol of the nation that will not forget the sacrifice and commitment of their police fallen in the fulfillment of their duty," Arévalo said at the ceremony.

## A father's grief

Outside the Interior Ministry, José Antonio Revolorio, 72, spoke of his son José Efraín Revolorio Barrera, 25, one of the officers killed.

"I hope that the criminals who did this to my son will one day pay for it, that the law will go after them," the elder Revolorio said. "And that this doesn't end here, because my son was an honest man, competent at his work."

## Life in the capital

Residents described lighter-than-usual traffic Monday and said fear was keeping people indoors.

"This situation is a shame. It affects people psychologically: they don't want to go out," said Óscar López, 68, a radio technician who ventured out for a doctor's appointment. "I agree with the president imposing the state of emergency because it doesn't stop the violence, but it relaxes people."

Ileana Melgar, 64, said she was afraid to miss an appointment to renew her identification. "But I was afraid to go out, I called my friend to go with me," she said. "You don't know if they will also stop transportation and we can't get back home."

## U.S. response

The U.S. Embassy in Guatemala instructed American government personnel to shelter in place Sunday, lifting that directive later in the day while advising them to "maintain a high level of caution when traveling."

On Monday, the embassy condemned the attacks. "These terrorists, as well as those who cooperate with them or are linked to them, have no place in our hemisphere," the embassy said in a statement. "The security of the Guatemalan people and the stability of our hemisphere must prevail. We reaffirm our support for Guatemala's security forces to curb the violence."

## Legal context

The attacks follow a series of measures targeting gang activity in Guatemala and the United States. In October, Guatemala's Congress passed laws declaring members of the Barrio 18 and Mara Salvatrucha gangs terrorists, lengthening prison sentences for gang members who commit crimes. The United States government separately designated both gangs as foreign terrorist organizations.
