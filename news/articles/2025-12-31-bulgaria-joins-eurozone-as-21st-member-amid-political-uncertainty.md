Bulgaria joined the euro currency union on New Year's Day, becoming the 21st member of the eurozone and marking the country's deepest step toward European integration since it entered the EU in 2007. The transition arrived amid majority public opposition, a collapsed government, and a pro-Russian disinformation campaign warning of economic catastrophe.

The milestone caps a years-long accession process but leaves Bulgaria without a functioning government, a 2026 budget, or a path out of a cycle that has produced eight general elections in five years — raising questions about whether the country can convert eurozone membership into stability.

## Divided public

Two Eurobarometer surveys conducted in 2025 found majority opposition among Bulgarians. A March poll of 1,017 people showed 53% opposed eurozone entry and 45% were in favor, with a margin of error of plus or minus 3.1 percentage points, according to the Associated Press. A second survey, taken between October 9 and November 3, showed roughly half opposed and 42% in favor.

The economic anxiety underlying that skepticism is concentrated in one fear: that merchants will exploit the currency changeover to raise prices. Bulgarian inflation, which the outgoing government had compressed to 2.7% to satisfy EU accession requirements, has since rebounded to 3.7%, according to the Associated Press.

European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde said that countries joining the euro have historically experienced a slight, transient price increase of 0.2% to 0.4% immediately after the changeover. Local economists note that Bulgaria's exposure may be limited because the lev has been pegged to the euro by law since 1999 at a fixed rate of 1 lev for every 51 euro cents, meaning the currency switch carries less economic disruption than it would for a country with a floating exchange rate.

## Political collapse overshadows the transition

The government that shepherded Bulgaria through the EU accession process resigned after less than a year in office, toppled by nationwide anti-corruption protests. The country now has no regular budget for 2026, and long-delayed structural reforms and decisions on EU support funds remain in limbo. A new general election — expected to be the eighth in five years — is anticipated for spring 2026, according to the Associated Press.

Petar Ganev, an analyst at the Sofia-based Institute for Market Economics, said the resignation undercut a moment that should have strengthened Bulgaria's standing with international investors.

"Instead of capitalizing on euro adoption as a strong and positive signal to the international community — investors, debt holders, and those investing in Bulgarian assets and economic activity — we risk sending the opposite message," Ganev said in an interview with the Associated Press.

Ganev said eurozone membership could serve as an additional mechanism to address corruption and the rule of law, though it cannot by itself break the country's cycle of political fragmentation.

## Pro-Russian factions spread false claims

Anti-euro rallies organized by the pro-Russian Vazrazhdane party in May and September attracted smaller crowds than the anti-corruption protests that ended the government. Social media circulated false claims that euro adoption could lead to confiscation of bank accounts, according to the Associated Press.

Dimitar Keranov, program coordinator for engaging Central Europe at the German Marshall Fund in Berlin, said the disinformation campaign has a defined strategic purpose.

"Anti-euro disinformation spread by pro-Russian politicians and social media aim to reduce support for the European Union, NATO and Ukraine," Keranov said. Bulgaria's European integration "is not in Moscow's interest at all, so if it can somehow polarize society and weaken support for the European Union that's what it tries to achieve," he said. Euro adoption is itself a check on that influence, Keranov added: "The further Bulgaria advances in its European integration, the harder it becomes for Russia to influence the country."

## Dual circulation through January

The lev and the euro will both be accepted for cash transactions throughout January 2026, but customers will receive change only in euros. After the month ends, the lev ceases to circulate.

Bulgaria, one of the poorest of the EU's 27 member states, has a population of 6.4 million and an average monthly wage of 1,300 euros ($1,530), according to the Associated Press.

Nevelin Petrov, 64, told the Associated Press he welcomed the change. "Bulgaria is a full member of the European Union, and its rightful place is alongside the other developed and democratic European nations," he said. "I am convinced that the adoption of the euro will contribute to the long-term prosperity of our country."

Darina Vitova, who runs a pedicure salon in Sofia, said the transition felt premature despite her general support for it in principle. "The standard of living and incomes in our country are far from those in the richest European countries, while prices here are rising and life for the average person will become more difficult," she said.
