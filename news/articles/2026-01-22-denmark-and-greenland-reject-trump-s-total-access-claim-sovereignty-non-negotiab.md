The exchange marks a partial de-escalation in a weeks-long dispute over Greenland's future, but a fundamental disagreement persists over the scope of any U.S.–Greenland security arrangement even as a diplomatic working group involving both governments and senior U.S. officials continues its work.

COPENHAGEN — Denmark and Greenland's leaders said Thursday that the island's sovereignty was non-negotiable, directly rebutting President Donald Trump's assertion that a framework agreement with NATO secretary general Mark Rutte would give the United States "total access" to the semiautonomous Arctic territory. NATO said Rutte had proposed no compromise on sovereignty in his discussions with Trump.

The declarations came a day after Trump reversed course on tariff threats he had issued against eight European nations to press for U.S. control over Greenland. Trump told Fox Business that "we're going to have total access to Greenland" and "all the military access we want" under what he described as "the framework of a future deal."

Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen said security in the Arctic was a matter for all of NATO and that it was "good and natural" that Trump and Rutte discuss it. But she added that Denmark and Greenland "cannot negotiate on our sovereignty" and said she had been informed that sovereignty had not been at issue in Rutte's discussions with Trump. She said she had spoken with Rutte before and after his meeting with Trump at the World Economic Forum in Davos.

## NATO's position

NATO spokesperson Allison Hart said Rutte "did not propose any compromise to sovereignty during his meeting with President Trump." She said negotiations between Denmark, Greenland, and the United States "will go forward aimed at ensuring that Russia and China never gain a foothold — economically or militarily — in Greenland."

Danish officials noted that NATO does not have a mandate to negotiate a deal on behalf of Denmark and Greenland.

## Greenland's response

Greenlandic Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen said he had received no briefing on any concrete details of the agreement Trump cited. "I don't know what there is in the agreement, or the deal about my country," Nielsen told reporters in the Greenlandic capital, Nuuk.

Asked whether the United States might obtain sovereignty over limited pockets of Greenland, Nielsen said that "we are ready to negotiate a better partnership and so on, but sovereignty is a red line."

Nielsen said he was cautiously relieved by the change in tone. "Until yesterday, we couldn't rule out anything," he said. "Respectful dialogue through the right channels is what we have been seeking from the get-go, and I also feel that's the intention now from the other parties, and I'm happy for that."

## The diplomatic working group

Nielsen pointed to a working group that Danish and Greenlandic foreign ministers agreed to establish with U.S. Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio the week before Trump issued the tariff threats. Denmark and the White House at the time offered differing public accounts of its purpose.

Nielsen said Rutte had delivered the message that Greenland was willing to do more and host a NATO mission.

Denmark has said the U.S., which already has a military presence in Greenland under a 1951 treaty granting it broad rights to set up military bases there with the consent of Denmark and Greenland, can expand those bases. Frederiksen said Denmark wanted to continue constructive dialogue with allies on strengthening Arctic security, including Trump's proposed "Golden Dome" missile defense system, "provided that this is done with respect for our territorial integrity."

Trump said a completed deal would allow the United States to install an element of the Golden Dome system in Greenland.

## European reaction

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, speaking at the World Economic Forum in Davos, said European NATO allies would protect "Denmark, Greenland, the north from the threat posed by Russia" and would uphold the principles of sovereignty and territorial integrity on which the trans-Atlantic partnership rested. He welcomed Trump's reversal on tariffs.

"We support talks between Denmark, Greenland (and) the United States on the basis of these principles," Merz said. "It is good news that we are making steps into that right direction."

Frederiksen traveled to the United Kingdom on Thursday for talks with British Prime Minister Keir Starmer, who credited Trump's "pragmatism" in withdrawing the tariff threats and said the "hard yards" of bolstering Arctic security could now begin.

## Skepticism in Copenhagen

On the streets of Copenhagen, residents expressed doubt about whether Trump's reversal would hold.

"I think the man has said many things and done a lot of different things to what he says," said Louise Pedersen, 22, a startup employee. "I have a hard time believing it. I think it's terrifying that we stand here in 2026."

Poul Bjoern Strand, 70, who works in advertising, said he did not trust Trump's statements. "I don't really trust anything Mr. Trump is saying," he said.

Pedersen said the decision over Greenland's future belongs to Greenlanders alone — "not Donald Trump."
