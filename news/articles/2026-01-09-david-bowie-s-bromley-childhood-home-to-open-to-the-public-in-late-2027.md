The announcement arrives as fans mark the 10th anniversary of Bowie's death, and adds a second major heritage site to London's growing tribute to a musician whose influence has stretched across six decades of popular culture.

LONDON — A London heritage charity has purchased David Bowie's childhood home in the south London suburb of Bromley and plans to open it to the public in late 2027, restoring the Victorian-era cottage to its 1960s appearance and offering visitors access to the small bedroom where the musician spent his formative years.

The Heritage of London Trust announced Thursday that the 19th-century railway worker's cottage will be returned to its period decor and will host public visits alongside creative workshops for children. The project is backed by Bowie's estate.

Bowie, born David Jones, lived in the house from 1955, when he was 8 years old, until 1967, when he was a 20-year-old working musician. Visitors will be able to enter the bedroom — measuring 9 feet by 10 feet (2.7 meters by 3 meters) — that the charity described as the place "where a spark became a flame."

Geoffrey Marsh, co-curator of the Victoria and Albert Museum's 2013 exhibition "David Bowie Is," said the house is where "Bowie evolved from an ordinary suburban schoolboy to the beginnings of an extraordinary international stardom."

Marsh cited a recollection from Bowie himself: "As he said, 'I spent so much time in my bedroom, it really was my entire world. I had books up there, my music up there, my record player.'"

## Funding and scope

The project has received a 500,000 pound charity grant and is seeking an additional 1.2 million pounds in donations. The trust has not disclosed how much it paid for the house; other properties on the same street have recently sold for upwards of 500,000 pounds (about $670,000).

Nicola Stacey, Heritage of London Trust director, said the aim is to give visitors an immersive rather than static experience.

"I'm keen that it doesn't feel static, it doesn't feel sterile, there's a sense of the family living there," Stacey said. "And a sense of that you've really walked into David Bowie's life in the 1960s."

Stacey said the idea of reinvention that Bowie embodied remains a draw for contemporary audiences. "We're used to people having all sorts of different personas and we celebrate it in a way that it wasn't celebrated back in the 1960s," she said. "And he helped pave the change."

## Tenth anniversary

The announcement came as fans mark a decade since Bowie's death at age 69 on Jan. 10, 2016, two days after the release of his final album, "Blackstar."

George Underwood, a childhood friend of Bowie's, said in a statement that the house holds particular significance.

"I've heard a lot of people say David's music saved them or changed their life," Underwood said. "It's amazing that he could do that and even more amazing that it all started here, from such small beginnings, in this house. We were dreamers, and look what he became."

The Bromley project is part of a broader wave of institutional attention to Bowie's legacy. Bowie's 90,000-item archive opened to the public last year at the V&A Museum's David Bowie Centre in east London.

From Bromley, Bowie went on to move through a succession of musical identities and styles — folk-rock, glam, soul, electronica and new wave — producing a songbook that includes "Space Oddity," "Changes," "Life on Mars," "Starman," "Young Americans" and "Heroes."
