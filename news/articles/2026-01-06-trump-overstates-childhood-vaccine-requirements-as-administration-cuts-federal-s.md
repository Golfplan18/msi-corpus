President Donald Trump posted misleading claims on social media Tuesday about the number of childhood vaccine injections required in the United States, a day after his administration took the unprecedented step of narrowing the federal vaccine schedule from protection against 18 diseases to 11.

Trump wrote that "America will no longer require 72 'jabs'" for children and shared what the Associated Press described as a misleading graphic comparing the United States to a "European country" that administered 11 "injections." Neither figure accurately represented the prior federal schedule or the legal requirements families faced, the AP reported.

Leading medical groups are rejecting the administration's revised schedule, saying no new scientific evidence warrants the change and warning that conflicting guidance could leave more children vulnerable to preventable illness and death.

## What the administration changed

The administration's new recommended-for-all schedule covers vaccines against 11 diseases, including measles, whooping cough, polio, chickenpox, and HPV, the AP reported. Additional vaccines that were once broadly recommended for all children are now separately categorized — either for children at elevated risk or as options available through "shared decision-making" between families and their doctors.

A year ago, the federal schedule recommended routine protection against 18 diseases, according to the AP.

## The injection count

Trump's claim of 72 injections significantly overstates the number of shots children received under the prior schedule. Excluding once-a-year flu vaccines — some of which can be administered as a nasal spray — and COVID-19 shots, the total was closer to three dozen, the AP reported. The precise count varied depending on the vaccine brand used and the availability of combination shots.

Under the new schedule, children receiving only the recommended-for-all vaccines would receive about 23 injections, the AP reported.

## The 'required' distinction

The vaccines on the federal schedule were never legally required of all families, the AP noted — families could opt out. States do require certain vaccinations before children enroll in school, but those state lists have been narrower than the prior full federal schedule, and many states offer different types of exemptions.

## Medical groups push back

Leading medical organizations said they are maintaining prior vaccine recommendations and cited no new scientific evidence to support the federal changes. The groups said they worry that two conflicting sets of guidance — one from federal health authorities and another from professional medical organizations — will leave parents uncertain and ultimately leave more children unprotected against preventable diseases.
