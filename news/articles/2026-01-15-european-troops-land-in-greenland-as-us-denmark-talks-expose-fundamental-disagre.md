The dispute pits President Donald Trump's stated goal of acquiring Greenland against Denmark's position that sovereignty over the island is not negotiable, while Greenland's own leaders have said the territory is not for sale. How each side characterized the same meeting illustrated how far apart the parties remain even as diplomatic contact continues.

Troops from six European countries began arriving in Greenland on Thursday in a show of solidarity with Denmark, as high-level talks between Danish, Greenlandic, and American officials in Washington laid bare what Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen called a "fundamental disagreement" over the Arctic island's future.

The divergence in how each side characterized Wednesday's White House meeting underscored how far apart the parties remain. The White House described plans for follow-on discussions as "technical talks on the acquisition agreement" for the United States to acquire Greenland. Rasmussen described the same working group as focused on how to "address the American security concerns, while at the same time respecting the red lines of the Kingdom of Denmark."

## 'Greenland is not for sale'

Greenland Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen stated his government's position Thursday in Nuuk. "Greenland is not for sale," he said. "Greenland does not want to be owned by the United States. Greenland does not want to be governed from the United States. Greenland does not want to be part of the United States."

Rasmussen, who met Wednesday at the White House alongside Greenland's Foreign Minister Vivian Motzfeldt with Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio, said afterward that it remained "clear that the president has this wish of conquering over Greenland" but that dialogue would continue at a high level in the coming weeks.

White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt said Thursday that the European troop deployments had no impact on President Donald Trump's decision-making or goal. "The president has made his priority quite clear, that he wants the United States to acquire Greenland," Leavitt said. "He thinks it's in our best national security to do that."

Trump, in an Oval Office appearance with reporters, said: "We'll see how it all works out. I think something will work out."

## European forces deploy

France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Norway, Sweden, and the Netherlands began sending troops or pledged to do so following Denmark's announcement of an expanded military presence. French authorities said approximately 15 soldiers from a mountain infantry unit were already in Nuuk for a military exercise as of Wednesday. Germany's Defense Ministry said it would deploy a reconnaissance team of 13 personnel to Greenland on Thursday.

Danish Defense Minister Troels Lund Poulsen said Thursday the intention was "to establish a more permanent military presence with a larger Danish contribution," with soldiers from multiple NATO countries operating on a rotation system. He described the working group outcome as "better than no working group" and "a step in the right direction," while adding that the dialogue did not mean "the danger has passed."

Denmark informed NATO of the exercises, and the alliance's Supreme Allied Commander Alexus Grynkewich spoke Thursday with Denmark's chief of defense, Col. Martin O'Donnell. A spokesperson for Grynkewich said such dialogue was typical and that "we all agree the Arctic — including Greenland — is important for transatlantic security." O'Donnell said the Danish exercises and additional troops "bolster our collective defenses there."

## Residents, Russia respond

Residents of Nuuk told the Associated Press they were relieved that initial talks had taken place but said many questions remained. Maya Martinsen, 21, said it was "comforting to know that the Nordic countries are sending reinforcements" because Greenland is part of Denmark and NATO. She said the dispute centered not on "national security" but on "the oils and minerals that we have that are untouched."

Russia's embassy in Brussels on Thursday criticized what it called the West's "bellicose plans," characterizing European military actions as part of an "anti-Russian and anti-Chinese agenda" by NATO. "Russia has consistently maintained that the Arctic should remain a territory of peace, dialogue and equal cooperation," the embassy said.

## Congressional contacts

Rasmussen and Motzfeldt also met with a bipartisan group of senators at the U.S. Capitol. Rasmussen told reporters Denmark would work to "accommodate any reasonable American requests" with Greenland and that the country appreciated having "close friends in the Senate and the House as well."

Juno Berthelsen, a lawmaker from Greenland's pro-independence Naleraq opposition party, said the most significant outcome was that Greenlanders were directly represented at the White House meeting and that diplomatic dialogue had begun. He suggested the United States could be involved in creating a coast guard for Greenland and providing funding and jobs for local people to help patrol the Arctic.
