## Kemp’s last State of the State—and a push to keep shaping Georgia after it
Georgia Gov. Brian Kemp delivered his final State of the State speech on Thursday in Atlanta, but Democrats and Republicans alike viewed it less as a farewell and more as a political blueprint for what Kemp may try to accomplish after he leaves office.

The Associated Press reported that Kemp is still working to mold politics in Georgia in ways that could outlast his governorship. It described Kemp as a Republican who has sometimes moved independently from President Donald Trump while staying influential in national politics.

## A small-government agenda tied to rebates and tax cuts
In his speech, Kemp argued for using Georgia’s surplus to deliver income tax rebates and accelerate a planned path toward a lower flat tax rate. He said he wants to spend **$1.17 billion** from the surplus for income tax rebates of **$500 per family or $250 per person**, which the report said would be the fourth set of such rebates.

Kemp also called to speed up Georgia’s planned income tax cuts so the state’s flat tax rate would reach **4.99%**. In the speech, he said: **“We must continue doing everything in our power to allow the hardworking men and women of our state to keep more of their hard-earned money in their pocket in the years to come.”**

## Teacher and employee bonuses, law enforcement retirement, and college aid
Kemp’s affordability agenda included additional proposals beyond rebates and tax cuts. He called for **$2,000 one-time bonuses** for teachers and for university and state employees.

The report said Kemp also proposed increasing retirement payments to law enforcement and endowing **$325 million** for Georgia’s first comprehensive need-based college aid program. Kemp framed the package as part of a broader approach, saying: **“Refusing to grow government, budgeting conservatively and paying off debt aren’t flashy.”**

## Democrats argue Kemp tilts Georgia toward the wealthy
The speech came as Democrats worked toward gains in Georgia’s top offices and in the legislature. The AP reported that Democrats hope to win the governor’s office for the first time since 1998, reelect Jon Ossoff to a second term, and move toward legislative majorities this year.

In comments carried in the report, Democratic state Rep. Al Williams of Midway said, **“I think the Republican Party has changed forever.”** Williams also described Kemp’s politics as **“conservative but not crazy.”** Charlie Bailey, chair of the Democratic Party of Georgia, said: **“I think that Brian Kemp as governor has not been honestly all that different than 22 years of Republican rule that has made the American dream less attainable for most Georgians.”**

## Planning for post-Kemp Georgia politics
The AP described Kemp’s focus as extending beyond policy proposals in the address. It said he wants to help his friend Derek Dooley, a former University of Tennessee football coach, oust Democrat Jon Ossoff from his U.S. Senate seat.

The report also said Kemp is supporting candidates for Georgia’s legislature and statewide offices who align with his small-government agenda, and that he is pushing to keep Georgia’s taxes low as a way to fuel the state’s economic growth.

## Kemp’s relationship with Trump—and how he survived internal GOP pressure
The AP traced Kemp’s political conflict with Trump over multiple years. It said Kemp first upset Trump in **2019** by naming Kelly Loeffler to a vacant U.S. Senate seat that Trump had wanted filled by then-U.S. Rep. Doug Collins.

The report also said Trump reacted negatively in **April 2020** after Kemp allowed Georgia businesses to mostly reopen during the pandemic. After the **2020** election, the AP said Kemp refused pressure from Trump to call a special legislative session to overturn the results.

It said Kemp later reconciled with Trump without endorsing Trump’s election denial. The report also said Kemp defeated a Trump-endorsed primary opponent in **2022**, after he had previously won reelection with broader suburban support.

Republican strategist Stephen Lawson said in the report that **“Brian Kemp has been a force in Georgia politics the likes of which we may not see again for some time.”** Ralph Reed, chair of the Faith and Freedom Coalition, described Kemp as someone who has been able to match different political roles, saying **“Historically, you’re either the back bench, bomb-throwing conservative candidate, or you’re the more moderate business candidate,”** and that **“But he’s been able to do both.”**

## Betting on the Senate and appointments lower on the ballot
The AP said Kemp declined to run for Senate this year despite pressure from Trump. It reported that Kemp’s effort to shape Georgia’s future includes betting on Dooley in the U.S. Senate primary in May, and that two other Republicans—Mike Collins and Buddy Carter—are also vying for the right to take on Ossoff.

The report also said Kemp has stayed out of the governor’s race. It added that Attorney General Chris Carr has called himself a **“Brian Kemp Republican.”**

The AP further reported that Kemp is trying to stamp his imprint lower on the ballot, citing his appointments of John King as insurance commissioner and Barbara Rivera Holmes as labor commissioner. It said the appointments made King and Holmes Georgia’s first two Hispanic officials in statewide elected offices and tied them to a Kemp push to expand the party as Georgia becomes more diverse. 

## Possible future beyond the governorship
The AP said some political observers have floated Kemp for national roles, including the Senate or even the presidency, but it also reported that Kemp could choose not to pursue another office. As Democrats look toward statewide gains, Kemp’s final State of the State speech served both as a policy statement and as a sign of how he intends to remain influential after leaving the governor’s mansion.
