A Dutch court on Wednesday ordered the government to draw up a plan to protect residents of the Caribbean island of Bonaire from the effects of climate change. The Hague District Court ruled that the government discriminated against the island's 20,000 inhabitants by failing to take "timely and appropriate measures" to shield them from rising sea levels and extreme weather. The decision marks a sweeping legal victory for eight residents and environmental group Greenpeace, who brought the case seeking to compel protective action before parts of the island are submerged.

The court found that government inaction constitutes discrimination and ordered binding emissions reduction targets within 18 months—a mandatory intervention into climate policy that could set a precedent for similar legal challenges elsewhere.

Judge Jerzy Luiten delivered the court's assessment of what awaits the island. "The island already suffers from flooding due to tropical storms and extreme rainfall, and according to several researchers, this will worsen in the coming years," he said. "Even conservative forecasts predict that parts of the island will be underwater by 2050, so in 25 years."

The court ordered the Dutch government to set binding targets within 18 months, written into law, to reduce greenhouse gas emissions in line with the Paris Climate agreement, which commits nations to keeping global temperature rise "well below" 2 degrees Celsius compared with preindustrial times.

## Bonaire's Victory

Bonaire, along with St. Eustatius and Saba, became special Dutch municipalities in 2010. The island's 20,000 residents hold Dutch citizenship due to the islands' colonial history. The case was brought by eight residents and supported by Greenpeace, which said it would carefully monitor how the government implements the court's orders.

In the courtroom, resident Jackie Bernabela spoke with visible emotion about the ruling. "The judges heard us," she said. "We are no longer second-class citizens. Equality. I am very happy."

Sophie Hermans, the Dutch minister for climate policy and green growth, said in response, "Today, the court delivered a ruling of significance for the residents of Bonaire and the European Netherlands." She said the government would study the decision with other departments before responding. The government can appeal the court's 90-page written decision.

Greenpeace campaigner Eefje de Kroon welcomed the outcome. "This is an amazing victory not only for the people of Bonaire," she said, "but for all of us, because the Dutch government needs to do more to stop the climate crisis and actually protect people who are already experiencing the effects."

## Inadequate Climate Commitments

Government lawyers had argued that the Netherlands is already doing its part to combat climate change, citing greenhouse gas reductions and mitigation efforts. They contended that climate policy should be determined by national administrations rather than judges. However, the court found that the government's target to reduce emissions by 55% by 2030 compared to 1990 levels was not binding and did not fully account for emissions from air and sea transport. The court also ruled that the Netherlands was "highly unlikely" to meet even this non-binding 2030 target.

## A Landmark in Climate Law

The ruling carries political weight as the Netherlands forms a new government. Negotiations following an October election appear to be producing a coalition led by Rob Jetten, a centrist D66 leader. Jetten earned the nickname "climate pusher" when he previously served as a minister responsible for advancing legislation to reduce the Netherlands' dependence on fossil fuels and cut carbon emissions. The incoming government will face pressure to tighten climate measures to comply with the court's order.

The Hague District Court has previously issued significant climate rulings. The same court heard the initial stage of the landmark Urgenda case, which the Dutch Supreme Court decided in 2019 in favor of climate activists, ordering the government to cut emissions. That decision enabled similar legal challenges worldwide. The Urgenda ruling was cited prominently in recent decisions by the European Court of Human Rights and the International Court of Justice, both of which found that failing to combat climate change violated international law.

Climate data underscores the stakes. Global sea levels rose by an average of approximately 4.3 centimeters over the decade through 2023, with some regions of the Pacific experiencing greater increases. The world has warmed 1.3 degrees Celsius since preindustrial times due to the burning of fossil fuels.
