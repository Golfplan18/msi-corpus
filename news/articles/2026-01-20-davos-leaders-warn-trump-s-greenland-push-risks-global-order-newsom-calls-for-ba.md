World leaders at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland, warned Tuesday that U.S. President Donald Trump's drive to seize Greenland and impose sweeping new trade tariffs is dismantling the rules-based international order that has governed the West since World War II. California Gov. Gavin Newsom, delivering some of the forum's sharpest remarks in the entrance hall, called on European governments to "stand tall and firm — have a backbone" and stop what he characterized as complicity in the face of American pressure.

The annual gathering of heads of government and global business leaders in the Swiss Alps convened as Trump escalated both territorial ambitions over Greenland and threats of broad new tariffs, provoking some of the bluntest public criticism of Washington from Western allies in recent memory — and drawing a counterargument from Trump's treasury secretary, who told critics to "calm down the hysteria."

## Newsom: 'Stop with this diplomacy of niceties'

Newsom, a prominent Democrat and California's governor, delivered some of the forum's most pointed language as he addressed European leaders directly.

"I can't take this complicity. People rolling over. I should've brought a bunch of knee pads for all the world leaders," Newsom said. "I hope people understand how pathetic they look on the world stage. I mean, at least from an American perspective, it's embarrassing."

He pressed further: "Diplomacy with Donald Trump? He's a T-Rex. You mate with him or he devours you. One or the other ... Wake up! Where the hell has everybody been? Stop with this (expletive) diplomacy of sort of niceties and somehow we're all going to figure it out, saying one thing privately and another publicly. Have some spine, some goddamn (expletive)."

## Macron warns of 'new colonial approach'

French President Emmanuel Macron, wearing aviator sunglasses because of an eye infection, opened his remarks with a wry joke — "It's a time of peace, stability and predictability" — before turning to a pointed indictment of what he described as a return to imperial logic.

"It's a shift towards a world without rules. Where international law is trampled underfoot and where the only law that seems to matter is that of the strongest, and imperial ambitions are resurfacing," Macron said.

He then directed his criticism specifically at Washington's trade posture, denouncing "competition from the United States of America, through trade agreements that undermine our export interests, demand maximum concessions, and openly aim to weaken and subordinate Europe, combined with an endless accumulation of new tariffs that are fundamentally unacceptable, even more so when they are used as leverage against territorial sovereignty."

## Von der Leyen: 'A deal is a deal'

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said the proposed additional U.S. tariffs represent a strategic error between allies rather than adversaries.

"The proposed additional tariffs are a mistake, especially between long-standing allies. The European Union and the United States have agreed to a trade deal last July. And in politics, as in business, a deal is a deal," she said.

Von der Leyen warned that a diplomatic "downward spiral" in the West would benefit its adversaries, saying the United States and Europe "consider the people of the United States not just our allies, but our friends. And plunging us into a downward spiral would only aid the very adversaries we are both so committed to keeping out of the strategic landscape."

She added that the continent must now chart a different course. "My point is: if this change is permanent, then Europe must change permanently too. It is time to seize this opportunity and build a new independent Europe," von der Leyen said.

## Carney: Middle powers 'on the menu'

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, the former central banker who leads the country that shares the longest undefended border with the United States, used his remarks to call on middle-tier powers to build new networks of cooperation rather than compete for Washington's favor.

"Great powers can afford for now to go it alone. They have the market size, the military capacity, and the leverage to dictate terms. Middle powers do not," Carney said. "In a world of great power rivalry, the countries in between have a choice: compete with each other for favor or to combine to create a third path with impact."

He offered a blunt summary: "(We) argue the middle powers must act together because if we're not at the table, we're on the menu."

Carney also offered unambiguous support for Denmark's position on its autonomous territory. "We stand firmly with Greenland and Denmark and fully support their unique right to determine Greenland's future," he said.

## De Wever: 'The end of 80 years of Atlanticism'

Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever, speaking on a Davos panel, said the Greenland provocations had already pushed beyond the limits of what European allies could absorb.

"Being a happy vassal is one thing. Being a miserable slave is something else. If you back down now you're going to lose your dignity," said De Wever, a former mayor of Antwerp who quoted the Italian philosopher Antonio Gramsci in his remarks.

He framed the stakes in generational terms. "We either stand together or we will stand divided, and if we are divided, there is the end of an era, of 80 years of Atlanticism, really drawing to a close," De Wever said. He added that it would be "up to him (Trump) to decide if he wants to be a monster - yes or no."

De Wever and Belgium's King Philippe were set to meet with Trump on Wednesday, according to the Associated Press, where he said he planned to press for a restoration of the traditional military alliance between Brussels and Washington.

## Bessent: 'Take a deep breath'

With Trump not scheduled to address the forum until Wednesday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent took up his principal's defense in Switzerland, arguing that the tensions have been overstated.

"I think our relations have never been closer," Bessent said, playing down the rift among Western countries over Greenland. "Calm down the hysteria. Take a deep breath."

Bessent acknowledged the disagreements while insisting they did not undermine the broader alliance. "We are in the middle of President Trump's policies. And of course, Europe is an ally, the U.S.-NATO membership is unquestioned. We are partners in trying to stop this tragic war between Russia and Ukraine, but that does not mean that we cannot have disagreements on the future of Greenland," he said.

## Tusk: 'Appeasement means humiliation'

Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk, writing on X, invoked the historical lessons of accommodation toward aggressive powers.

"Appeasement is always a sign of weakness. Europe cannot afford to be weak — neither against its enemies, nor ally. Appeasement means no results, only humiliation. European assertiveness and self-confidence have become the need of the moment," Tusk wrote.
