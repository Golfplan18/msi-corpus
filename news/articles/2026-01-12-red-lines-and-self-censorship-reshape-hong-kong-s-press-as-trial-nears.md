Hong Kong’s press freedom has deteriorated sharply over two decades as Beijing’s 2020 national security law reshaped the territory’s media environment, according to the Associated Press. The AP said Hong Kong’s ranking in a global press freedom index fell from 18th to 140th over about 20 years. It linked the decline to the shutdown of pro-democracy outlets, more “red lines” for journalists, and increasing self-censorship.

The AP said the erosion of press freedom parallels a broader curtailment of Western-style civil liberties in Hong Kong since 2020, when Beijing imposed the national security law it said was needed to eradicate challenges to its rule. The AP reported that Apple Daily founder Jimmy Lai was convicted in December under the law and faces up to life in prison.

Hearings began Monday for Lai and other defendants to argue for a shorter sentence. The AP said Lai’s trial has been watched closely by foreign governments and political observers as a barometer of media freedom in the former British colony, which returned to Chinese rule in 1997. The AP also reported that the government has insisted the case has nothing to do with press freedom.

In detailing how Hong Kong’s media landscape changed, the AP said Apple Daily’s newsroom had once been able to ask aggressive questions of the government even as its ownership was pro-Beijing, with outlets breaking stories critical of politicians and officials. That space narrowed after China imposed the security law and, the AP said, Lai became one of the first prominent figures charged under it in 2020.

The AP said authorities arrested senior executives of Apple Daily within a year, raided the outlet’s office, and froze $2.3 million of its assets, forcing the newspaper to shut down in June 2021. It said the online outlet Stand News met a similar fate in December 2021, when arrests, police raids and asset freezes forced its shutdown. By 2022, the AP said, Hong Kong had dropped 68 places to 148th in a press-freedom index compiled by Reporters Without Borders.

The AP said the legal crackdown continued in subsequent years. It reported that in 2024 two Stand News editors became the first journalists since 1997 to be convicted of conspiracy to publish seditious articles under a separate, colonial-era law. In December, the AP said Lai was found guilty of conspiring with others to collude with foreign forces and conspiracy to publish seditious articles, while six Apple Daily executives charged in the same case entered guilty pleas.

The AP said the six executives admitted they conspired with Lai to request sanctions, blockades or engage in other hostile activities against Hong Kong or China. It also described how academic and industry figures say the security law has narrowed what journalists can do in practice. Francis Lee, a journalism and communication professor at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, said the Apple Daily and Stand News cases indicate that some common news practices from the past are no longer permitted. Lee said the Stand News case showed that strongly critical commentaries with relatively intense expression might be considered seditious, and that Lai’s case involved allegations of calling for foreign sanctions.

“Maybe some advocacy journalism was at least permitted within the legal framework back then,” Lee said, referring to before the security law was introduced. “Today, it’s no longer allowed.”

The AP reported that Lee said self-censorship has become more prominent, but that pressure is not only political. He said mainstream news outlets face pressure not to upset vital revenue streams, including advertisers and big companies, amid a difficult business environment, and that many large companies in the city value the vast mainland Chinese market and ties with the government. Lee said finding interviewees is also harder, adding: “No one is willing to speak. Self-censorship is a broad social phenomenon.” The AP said many opposition politicians and leading activists were jailed under the security law and dozens of civil society groups closed down, and that residents also became more reluctant to talk to reporters.

Selina Cheng, chairperson of the Hong Kong Journalists Association, told the AP that many stories perceived to be politically sensitive or potentially questioning the authorities are not always easily published, and that there is an outsized concern over including responses from the government and pro-China groups to create balance. “To do journalism in Hong Kong means that people always have to worry at the back of their heads: What are the risks that they may get involved in?” Cheng said.

The AP said the aftershocks of a massive deadly fire in late November illustrated those constraints. It reported that after the fire broke out on Nov. 26, reporters including those from newer online outlets covered Hong Kong’s deadliest blaze in decades by interviewing affected residents, investigating scaffolding nettings that authorities said contributed to the fire’s rapid spread, and reporting on concerns over government oversight.
