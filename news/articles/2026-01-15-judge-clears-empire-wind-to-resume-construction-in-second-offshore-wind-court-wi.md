The ruling is the latest in a string of courtroom losses for the Trump administration's effort to halt five major offshore wind projects it froze on the East Coast days before Christmas, citing national security. Offshore wind represents the primary path to large-scale renewable energy for densely populated coastal states that have little room for onshore turbines or solar arrays.

A Trump-appointed federal judge on Thursday allowed the Empire Wind offshore wind project in New York to resume construction, dealing the Trump administration its second consecutive legal defeat this week in its effort to freeze East Coast offshore wind development.

Judge Carl J. Nichols of the U.S. District Court ruled that Equinor, the Norwegian energy company that owns Empire Wind, could continue building while he evaluates the merits of the government's suspension order. Nichols criticized the administration for failing to address key arguments in Equinor's legal filings, including the company's contention that the government violated proper administrative procedure.

"We want to build this project and deliver a major and essential new energy source for New York," said Molly Morris, Equinor's senior vice president overseeing Empire Wind.

## Second court loss in a week

The Empire Wind ruling follows a Monday decision by Senior Judge Royce Lamberth, who cleared Danish energy company Ørsted to resume construction of Revolution Wind, a project serving Rhode Island and Connecticut that is nearly complete. Lamberth said the government had not adequately explained the need for a complete construction halt. Revolution Wind is expected to cover roughly 20% of Rhode Island's electricity needs and about 5% of Connecticut's.

The Trump administration froze five large East Coast offshore wind projects days before Christmas, citing national security concerns. Developers and state authorities sued to block the order. Trump has repeatedly attacked offshore wind since returning to the White House, recently calling wind farms "losers" that lose money, destroy the landscape, and kill birds.

## Empire Wind's stakes

Empire Wind is 60% complete and is designed to serve more than 500,000 New York homes. Equinor said the project was at risk because of limited availability of specialized construction vessels and mounting financial losses from the delay.

During a hearing Wednesday, Nichols said the government's primary national security concern appeared to be about turbine operation rather than construction — a characterization the government disputed. Justice Department attorney Stanley Woodward Jr., arguing for the administration, said he was skeptical of Equinor's claims that the project would be irreparably harmed if construction did not resume immediately. He compared the situation to a nuclear facility under construction that posed a national security risk, arguing the government would oppose both building and operation of such a project.

Not all parties supported the ruling. Robin Shaffer, president of Protect Our Coast New Jersey, said the administration acted correctly in halting construction on national security grounds and urged officials to immediately appeal the adverse decisions and seek a stay of all pending construction work. Opponents of offshore wind projects are particularly active and organized in New Jersey, according to the Associated Press.

## Remaining projects

Three other offshore wind projects remain under the government's freeze. Ørsted is also challenging the suspension of its Sunrise Wind project, which would serve New York; no hearing date has been set. Dominion Energy Virginia, which is developing Coastal Virginia Offshore Wind, planned to ask a judge Friday to lift the government's order as well.

Vineyard Wind, under construction off Massachusetts and owned by Avangrid and Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners, also remains halted. Neither company has publicly indicated whether it plans to mount a legal challenge to the federal suspension.

Offshore wind is a cornerstone of renewable energy plans for densely populated coastal states that have limited space available for onshore turbines or solar panels.
