Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney departed Beijing on Saturday after meetings with Chinese President Xi Jinping produced preliminary trade agreements reducing Canada's tariff on Chinese electric vehicles and China's tariff on Canadian canola seeds, Carney said. The EV deal sets an initial annual import cap of 49,000 vehicles at a 6.1% tariff rate, growing to approximately 70,000 over five years, while China agreed to cut its canola tariff from 84% to approximately 15%.

The agreements represent a significant step in rebuilding Canada-China ties that have been largely stalled for nearly a decade, and come as Ottawa works to reduce its economic dependence on the United States amid strained relations under President Donald Trump. Carney said his government is focused on building "an economy less reliant on the United States at a time of global trade disruption."

## Trade agreement terms

Under the preliminary EV arrangement, Chinese electric vehicles would enter Canada at a 6.1% tariff rate subject to an initial annual cap of 49,000 vehicles, rising to approximately 70,000 over five years, Carney said. Canada had previously applied a 100% tariff on Chinese EVs.

China's reduction of the canola tariff — from 84% to approximately 15% — stands to benefit Canadian agricultural exporters significantly. Canola seeds are among Canada's major export commodities. China is Canada's second-largest trading partner after the United States.

The Chinese side offered fewer specifics about the outcomes of the meetings. Both agreements remain preliminary, Carney said.

During his time in Beijing, Carney also met with several leading Chinese companies.

## Leaders' statements

Xi, in remarks after the meeting, offered measured praise for the direction of relations. "It can be said that our meeting last year opened a new chapter in turning China–Canada relations toward improvement," Xi said.

Carney addressed reporters Saturday at an outdoor Beijing news conference, framing the visit in terms of security as well as trade. "The security landscape continues to change," Carney said. "We face many threats. You manage those threats through alliances."

Asked to compare the predictability of Canada's relationships with China and the United States, Carney said the trajectory with China had become "more predictable" in recent months, though the precise referent of his comparison was ambiguous in context. Carney also acknowledged differences in culture and "ways of life" between the two countries when asked about China's approach to human rights.

Chinese state media framed the week's encounters in terms of shared pressures. China's official Xinhua News Agency cited the importance of ties with Canada particularly "in a time of heightened uncertainty," pointing to "economic fragmentation, geopolitical tensions and resurgent protectionism."

## U.S. backdrop

Trump has not struck a deal with Canada to reduce tariffs that have weighed on the Canadian economy, and has made repeated statements suggesting Canada could become the 51st U.S. state, remarks that drew sharp criticism in Canada.

Trump nonetheless commended the Beijing outcome. "That's what he should be doing and it's a good thing for him to sign a trade deal," Trump said. "If you can get a deal with China, you should do that."

## Analysts on the implications

Nelson Wiseman, professor emeritus of political science at the University of Toronto, said the week's outcome reflected a shift in the North American diplomatic landscape. "China is succeeding in driving a small wedge between Canada and the U.S.," Wiseman said.

Robert Bothwell, professor of Canadian history and international relations at the University of Toronto, contrasted what he described as the divergent interests of Beijing and Washington regarding Canada. "My guess is that the Chinese want the opposite in Canada to what Trump wants — a coherent country with a functioning economy. And of course friendlier to China, where a relatively strong Canada is more desirable than not," Bothwell said. "Trump wants the opposite — a fragmented satellite that is easier to bully and extort from."
