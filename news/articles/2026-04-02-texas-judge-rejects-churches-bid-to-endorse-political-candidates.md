## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- A federal judge in Tyler dismissed a lawsuit seeking to let churches endorse political candidates without losing tax-exempt status.
- District Judge Cam Barker said he lacked authority to approve a proposed consent judgment because it could directly affect taxes that had not yet been collected.
- The lawsuit, brought by Texas churches and national Christian groups, challenged the Johnson Amendment’s limits on political activity by 501(c)(3) nonprofits under the First Amendment.
- Americans United for Separation of Church and State said the Johnson Amendment should remain in place, calling the proposed settlement unfair.

Texas churches and national Christian groups seeking to broaden political endorsements from the pulpit suffered a setback in federal court, where a judge dismissed their challenge to the Johnson Amendment. District Judge Cam Barker of the U.S. District Court in Tyler ruled that the court lacked authority to approve a settlement that would have halted the law’s enforcement against the plaintiffs.

The case targeted the Johnson Amendment, a decades-old federal restriction that bars tax-exempt charities under Section 501(c)(3) from endorsing or opposing political candidates. The plaintiffs argued that forcing churches to avoid electioneering from the pulpit in order to keep their exemption violated their First Amendment rights, and they asked the Internal Revenue Service not to enforce the Johnson Amendment against them.

In the final days of the Biden administration, the Department of Justice sought dismissal of the case, according to the court filings described in the lawsuit record. The Trump administration revived the matter and sided with the plaintiffs as the parties sought a deal in which the IRS would agree not to enforce the Johnson Amendment against the churches.

As outlined in the lawsuit materials and court posture, the plaintiffs and their allies had argued the proposed consent judgment would effectively change how the tax code applies to religious speech, a requirement that has been part of the U.S. tax code since it was introduced in the 1950s and named for then-Texas Sen. Lyndon B. Johnson. The plaintiffs sought judicial relief that would have protected their tax-exempt status while allowing them to make political endorsements.

Barker rejected that approach even though both sides asked him to approve the settlement. He cited federal limits on courts approving relief that would function as a direct bar to future taxation before taxes had been assessed and collected, and he concluded the proposed relief would directly affect the amount of tax that could be collected.

In his ruling, Barker wrote that “Relief enjoining the Johnson Amendment’s enforcement or declaring that it does not apply to specific conduct would thus directly bear on the amount of tax that could be collected.” He added: “Put differently, if the plaintiffs here gave up their § 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status, none of the harms they allege could occur.”

Barker also noted that other legal pathways remained available to challenge the issue after taxes were collected, or after any loss of tax-exempt status tied to alleged Johnson Amendment violations. He concluded the case still was not the proper venue for the relief the parties sought, even if both sides agreed to the terms of the proposed consent judgment.

Americans United for Separation of Church and State, which attempted to intervene, applauded the decision. Rachel Laser, the group’s president, said in response to the ruling: “We’re glad that the Johnson Amendment will remain a strong bulwark to stop religious extremists from exploiting houses of worship,” adding that the proposed settlement agreement “would have been unfair and a violation of church-state separation.”

Even before the court could approve any judgment, some conservative Christian pastors began touting what they viewed as a victory and preparing to intensify political rhetoric. Others, including the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops, said they would continue steering clear of candidate endorsements from the pulpit regardless of the outcome in the case.

The Johnson Amendment has faced repeated challenges, and enforcement has varied across administrations. The Texas Tribune and ProPublica reported that over a two-year period, at least 20 examples of churches violating the statute were identified, a tally that they said exceeded the number of investigations the IRS had conducted in the past decade.
