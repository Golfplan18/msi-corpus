Both Israel and Iran emerged from a 12-day war last summer with heavy casualties and diminished appetite for renewed fighting, according to analysts, placing the ongoing protests — which have spread across Iran and resulted in dozens of deaths — in a tense regional holding pattern that neither government appears eager to internationalize.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said Sunday that Israel is "closely monitoring" the fallout from widespread protests inside Iran, praising the "tremendous heroism" of Iranian citizens while condemning the killing of civilians. He said he hoped to rebuild relations between Israel and Iran once the country was "freed from the yoke of tyranny."

Netanyahu's remarks, delivered at the start of the weekly cabinet meeting, came as U.S. President Donald Trump renewed threats to strike Iran — threats that analysts warned could have the paradoxical effect of weakening the protests by rallying Iranian public sentiment against an outside enemy.

Netanyahu and U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio spoke overnight Saturday about a number of issues, including Iran, according to an Israeli official who spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to speak to the media.

## Israel's military posture: watchful but restrained

Israel's military said the protests are an "internal Iranian matter" and that it has issued no new guidelines directing civilians to stay near bomb shelters — a precaution taken during the 12-day Israel-Iran war last summer when concrete threats of Iranian missile fire prompted such instructions. The military said it "will be equipped to respond with power if need be."

Danny Citrinowicz, a former head of Iran research in one of the Israeli military's intelligence branches and now a senior researcher at the Institute for National Security Studies, said Israel is unlikely to instigate an attack against Iran, even with Iranian leadership weakened and distracted by the protests.

"From an Iranian standpoint, the last thing Iran wants to see is diverting their attention towards Israel," Citrinowicz said. "Their priority, first and foremost, is to retrieve the calmness and stability in Iran."

He added that "neither side has an appetite" to start a new round of the war.

## Background: the summer war and its toll

The 12-day conflict last summer began with Israel targeting Iranian nuclear and military sites, citing its determination to prevent Tehran from developing atomic weapons and its assessment that the Islamic Republic was close to doing so. Iran has long maintained that its nuclear program is peaceful. The U.S. both brokered the ceasefire and assisted Israel during the war by dropping bunker-buster bombs on multiple Iranian nuclear sites — strikes that were crucial, Netanyahu told the Israeli public, in achieving Israel's objectives before accepting Trump's truce.

Israeli strikes killed 1,190 people in Iran and wounded another 4,475, according to the U.S.-based Human Rights Activists News Agency. Iran's missile barrages killed almost 30 people in Israel and wounded 1,000.

## Iran's parliament issues threats

On Sunday, Iran's parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf warned that the U.S. military and Israel would be "legitimate targets" if the United States strikes Iran. Lawmakers rushed the speaker's dais in the Iranian parliament, shouting "Death to America!"

Trump, who posted multiple times on social media about Iran over the weekend, has a history of following through on stated threats, the State Department indicated Saturday. "Do not play games with President Trump. When he says he'll do something, he means it," the department said.

Citrinowicz said an attack — whether American or Israeli — could produce the opposite of the intended effect on the protests, possibly weakening them by "fostering a sense of patriotism and uniting against a common enemy."

## Israel's focus: missiles, not political outcomes

Menahem Merhavy, an expert on Iran at Hebrew University of Jerusalem, said Israel's primary concern is Iran's ballistic missile capabilities, not the political character of whatever government emerges from the unrest.

"What Israel is really concerned with is ballistic missiles, and stuff like that, not what kind of regime is going to be in Iran," Merhavy said. "Unless there's something really dramatic happening with missiles, I don't see Israel stepping into this."

An Iranian attack against Israel would be "a suicide note for the regime," Merhavy added, because there would be little international outcry if Israel responded forcefully against the Iranian leadership given the reaction to the government's handling of the protests. "There are few tears that will be shed if, say, Israel kills the minister of foreign affairs," he said.

"Israel doesn't want to meddle with this. It's internally an Iranian matter," Merhavy said.
