Gaza's only gateway to the outside world not controlled by Israel reopened on Monday as part of the two-year Israel-Hamas war's ceasefire agreement, according to Israeli and Egyptian officials. The Rafah border crossing had been shuttered since Israeli troops seized it in May 2024. Though the reopening provides hope for Palestinians seeking medical treatment or family reunification, authorities are severely limiting initial passage—allowing only 50 medical evacuees plus two escorts to cross while 50 Palestinians who fled during the war can return.

The symbolic reopening falls far short of Gaza's humanitarian crisis. The gap between what the crossing allows and the needs of thousands reveals how fragile the ceasefire remains, with critical questions unresolved about long-term management and whether the crossing can scale to support reconstruction.

Before the war began with Hamas-led attacks on southern Israel on Oct. 7, 2023, the Rafah crossing handled movement of people and cargo. It was Gaza's only crossing not under Israeli control. After the war started, Egypt tightened restrictions, effectively sealing off the strip's sole non-Israeli gateway.

## Acute medical needs outpace limited capacity

The reopening's limitations expose the scale of the crisis. Gaza's Health Ministry says approximately 20,000 sick and wounded people require treatment abroad. More than 30,000 Palestinians are registered in Cairo seeking to return home, according to an embassy official speaking on condition of anonymity because talks are ongoing. Both numbers vastly exceed what the crossing currently allows.

As much of Gaza lies in rubble, the United Nations says the Palestinian territory's population of over 2 million people needs a massive influx of fuel, food, medicine and tents. How quickly the crossing can scale its operations to allow cargo passage will likely determine the pace of Gaza's reconstruction.

## Damage and governance uncertainty

The Gaza side of the Rafah crossing was heavily damaged during the war, complicating capacity for increased operations. With the ceasefire calling for Hamas to have no role in governing Gaza, it remains unclear who will operate the territory's side of the crossing once the war formally ends.

Currently, an EU mission is managing the crossing with assistance from plainclothes Palestinian security officers—an arrangement similar to one used during a brief ceasefire at the start of 2025. Israel said it will conduct security checks on Palestinians once they enter the zone under Israeli military control.

## Reconstruction and political leverage

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu last week said there would be no reconstruction in Gaza without demilitarization, a position that could make Israeli control of the Rafah crossing a key leverage point in postwar negotiations. U.S. President Donald Trump's son-in-law and Middle East adviser Jared Kushner said last month that postwar construction would first focus on building what he called "workforce housing" in Rafah, Gaza's southernmost city near the crossing.

Among other unknowns is the expected arrival of a new Palestinian committee of administrators appointed to govern day-to-day affairs in Gaza under Trump's proposed international "Board of Peace." The committee remains in Cairo without Israeli authorization to enter.

## A narrow window of relief

For families with urgent medical needs, the limited opening offers only marginal relief. "We hope this will close off Israel's pretexts and open the crossing," said Abdel-Rahman Radwan, a Gaza City resident whose mother is a cancer patient requiring treatment outside Gaza.
