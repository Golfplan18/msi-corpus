NATO's Article 5 mutual-defense clause — the treaty commitment that an attack on one member will be met by all — cannot function if one member targets another, a contradiction the alliance has never been forced to confront at this scale. Analysts said the standoff benefits adversaries including Russia and China, even as NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte declared weeks ago that the alliance had never been stronger.

BRUSSELS — The White House said Tuesday it is weighing military options to take control of Greenland, a semi-autonomous territory of NATO ally Denmark, prompting six European governments and Canada to defend Danish sovereignty and raising questions among analysts about whether an American move against a member state would shatter the alliance's founding mutual-defense guarantee.

In an official statement, the White House declared Greenland "a national security priority" and declined to rule out the use of force. "The president and his team are discussing a range of options to pursue this important foreign policy goal, and of course, utilizing the U.S. military is always an option at the commander in chief's disposal," the statement said.

Ian Lesser, a distinguished fellow at the German Marshall Fund of the United States and an expert on NATO, described the statement as "very striking."

"It's a low-probability, high-consequence event if it were to happen. But the odds have changed, and so it becomes more difficult to simply dismiss this as bluster from the White House," he said.

## The Article 5 Problem

NATO was founded in 1949 to counter the threat to European security posed by the Soviet Union during the Cold War, and it has kept its collective-defense commitment intact through internal disputes — including decades of military harassment between uneasy members Greece and Turkey — without one member threatening another at this scale.

Article 5 of the alliance's founding treaty commits all members to respond collectively to an attack on any one of them. But in an organization that operates on unanimity, Article 5 does not function if one member targets another.

Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen warned that the U.S. threat must be taken seriously — particularly after Trump ordered the capture of Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro in a nighttime raid — and that any U.S. attempt to take control of the island could mean the end of NATO.

Asked whether Frederiksen was right when she said that an American attack on another NATO country means "everything stops," a NATO official declined to engage the scenario. "NATO does not speculate on hypotheticals," said the official, who spoke on condition of anonymity because NATO protocol forbids the use of their name.

## Allied Response

The leaders of Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Poland and Spain issued a joint statement Tuesday backing Denmark's sovereignty. "It is for Denmark and Greenland, and them only, to decide on matters concerning Denmark and Greenland," the statement said. Canada also expressed its support.

The U.S. government recognized Danish sovereignty over Greenland at the beginning of the 20th century. Greenland has been strategically significant to North American defense since World War II, and the NATO official noted the broader security dimension.

"The Arctic is an important region for our collective security, and NATO has a clear interest in preserving security, stability and cooperation in the high north," the official said.

NATO itself remained reluctant to say anything that might provoke its leading member. Maria Martisiute, a defense analyst at the European Policy Center, said the restraint does not reduce the damage. When a leading alliance member undermines another member, it hurts "NATO's cohesion and credibility, and it serves only our adversaries such as Russia and China," she said.

## A Contradictory Moment

The standoff comes just weeks after NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte offered an assessment difficult to reconcile with the current pressure.

"I believe fundamentally that thanks to Donald J. Trump, NATO is stronger than it ever was," Rutte told BBC radio just before Christmas. "NATO has never been as strong as this moment since the fall of the Berlin Wall."

In a separate year-end address in Germany, Rutte, the former Dutch prime minister, warned that Russia might attack elsewhere in Europe within a few years should it win in Ukraine. "Conflict is at our door," he said. "Russia has brought war back to Europe, and we must be prepared for the scale of war our grandparents or great-grandparents endured."

Last summer, NATO leaders rallied behind Trump's demand that they increase defense spending, with most alliance members agreeing to match U.S. per-capita defense investment within a decade. Lesser said the Greenland threat puts those gains in a different light.

"What good is it to have revived NATO capability if it's no longer a functional political alliance" afterward? he asked. A breakdown of the alliance, he said, would be "a gift to Moscow, and it's a gift to Beijing."

## Ukraine Implications

Analysts said the Greenland dispute also threatens to distract alliance members from their efforts to support Ukraine as U.S.-led negotiations to end that war enter what officials describe as a pivotal stage.

On social media Wednesday, Trump posted that "RUSSIA AND CHINA HAVE ZERO FEAR OF NATO WITHOUT THE UNITED STATES," while also adding: "We will always be there for NATO, even if they won't be there for us."
