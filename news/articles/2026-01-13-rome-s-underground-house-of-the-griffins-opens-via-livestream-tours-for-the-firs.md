ROME — One of ancient Rome's best-preserved Republican-era homes, buried underground for nearly 2,000 years beneath the Palatine Hill, is opening to the general public for the first time. Visitors will not walk its rooms in person. Instead, a tour guide wearing a head-mounted smartphone will descend a perilously steep staircase into the House of the Griffins and livestream the visit to a small group watching above ground.

Weekly guided tours begin March 3, with sessions in Italian and English held each Tuesday. Groups are limited to 12 people, require advance reservations, and carry an additional fee beyond the standard Colosseum-Palatine Hill entrance ticket, the Associated Press reported.

The project is one of 10 European Union-funded restorations in Rome's Colosseum archaeological park, part of a broader effort to spread tourists beyond the park's most overwhelmed landmarks.

## A Republican-era home hidden for 19 centuries

The Palatine Hill rises above the Roman Forum and overlooks the Colosseum. During Rome's Republican era, traditionally dated from 509 B.C. to 27 B.C., the hill served as the site of temples and homes of leading citizens. When the Empire followed, aristocratic palaces rose atop the older structures.

The House of the Griffins is one of those buried Republican-era residences. Emperor Domitian built his palace over it in the first century A.D., pushing the earlier home underground and sealing it from public view. Excavations in the early 20th century uncovered it, but its rooms remained inaccessible to general visitors.

Project chief Federica Rinaldi said archaeologists do not know much about the family who lived there, but said they were clearly well-off. The level of decoration, she said, recalls the elegant homes of the same era in Pompeii: frescoes of richly colored faux marble, and floor mosaics of three-dimensional cubes.

Among the decorations is the fresco that names the house — an arched lunette depicting two griffins, the half-eagle, half-lion mythological creatures.

"Its location at the highest point of the hill, its distribution over several levels that take advantage of the slopes of the Palatine Hill itself, and its preservation make it today an almost textbook reference," Rinaldi said. "It was certainly a domus of the highest standard."

## Livestreaming as access and conservation

The format solves two problems at once. The home's underground rooms are accessible only via a steep staircase, making in-person general access impractical. The livestreamed tours allow visitors above ground to see the rooms through the guide's mounted camera while limiting the number of people inside — a restriction that protects the delicate frescoes from excessive humidity and carbon dioxide.

Park head Simone Quilici said the project offers a broader opportunity for the site.

"It's a great occasion to value the full territory of the park," Quilici said.

The Colosseum and Roman Forum draw the largest share of visitors to the archaeological park, often to the point of overcrowding. The EU-funded restoration program, of which the House of the Griffins is one of 10 projects, aims to make lesser-known sites accessible and to distribute foot traffic more evenly across the park's wider terrain.
