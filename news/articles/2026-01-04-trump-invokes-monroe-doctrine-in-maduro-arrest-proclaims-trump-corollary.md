Scholars say Trump's invocation follows a long line of presidential corollaries to the Monroe Doctrine stretching from Theodore Roosevelt to the Cold War era, but warn that an open-ended U.S. role governing Venezuela would be historically unprecedented — and could fracture Trump's own political coalition.

President Donald Trump invoked the Monroe Doctrine on Saturday as partial justification for the U.S. military operation that captured Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro, citing a foreign policy framework more than two centuries old to claim expansive authority over the Western Hemisphere. Trump's administration has formalized what its national security strategy calls a "Trump Corollary" to the doctrine, intended to "restore American preeminence in the Western Hemisphere." Trump also quipped that some now called it "the Don-roe Doctrine."

The Monroe Doctrine, articulated in President James Monroe's 1823 address to Congress, was originally aimed at opposing European colonization or interference in the independent nations of the Western Hemisphere. In exchange, the United States agreed to stay out of European wars and internal affairs. The doctrine has been invoked repeatedly by successive presidents to justify U.S. intervention across Latin America.

Trump said Venezuela, under Maduro's rule, had been "increasingly hosting foreign adversaries in our region and acquiring menacing offensive weapons that could threaten U.S. interests," calling those actions "in gross violation of the core principles of American foreign policy dating back more than two centuries." He added that Washington would "run" Venezuela until a suitable replacement for Maduro was in place, and framed the move in terms of energy access: "We want to surround ourselves with good neighbors, we want to surround ourselves with stability, and we want to surround ourselves with energy."

## A doctrine with deep roots — and repeated expansions

Historians noted that the Monroe Doctrine has been expanded at key turning points in U.S. history, typically at moments of regional friction or strategic interest.

The first major challenge came in the 1860s, when France installed Emperor Maximilian in Mexico. After the Civil War, France withdrew under U.S. pressure. In 1904, President Theodore Roosevelt formulated what became the Roosevelt Corollary, arguing that the United States should be permitted to intervene in unstable Latin American countries. That corollary was invoked to support Panama's secession from Colombia, which secured the Panama Canal Zone for the United States.

During the Cold War, the Monroe Doctrine was cited as a defense against communism — including the U.S. demand in 1962 that Soviet missiles be withdrawn from Cuba, and the Reagan administration's opposition to the Sandinista government in Nicaragua.

Jay Sexton, a history professor at the University of Missouri and author of *The Monroe Doctrine: Empire and Nation in Nineteenth-Century America*, said the country that has repeatedly anchored these expansions is Venezuela itself. "Historically, Venezuela has been the pretext or the trigger for a lot of corollaries to the Monroe Doctrine," Sexton said, "citing instances from the late 1800s, all the way through Trump's first administration."

## Scholars see a familiar pattern

Gretchen Murphy, a professor at the University of Texas and author of *Hemispheric Imaginings: The Monroe Doctrine and Narratives of U.S. Empire*, said Roosevelt had "claimed that the Monroe Doctrine could be extended to justify interventions that instead of defending Latin American nations from European intervention, policed them to make sure their governments acted in U.S. commercial and strategic interests." She said Trump was following the same template. "I think Trump is jumping on this familiar pattern — citing the Monroe Doctrine to legitimate interventions that undermine real democracy, and ones where various kinds of interests are served, including commercial interests," Murphy said.

Sexton said the self-branding dynamic was also predictable. "What presidents used to do is they would cloak whatever their agenda was in the Monroe Doctrine by issuing corollaries," he said. He had anticipated Trump would eventually seek to rebrand that history in his own name. "When you're talking about a Trump Corollary, I just knew Trump wouldn't want to be a corollary to another president's doctrine, that somehow this would evolve into a Trump doctrine," Sexton said.

## Risks of an open-ended commitment

Sexton warned that an extended U.S. role in Venezuela carries different risks than previous military strikes. "This is not just the sort of hit-and-run kind of job where, like in Iran a couple months ago, we dropped the missiles, and then you can then you can go on and carry on as normal," he said. "This is going to be potentially quite a mess and contradict the administration's policies on withdrawing from forever wars — and there's a lot of isolationists, within the MAGA coalition."

The Trump administration's national security strategy, released in December, laid out a series of military strikes on alleged drug trafficking vessels in the Caribbean Sea and the eastern Pacific Ocean as an expression of the Trump Corollary, aiming to combat narcotics flows and control migration. The document portrayed European allies as weak and sought to redefine the U.S. military footprint in the region.
