President Volodymyr Zelenskyy of Ukraine replaced the head of the country's Security Service on Monday and appointed former Canadian Deputy Prime Minister Chrystia Freeland as an economic development adviser, continuing a top-level government reshuffle days before a Paris summit where allied nations are expected to negotiate security guarantees for Ukraine.

Lt. Gen. Vasyl Maliuk resigned as head of Ukraine's Security Service, known as the SBU, according to the agency's website. Zelenskyy published a decree appointing Ievhen Khmara — formerly head of the SBU's "A" Special Operations Center — as acting chief. Freeland, a former journalist and longtime Canadian lawmaker of Ukrainian heritage, will focus on attracting investment and leading economic transformation, Zelenskyy said.

The appointments are the latest in Ukraine's most significant leadership shuffle in about six months, as Zelenskyy seeks to sharpen his administration's focus on defense and peace negotiations. Paris talks expected to include leaders of some 30 nations — a self-described "coalition of the willing" — will center on what security guarantees allies are prepared to offer Ukraine to deter Russia from repeating an invasion if a ceasefire is reached.

## Paris summit: troops, guarantees, and Russian resistance

Key questions heading into the Paris talks include whether member countries are willing to deploy troops inside or close to Ukraine and what authority any ceasefire oversight force would carry. Russia has said it will not accept troops from NATO countries on Ukrainian soil.

Zelenskyy said in his New Year's address that a proposed U.S.-brokered peace deal was "90% ready," but warned that the remaining 10% — believed to include the future of disputed territory — would determine whether the push for peace succeeds. Moscow has not disclosed details of the negotiations and has restated its demand that no ceasefire take effect until a comprehensive settlement is agreed.

Zelenskyy told reporters Monday he met with Budanov — the military intelligence chief appointed chief of staff the previous Friday — to examine ways to reduce Russia's numerical advantage in forces. "Russia has one significant advantage in this war, namely the ability to put pressure on Ukraine with the scale of strikes, the scale of assaults," Zelenskyy said on social media. "We have and must respond with more active use of technology, faster development of new types of weapons, new tactics."

## Freeland's credentials — and her complications with Trump

Freeland brings extensive international economic experience to her new role. Beyond serving as deputy prime minister, she held the portfolios of international trade, foreign affairs, and finance in successive Canadian cabinets and helped negotiate trade agreements with both the European Union and the United States. She is a Harvard University graduate and served as Canada's special representative for Ukraine's reconstruction while still a member of Parliament.

Zelenskyy described her as an expert with "significant experience in attracting investment and carrying out economic transformations."

Freeland's relationship with U.S. President Donald Trump could complicate the role. During Trump's first meeting with Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney in the Oval Office, Trump recalled his antipathy for Freeland. "She was terrible, actually -- she was a terrible person," Trump said. When Freeland departed former Prime Minister Justin Trudeau's cabinet, Trump wrote on social media that "her behavior was totally toxic."

One opposition lawmaker in Canada also argued that Freeland could not simultaneously serve in the country's Parliament and as an adviser to a foreign government.

## Outgoing SBU chief leaves a notable record

Maliuk's tenure at the SBU included Operation Spiderweb, which Ukraine said damaged or destroyed 41 Russian military aircraft in coordinated strikes on four air bases. The operation drew widespread attention as one of the war's more audacious strikes deep inside Russian-held airfields.

## Fighting continues on the front and in Kyiv

The war's grinding front line — stretching roughly 1,000 kilometers (about 600 miles) through southern and eastern Ukraine — showed no sign of easing. Russia fired nine ballistic missiles and 165 long-range drones at Ukraine overnight, according to the Ukrainian air force.

An overnight drone strike hit a private clinic in Kyiv's Obolon district, killing a 30-year-old patient and wounding three others, according to the capital's prosecutor's office. Energy infrastructure sustained damage across the country from drone attacks, causing additional power disruptions for civilians during the winter, Zelenskyy said.

A Ukrainian drone sparked a fire at an industrial facility in Yelets, in Russia's western Lipetsk region, regional Gov. Igor Artamonov said. No casualties were reported. The Russian airports of Ivanovo, Nizhny Novgorod, and Yaroslavl briefly suspended flights because of Ukrainian drone attacks, Russian authorities said. The Russian Defense Ministry reported downing 50 Ukrainian drones later Monday over the Belgorod, Kursk, and Lipetsk regions.
