CAIRO — Israeli troops have killed at least 77 Palestinians near Gaza's "yellow line" ceasefire demarcation since the truce took effect in October, the Gaza Health Ministry reported, as residents, medical staff, and mapping specialists say the dividing line is unmarked in some places and in others has been placed deeper into Palestinian territory than the ceasefire agreement specifies. Among those killed were teenagers and young children, the Associated Press reported Saturday.

Of the 447 Palestinians killed between the ceasefire's start and last Tuesday, at least 62 crossed the line before being shot, according to the Ministry. The Israeli military had acknowledged killing 57 people around the yellow line as of Tuesday, saying in a statement that most were militants.

The deaths, which include a 17-year-old whose body was crushed by an armored bulldozer and a 3-year-old girl killed outside her tent, have focused attention on a buffer zone that Israel's defense minister has described as "a new border line" — raising questions among Palestinians about whether a temporary military position is becoming a permanent partition of Gaza's territory.

## A line that is hard to find

Under the ceasefire, Israel withdrew its troops to a buffer zone up to 7 kilometers deep that encompasses most of Gaza's arable land, elevated terrain, and all of its border crossings, hemming more than 2 million Palestinians into a strip along the coastline and central Gaza.

Yellow barrels and concrete barriers mark portions of the line, but stretches remain unmarked. Where markers do exist, their placement does not consistently match published maps. Ahmed Abu Jahal, a resident of Gaza City's Tuffah neighborhood, said the barrels near his house stand less than 100 meters away, rather than the roughly 500 meters outlined on the Israeli military's own map. "We stay away from the barrels. No one dares to get close," he said.

Open-source intelligence analyst Chris Osiek said he geolocated yellow blocks in at least four urban areas placed several hundred meters deeper into Gaza than the line shown on the Israeli military's published map. "If it's not a proper system, with coordinates that make it easy for people to navigate where it is, then you leave the ambiguity free for the IDF to interpret the yellow line how they basically want," Osiek said.

A military official, speaking on the condition of anonymity in line with military rules, dismissed such criticism, saying any deviations from the map amount to just a few meters. The official said troops issue audible warnings and then fire warning shots whenever someone crosses the line, and that many civilians retreat when warning shots are fired, though some have been killed.

The line's exact position also differs between maps published by the Israeli military and the White House, and neither matches where troops appear to be marking it on the ground, according to Palestinians and geolocation specialists, the AP reported.

## Casualties near the line

Fadel Naeem, director of Gaza City's Al-Ahli hospital, said people of all ages, some already dead, have been arriving almost daily with bullet wounds from straying near the line. During a recent visit to the southern city of Khan Younis, Naeem said he himself nearly crossed the line unaware before locals shouted at him to turn back.

On Dec. 10, Zaher Shamia, 17, was playing with his cousin and friends near the line in northern Gaza's Jabaliya refugee camp, according to video he took before his death. Soldiers approaching with an armored bulldozer fired on the group, hitting Zaher, a witness said. His grandfather, Kamal al-Beih, said a neighbor found Zaher's body, which the bulldozer had subsequently crushed. "We only recognized him from his head," al-Beih said. Two doctors, Mohamed Abu Selmiya and Rami Mhanna, confirmed that Zaher died from gunshot wounds and was run over by a bulldozer. The military official said he was aware Shamia was a civilian and that the military was looking into the incident.

On Dec. 7, Maram Atta said her 3-year-old daughter, Ahed al-Bayouk, was playing with siblings outside their tent near the yellow line along Gaza's southern coast when Atta heard aircraft overhead, then shots. A projectile struck Ahed, who was dead before they reached the clinic. "I lost my daughter to what they keep calling a 'ceasefire,'" Atta said. "What ceasefire are they talking about?" A military official denied the killing.

## Demolitions beyond the line

Since November, Israeli forces have leveled a section of Gaza City's Tuffah neighborhood extending some 300 meters beyond the official yellow line, according to satellite photos from Planet Labs dated Oct. 14 and Dec. 18. The Israeli military says demolitions are necessary to destroy tunnels and prepare the area for reconstruction.

Abu Jahal, who returned to his damaged home in Tuffah at the ceasefire's start, said he has frequently watched new yellow barrels appear and soldiers force out anyone living on their side of the markers. On Jan. 7, Israeli fire struck a nearby house and the residents had to evacuate, he said. His own family — his wife, their child, and seven other relatives — may have to leave soon.

"The line is getting very close," he said.

## Questions about permanence

Under the ceasefire terms, Israeli forces are to remain at the yellow line only temporarily before a fuller withdrawal, though the agreement sets no timetable for that next step. With that phase of the deal stalled and Israeli troops digging into positions, some Palestinians have said they doubt the withdrawal will occur.

In December, Israel's defense minister described the yellow line as "a new border line — serving as a forward defensive line for our communities and a line of operational activity."

The Israeli military has continued leveling buildings inside the Israeli-held zone. Almost all of the city of Rafah, on Gaza's border with Egypt, has been razed over the past year, according to the AP.
