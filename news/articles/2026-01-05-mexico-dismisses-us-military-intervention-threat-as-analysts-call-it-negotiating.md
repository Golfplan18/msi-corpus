MEXICO CITY — Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum said Monday she does not believe the United States will take unilateral military action against Mexican drug cartels, dismissing the prospect days after U.S. forces intervened in Venezuela to capture President Nicolás Maduro. Mexican analysts broadly agreed, citing the two countries' deep economic interdependence and Mexico's sustained cooperation with Washington as the primary barriers to any such operation.

Analysts describe Trump administration threats of military action in Mexico as a calculated negotiating instrument aimed at extracting concessions on trade, security cooperation, and diplomatic alignment — not a genuine operational plan. That calculus, they warn, could shift as the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement heads toward mandatory revision this year.

MEXICO CITY — Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum said Monday she sees no realistic risk of U.S. military action against Mexican drug cartels, offering her most direct public reassessment days after American forces intervened in Venezuela to capture President Nicolás Maduro.

"I don't see risks (of that)," Sheinbaum said. "There is coordination, there is collaboration with the United States government."

She went further, telling reporters: "I don't believe in (the possibility of) invasion, I don't believe even that it's something they are taking seriously. Organized crime is not taken care with (foreign military) intervention."

## Mexico's position differs sharply from Venezuela's

Mexican analysts cited several structural reasons why Mexico is unlikely to face the kind of U.S. action that toppled Maduro.

Sheinbaum is a popularly elected president who commands broad legitimacy, former Mexican ambassador to the United States Martha Bárcena said. Mexico is also the United States' principal commercial partner, with approximately 40 million Mexicans living in the U.S. Bárcena noted that Secretary of State Marco Rubio "has recognized that there is high-level cooperation with Mexico" — a significant diplomatic distinction from Venezuela's adversarial posture toward Washington.

Carlos Pérez Ricart, a political analyst at Mexico's Center for Economic Research and Teaching, said U.S. military intervention would immediately sever that cooperative relationship. "Intervention, military action in Mexico would suspend that cooperation," he said. That outcome, he argued, would leave the United States without the partner it relies on for drug interdiction, border management, and deportee reception.

## Threats as a negotiating tool

Sheinbaum confirmed that the prospect of U.S. military action in Mexico has come up repeatedly in her conversations with President Donald Trump, and that she has consistently rejected it. She described her relationship with Trump as one of mutual respect.

Mexican security analyst David Saucedo said the pattern of threats — similar to those over tariffs on Mexican imports, some carried out and others not — has functioned as a "negotiation weapon" to extract "commercial, diplomatic and political advantages." He said Trump and Rubio "play good cop, bad cop," with Trump threatening and Rubio smoothing over.

Saucedo added that U.S. military action in Mexico would require money, logistics, and operational risk, while "a comment, a post on social networks doesn't cost anything" and has proven effective at generating concessions.

## Mexico has been complying

Analysts said Sheinbaum's administration has moved substantially in the direction Washington has demanded since Trump began imposing tariffs. Mexico has increased arrests, drug seizures, and extraditions. It has agreed to receive more deportees from third countries. Sheinbaum has pursued a more aggressive posture toward the cartels than her predecessor.

Pérez Ricart said this compliance record is itself a deterrent to intervention. Removing a cooperative government in exchange for the operational costs and risks of a military campaign would, by most calculations, leave the United States worse off.

## Pressure expected to intensify

Analysts do not expect the threat dynamic to ease. With the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement due for mandatory statutory revision in 2026, the Trump administration has additional leverage — and additional reasons to keep Mexico under pressure.

Former Mexican ambassador Arturo Sarukhán said the combination of ongoing tariff negotiations, the USMCA revision, and the antidrug cooperation agenda means that "the Mexican government will have to be very meticulous in its position and statements." He said Mexican alignment with Maduro's government or continued support for Cuba "would cost Mexico dearly."

Saucedo said Trump could demand greater access for U.S. security agencies operating in Mexico — even if such operations are publicly presented as coordinated and executed by Mexican authorities — and could press for more high-profile captures or restrictions on Mexican oil exports to Cuba.

## No one rules it out entirely

Despite the near-consensus that intervention is unlikely, analysts declined to foreclose the possibility entirely.

Pérez Ricart offered the most direct caveat. "The United States does not function under rational logic," he said. "At this moment all possibilities are open, including those unimaginable a year ago."
