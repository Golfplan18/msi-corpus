LISBON — André Ventura, leader of Portugal's Chega party, placed second in Sunday's presidential election with roughly 24% of the vote, setting up a Feb. 8 runoff against center-left Socialist António José Seguro, who led the 11-candidate field with nearly 31%, according to results with almost 98% of ballots counted.

None of the 11 candidates reached the more than 50% threshold required for a first-round victory. Ventura and Seguro will meet in a second-round ballot on Feb. 8 to determine who succeeds President Marcelo Rebelo de Sousa and serves a five-year term.

Ventura's showing adds to a string of electoral gains by nationalist and populist parties in Europe, which have moved closer to or obtained power in France, Germany, Italy, and neighboring Spain in recent years, according to the Associated Press.

## Chega's rise

Ventura founded Chega — the name means "Enough" in Portuguese — less than seven years ago. The party grew to become the second-largest in Portugal's parliament in 2025, six years after its founding, according to the AP.

Ventura has made immigration a central campaign issue. "Portugal is ours," he said during the campaign. He put up billboards across the country reading "This isn't Bangladesh" and "Immigrants shouldn't be allowed to live on welfare," according to the AP. Such explicit public anti-immigrant sentiment was considered unthinkable in Portugal just a few years ago, the AP reported.

Despite the campaign's focus on immigration, voters appeared more concerned about housing costs and the cost of living, according to the AP.

Chega's surge has drawn support away from Portugal's two main parties — the center-right Social Democratic Party, currently in government, and the center-left Socialist Party — which have alternated in power for the past half-century, according to the AP.

## Presidential powers

The winner will replace President Rebelo de Sousa, who has served the constitutional limit of two five-year terms.

The Portuguese presidency is largely a figurehead role without executive power; the head of state aims primarily to stand above day-to-day politics, mediating disputes and defusing tensions. The office does carry significant constitutional tools: the president can veto legislation passed by parliament, though parliament can override the veto, and can dissolve parliament and call early elections — a step referred to in Portuguese political circles as the "atomic bomb," according to the AP.

Portugal has endured its worst stretch of political instability in decades, holding three general elections in three years through last May, according to the AP. Steadying the country's political situation is among the key challenges facing the next president.

A law permitting euthanasia and physician-assisted suicide, which parliament passed in 2022 but that has been held up by constitutional challenges, is expected to reach the next president's desk for a decision, according to the AP.

Portugal has never had a female or non-white head of state. Only one woman was among the 11 candidates in Sunday's vote.

## What comes next

The runoff between Ventura and Seguro is scheduled for Feb. 8. The winner will serve a five-year term at the president's official residence in Lisbon.
