## Kyiv residents stranded in dark towers as Russia's bombs shutter power plants

Olena Janchuk lives on the 19th floor—650 steps from the ground—of a Kyiv tower block, climbing in darkness when the power cuts out, phone light bouncing off concrete. The 53-year-old former kindergarten teacher has severe rheumatoid arthritis. She has been trapped in her apartment for weeks.

The cause is relentless. Long daily blackouts—sometimes stretching 17.5 hours—have made working elevators a luxury as Russia hammers Ukraine's power plants and transmission lines. With January temperatures at minus 10 degrees Celsius (14 degrees Fahrenheit), frost creeps across the inside of her windows by morning. Her electric blanket is rationed to the coldest hours. USB charging cables sprawl across the floor. She has fashioned a makeshift fireplace from candles arranged beneath stacked bricks, designed to absorb and slowly release heat into a small room where her family huddles.

"When there's no light and heat for seventeen and a half hours, you have to come up with something," Janchuk said. "The bricks work best in a small room, so we stay in there."

## Life on the Blackout Schedule

Kyiv, a city of about 3 million people dominated by Soviet-era tower blocks, is living through the fourth winter of war. Electricity is now a rationed commodity. Residents plan their lives around electricity schedules: when to cook, shower, charge phones, run washing machines. Food is chosen for shelf life. Water is filtered into bottles and stored in buckets. Small camping gas burners heat soup or tea during blackouts.

Janchuk's mother, Lyudmila Bachurina, 72, is in charge of household chores. When power returns—usually for just a few hours—she pivots into controlled chaos.

"When the lights come on, I start turning on the washing machine, fill up water bottles, cook food, charge power banks, run around the kitchen and run around the house," Bachurina said.

By day, the family shifts into rooms catching the weak winter sun. At night, they remain fully clothed indoors as the apartment cools rapidly without central heating. Sleep is broken by air raid sirens and the need to use electricity during off-peak hours.

Outside, diesel generators rumble on commercial streets. Shoppers navigate store aisles with phone flashlights. Bars glow by candlelight. Telegram chats help neighbors check on the elderly and swap blackout schedules. Apps send alerts when electricity windows narrow—usually just hours—enough for a household reboot.

## Inequality in the Dark

The burden falls heaviest on the high-rise poor. In upscale neighborhoods, residents have pooled funds for generators to keep elevators running. But most blocks—home to pensioners, families, and people with disabilities—cannot afford them.

Disability advocates, including groups representing wounded war veterans, say staircases have become an invisible social barrier, cutting people off inside their own homes. They are urging city officials to fund generators for residential buildings.

## The Shattered Power Grid

The underlying damage is vast. Too many power stations and transmission lines have been hit to meet demand, even with electricity imports from Europe. To prevent a grid collapse, operators impose rolling blackouts, keeping hospitals and critical services alive while homes go dark.

At one coal-fired power plant struck repeatedly, shift supervisor Yuriy walks through wreckage—charred machinery, collapsed roofs, control panels melted into useless lumps. Repairs are carried out by torchlight, with giant sandbags shielding what still works. Photographs of colleagues killed on the job hang near the plant's entrance.

"After missile and drone attacks, the consequences are terrible—large-scale," Yuriy said.

"Our energy equipment has been destroyed. It is expensive," Yuriy said. "Right now, we're restoring what we can."

Ukraine's energy sector has suffered more than $20 billion in direct war damage, according to a joint estimate by the World Bank, the European Commission, and the United Nations.

## Endurance Through Winter

Kyiv has repeatedly updated its austere winter power-saving schedule, dimming or cutting streetlights in low-traffic areas and investing in less centralized power generation. But in the tower blocks, the path to restoration feels distant.

Bachurina, speaking from her daughter's frozen apartment, described the weight of winter in wartime.

"I'm tired, really tired, to be honest. When you can't go outside, when you don't see the sun, when there's no light and you can't even go to the store on your own. It wears you down," Bachurina said.

Then she paused.

"But the important thing, as all Ukrainians say now, is that we will endure anything until the war ends," she added.
