BANGKOK — Cambodia arrested and extradited Prince Holding Group chairman Chen Zhi and two other Chinese nationals to China on Tuesday at Beijing's request, Cambodia's Interior Ministry announced Wednesday. The move delivers to Chinese custody a businessman whom U.S. prosecutors charged in October with masterminding a multinational cyberfraud network that allegedly used forced labor and generated what the U.S. indictment described as $30 million a day from scams.

Chen, 38, whose Cambodian citizenship was revoked in December, was extradited along with two other Chinese citizens identified by the Interior Ministry as Xu Ji Liang and Shao Ji Hui. There was no immediate comment from the federal prosecutors' office in Brooklyn, where Chen had been indicted. Chen and the Prince Holding Group denied any wrongdoing.

"Handing Chen Zhi to China was the path of least resistance. It defuses Western scrutiny while aligning with Beijing's likely preference to keep a politically sensitive case out of U.S. and U.K. courts," said Jacob Daniel Sims, a transnational crime expert and visiting fellow at Harvard University's Asia Center.

## Multi-government sanctions and seizures preceded the extradition

U.S. prosecutors had charged Chen with wire fraud and money laundering conspiracies. U.S. authorities seized an estimated $14 billion in bitcoin linked to him or his operations, and the U.S. Treasury Department and the U.K. Foreign Office imposed sanctions against him and his companies.

British authorities froze Chen's British businesses and assets, including a 12-million-euro mansion and a 100-million-euro office building in London. Singapore launched an investigation and announced the seizure of financial assets worth more than 150 million Singaporean dollars ($114 million) and a yacht. Taiwan police seized 26 luxury cars, including a Ferrari, a Bugatti, and a Porsche.

The U.S. indictment alleged that Chen's organization scammed 250 Americans out of millions of dollars, with one victim losing $400,000 in cryptocurrency. The U.S. Treasury Department has said Americans lost at least $10 billion to Southeast Asia-based scams in 2024. The indictment further alleged that Prince Holding Group built at least 10 compounds in Cambodia, naming three — Golden Fortune, Jinbei, and Mango Park.

The Prince Holding Group issued a statement through lawyers in November saying it "categorically rejects the notion that it or its Chairman has engaged in any unlawful activity."

## Scam networks built on trafficked labor have flourished across the region

Online fraud has flourished across Southeast Asia, where law enforcement has historically been weak, particularly in Cambodia and Myanmar. Trafficked foreign nationals have been employed to run "romance" and cryptocurrency scams, recruited with false job offers and then forced to work in conditions described by investigators as near-slavery.

The U.N. Office on Drugs and Crime has estimated that scam victims worldwide lost between $18 billion and $37 billion in 2023. A 2023 report by the U.N. human rights office estimated that at least 120,000 people across Myanmar and 100,000 people in Cambodia may have been held and forced to work on online scams.

## Beijing has pressed governments across the region to act

The operations became a diplomatic embarrassment for Beijing, particularly as Chinese citizens increasingly appeared among victims. China pressured Myanmar to crack down in mid-2023, and some operators were extradited to stand trial in China, where several received death sentences. Beijing also worked with Thailand and Myanmar to conduct a large-scale release of nearly 10,000 people from scam compounds along the Thai-Myanmar border.

Cambodia had conducted its own campaign during the summer, arresting thousands of people from various scam centers. The Cambodian government, while saying it does not protect lawbreakers, had declined to directly accuse Chen or the Prince Group of wrongdoing.

Sims said sustained international pressure had left Cambodia with no viable alternative to taking action against Chen.
