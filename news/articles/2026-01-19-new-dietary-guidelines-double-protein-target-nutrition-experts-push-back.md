New federal dietary guidelines advise Americans to nearly double their daily protein intake and prioritize animal-based sources at every meal, the Trump administration announced this month. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said the shift marks the end of "the war on protein."

Leading nutrition researchers said the higher targets exceed what current evidence supports for the general population and warned that sharply increased intake could raise health risks including abdominal fat accumulation and diabetes for people not engaged in strength training.

The revision marks the most significant upward adjustment to U.S. protein recommendations in decades, but several nutrition scientists said it risks accelerating demand for protein-enriched processed foods — working against a separate directive in the same guidelines urging Americans to eat more whole foods.

The new guidelines recommend adults consume 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight each day — up from the longstanding federal standard of 0.8 grams per kilogram, or about 54 grams daily for a 150-pound person. Adults should consume a minimum of 100 grams of protein daily, with at least half from animal sources such as red meat and whole milk, the guidance says. The guidelines also include a revised food pyramid that reduces emphasis on plant-based protein options.

"We are ending the war on protein," Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said in a White House social media post.

A scientific review published with the new guidelines said the previous recommendation "represents the lowest intake that maintains equilibrium in most healthy adults but does not reflect the intake required to maintain optimal muscle mass or metabolic function under all conditions." The new guidance relied on evidence from 30 studies examining the effects of higher protein diets on weight management and nutrient adequacy, the review said.

Several prominent nutrition researchers raised concerns about the shift. Dr. Dariush Mozaffarian, a nutrition expert at Tufts University, said in an article published in the Journal of the American Medical Association that there is little evidence, outside of strength or resistance training, that "higher protein builds muscle or provides other health benefits."

"If you're actively building muscle with strength or resistance training, more protein can help," Mozaffarian said. "Otherwise, you're getting enough."

Mozaffarian also warned of potential harms from excess intake. "In fact, excess dietary protein can be converted to fat by the liver," he wrote in the JAMA article. That conversion can increase dangerous fat surrounding vital abdominal organs and raise the risk of diabetes, he said.

Christopher Gardner, a nutrition expert at Stanford University, said the protein push would likely produce effects opposite to those the broader guidelines intend. Sales of protein-enriched packaged foods — bars, cereals, salty snacks, and even water — were already climbing, Gardner said, and the new targets would accelerate that trend. "I think they're going to confuse the public in a big way," Gardner said. He added: "I think the American public's gonna go buy more junk food."

Not all nutrition experts opposed the direction of the change. Dr. David Ludwig, an endocrinologist and researcher at Boston Children's Hospital, said the protein emphasis could help if it steered consumers away from highly processed carbohydrates. "The main problem with the food supply is the processed carbohydrates," Ludwig said.

The average adult man in the United States already consumes about 100 grams of protein daily — twice the previous federal recommendation — according to the AP.
