## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- President Donald Trump's name and image have been installed on federal buildings, proposed for currency, and attached to infrastructure projects in Washington, according to a Guardian editorial published Thursday.
- The Guardian's editorial board compared the proliferation to displays seen in authoritarian regimes and said it overturns "well-advised democratic taboos on glorifying sitting leaders."
- A federal judge last week ordered Trump's name removed from the Kennedy Center, ruling the arts venue could not be renamed without congressional approval.
- Polling cited by the Guardian found 68% of voters said Trump was not focusing enough on problems facing Americans, and only 9% said it was acceptable to name government buildings after him while in office.

Large banners bearing President Donald Trump's image were displayed at the departments of Justice, Labor, and Agriculture in Washington last year, according to the Guardian's editorial board. The administration has also pursued plans for a $250 bill depicting Trump to commemorate the 250th anniversary of independence, though federal law currently prohibits banknotes from depicting living persons, the Guardian noted. Trump's signature is expected to appear on $100 bills — a first for a U.S. president, the newspaper said.

Trump has reportedly offered to release federal infrastructure funds in exchange for renaming Dulles International Airport and Penn Station in his honor, the Guardian reported. He is also pursuing a $1.4 billion White House ballroom renovation project. MSI previously reported that Trump showed reporters the East Wing ballroom construction in May, despite an impasse with Congress over funding for the project.

Not all of the name-and-image initiatives have advanced. A federal judge last week ordered Trump's name removed from the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, saying the venue could not be renamed without congressional approval.

The Guardian's editorial board compared the display of presidential imagery to practices in authoritarian states, citing the ubiquity of Mussolini's face in fascist Italy, Kim Jong-un's portraits in North Korea, and a golden statue of Turkmenistan's leader Gurbanguly Berdymukhamedov. In thriving democracies, the board wrote, such displays are "rightly judged" to be safer for leaders once they are out of power — noting that George Washington refused to appear on currency, believing it reminiscent of European monarchs.

The Guardian's analysis characterized the projects as reflecting both personal vanity and brand-building, noting Trump's history as a reality TV star and his previous product ventures including Trump Steaks and Trump University. Sycophantic attempts at ingratiation by subordinates may spur additional projects, the editorial said.

The editorial described Trump's administration as a "21st-century feudal court where the ambitious compete for approval, the powerful trade favours, and the lines between political authority and personal interest and profit are blurred to the point of invisibility."

Academic research cited by the Guardian questioned the effectiveness of such leader-image campaigns. A study of image displays in the United Arab Emirates did not find evidence that images of leaders increased compliance or support, the newspaper reported. Research on propaganda in China suggested that such displays can backfire, eroding state legitimacy and public satisfaction over the long term.

Public opinion polling cited by the Guardian indicated broad public resistance to the personalization of federal property. A Pew Research Center survey last month found that only 9% of Americans said it was acceptable to name government buildings after Trump while he is in office, with 50% opposed. A Quinnipiac University poll found that 68% of voters said Trump was not focusing enough on problems facing them. The Guardian quoted Trump's declaration that "I don't think about Americans' financial situation" when considering his policy toward Iran.
