Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney responded Thursday to President Donald Trump's assertion that "Canada lives because of the United States," saying instead that Canada "thrives because we are Canadian." Carney made the remark after returning from the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland, where he had delivered a speech condemning coercion by great powers—remarks Trump later said showed insufficient gratitude for American support.

The confrontation underscores rising tensions over trade and sovereignty as the Canada-U.S.-Mexico trade agreement faces mandatory review this year, and Trump continues to advance territorial and policy demands involving North American neighbors.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney on Thursday directly rebutted President Donald Trump's assertion that Canada's existence depends on the United States, saying instead that "Canada thrives because we are Canadian."

The exchange stemmed from remarks Trump made to Carney at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland. Trump told Carney: "Canada lives because of the United States. Remember that, Mark, the next time you make your statements."

Carney had delivered a speech at Davos condemning coercion by great powers against smaller nations—remarks Trump later said showed insufficient gratitude for American economic support.

"Canada doesn't live because of the United States," Carney said upon returning home. "Canada thrives because we are Canadian."

Carney acknowledged that Canada and the United States have built "a remarkable partnership in the areas of economy, security and rich cultural exchange." But he stressed that the relationship was between equals, telling reporters: "We are masters in our home, this is our own country, it's our future, the choice is up to us."

## Trump Retaliates

Trump responded by revoking his invitation to Carney to join a Board of Peace he said would be "the most prestigious Board of Leaders ever assembled, at any time." The Board was created to lead efforts in maintaining a ceasefire between Israel and Hamas. Carney had left Davos before Trump inaugurated the Board.

The prime minister framed his Davos speech as a case for Canada's model of democracy and diversity. Speaking before a cabinet retreat in Quebec City after the forum, Carney said Canada could demonstrate to the world that "the arc of history isn't destined to be warped toward authoritarianism and exclusion; it can still bend toward progress and justice."

"Canada must be a beacon—an example to a world at sea," Carney said, contending that amid "rising populism and ethnic nationalism," Canada shows how "diversity is a strength, not a weakness."

He pointed to Canada's pluralistic character: "There are billions of people who aspire to what we have built: a pluralistic society that works. It's a great country for everyone. It is the greatest country in the world to be a regular person. You don't have to be born rich, or to a landed family. You don't have to be a certain color or worship a certain god."

## Trump's Different Vision

Trump has characterized Canada differently. He said the country receives "freebies" from the United States and should be grateful for them. He also said Canada wants to participate in what he calls "Golden Dome"—a multibillion-dollar missile defense system he said would be operational before his term ends in 2029.

U.S. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick criticized Carney's Davos remarks on Bloomberg Television: "Give me a break. They have the second best deal in the world and all I got to do is listen to this guy whine and complain." Lutnick was referring to the Canada-U.S.-Mexico trade agreement, known as USMCA, which is up for mandatory review this year.

California Gov. Gavin Newsom offered a contrasting view of Carney's speech. Newsom, who heard transcripts of the address from multiple U.S. leaders, said: "I respect what Carney did because he had courage of convictions. He stood up and I think we need to stand up in America and call this out with clarity."

Newsom pointed to Carney's negotiation of a deal with China to introduce low-cost, high-quality electric vehicles into Canada as evidence of independent Canadian policy. "It's a remarkable thing to break down 80-plus years of alliances," Newsom said of Trump's approach to foreign policy, warning: "We can lose our republic as we know it. Our country can become unrecognizable."

## Territory and Trade at Stake

Trump's territorial ambitions extend beyond Canada. He has posted an altered map of the United States that includes Canada, Greenland, Venezuela, and Cuba as U.S. territory, and has made public statements suggesting interest in annexing Greenland and pursuing control of other North American territory.

The USMCA renegotiation, set for this year, will test whether Canada can maintain favorable terms in the face of Trump's more assertive stance toward continental trade relationships.
