The Trump administration redirected the Environmental Protection Agency toward fossil fuel promotion and deregulation in its first year, cutting civil enforcement actions to roughly one-fifth of Biden-era levels and shrinking the agency's workforce by about 20 percent, the Associated Press reported Monday.

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin proposed overturning the agency's foundational finding that climate change poses a threat to human health, pledged what he called "the greatest day of deregulation our nation has seen," and canceled a $20 billion clean energy fund established under Biden's landmark climate law — a move a federal judge later rejected on the merits.

Scientists and public health experts said the overhaul will put more mercury, lead, and fine airborne particles into the environment and that higher greenhouse gas emissions will worsen atmospheric warming driving more frequent, costly, and deadly extreme weather.

As MSI previously reported on the scope of the agency's first-year transformation, the fossil fuel pivot has proceeded across enforcement, staffing, and rulemaking simultaneously. <a href="/articles/2025-12-30-trump-s-epa-cuts-pollution-rules-and-staff-in-fossil-fuel-pivot">Read MSI's earlier coverage of the EPA's pollution rule rollbacks and staff reductions.</a>

## Enforcement and staffing

The number of new civil environmental enforcement actions is roughly one-fifth what it was in the first eight months of the Biden administration, according to the nonprofit Environmental Integrity Project.

EPA spokeswoman Brigit Hirsch said the number of legal filings is not the best way to judge enforcement because proceedings require work outside the agency and can slow compliance. She said the EPA is "focused on efficiently resolving violations and achieving compliance as quickly as possible" and not making demands beyond what the law requires.

Leif Fredrickson, visiting assistant professor of history at the University of Montana, said: "You can effectively do a lot of deregulation if you just don't do enforcement."

Zeldin also reduced EPA staffing by about 20 percent to levels last seen in the mid-1980s. Justin Chen, president of the EPA's largest union, called the cuts "devastating," citing the dismantling of research and development offices at labs across the country and the firing of employees who signed a letter of dissent opposing the reductions.

## Zeldin's targets

Zeldin announced "five pillars" to guide EPA's work; four were economic goals, including energy dominance — the Trump administration's term for expanded fossil fuel output — and boosting the auto industry.

He announced plans to abandon soot pollution rules, loosen rules around harmful refrigerants, limit wetlands protections, and weaken gas mileage requirements. He dismissed staff and canceled billions in grants for environmental justice programs and projects categorized under diversity, equity, and inclusion — both Biden administration priorities.

Much of the new direction aligns with Project 2025, the Heritage Foundation blueprint that argued the agency should cut staffing, reduce regulations, and end what it called a war on coal and other fossil fuels.

Diana Furchtgott-Roth, director of Heritage's Center for Energy, Climate, and Environment, said: "A lot of the regulations that were put on during the Biden administration were more harmful and restrictive than in any other period. So that's why deregulating them looks like EPA is making major changes."

Matthew Tejada, a former EPA official under both the first Trump administration and Biden who now works at the Natural Resources Defense Council, said: "I think it would be hard for them to make it any clearer to polluters in this country that they can go on about their business and not worry about EPA getting in their way."

## Context and criticism

The EPA was launched under President Nixon in 1970 as industrial pollution fouled air and waterways across the country. The agency's aggressiveness has shifted with each administration, but critics from across the political spectrum described the current pivot as unusually sharp.

Historian Douglas Brinkley said the EPA "just constantly wants to pat the fossil fuel business on the back and turn back the clock to a pre-Richard Nixon era" when the agency did not exist.

Christine Todd Whitman, who led the EPA under President George W. Bush and has since joined a centrist third party, said watching Zeldin attack air and water protections has been "just depressing."

"It's tragic for our country. I worry about my grandchildren, of which I have seven. I worry about what their future is going to be if they don't have clean air, if they don't have clean water to drink," Whitman said.

Chris Frey, an EPA official under Biden, said the regulations Zeldin has targeted "offered benefits of avoided premature deaths, of avoided chronic illness … bad things that would not happen because of these rules."

## Zeldin's rationale

Zeldin has argued the EPA can protect the environment and grow the economy simultaneously. He told reporters at EPA headquarters in early December: "We should not be causing … extreme economic pain for an individual or a family" because of policies aimed at "saving the planet."

Many of Zeldin's proposed rollbacks are not yet in effect. It takes time to propose rules, gather public comment, and finalize changes. Enforcement pullbacks and grant cancellations, however, take effect more quickly and have already altered the agency's footprint.

Hirsch framed the agency's approach in contrast to its predecessor: "While the Biden EPA repeatedly attempted to usurp the U.S. Constitution and the rule of law to impose its 'Green New Scam,' the Trump EPA is laser-focused on achieving results for the American people while operating within the limits of the laws passed by Congress."
