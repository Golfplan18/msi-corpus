The standoff places California between competing federal and civil-rights pressures over immigration-status verification in the commercial trucking sector, where immigrant workers account for roughly 20% of the workforce.

California announced Tuesday it will postpone the revocation of approximately 17,000 commercial driver's licenses until March, giving truckers and bus drivers additional time to demonstrate legal eligibility — one week after the Sikh Coalition and Asian Law Caucus filed a class-action lawsuit arguing that migrant commercial drivers were being treated unfairly.

U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy rejected the delay. He said the state faces the loss of $160 million in federal highway funds if it does not meet a January 5 deadline to complete the revocations, and has already withheld $40 million in federal funds from California for what he described as the state's failure to enforce English proficiency requirements for commercial drivers.

As MSI previously reported, California's decision to postpone the license revocations emerged from weeks of escalating federal pressure over the state's commercial driver's license practices. <a href="/articles/2025-12-31-california-delays-revocation-of-17-000-commercial-licenses-after-migrant-lawsuit">Read MSI's initial coverage of the delay.</a>

## Federal pressure and withheld funds

Duffy had pressed California to revoke the licenses after an audit found significant compliance failures: commercial driver's licenses that remained valid long after a driver's immigration permit had expired, and cases in which the state could not demonstrate it had verified a driver's immigration status.

Posting on X, Duffy said California does not have an extension to keep violating the law and putting Americans at risk on the roads.

He had previously threatened to withhold millions in federal funds from California, Pennsylvania, and Minnesota following audits that found similar problems. He withdrew the $160 million threat against California after the state agreed to revoke the permits, but said the delay now restores that risk.

## The lawsuit

The Sikh Coalition, a national civil rights organization, and the Asian Law Caucus, based in San Francisco, filed the class-action suit on behalf of California commercial drivers, contending the enforcement actions subjected migrant truckers to unfair treatment.

Mumeeth Kaur, the Sikh Coalition's legal director, said the delay is "an important step toward mitigating the immediate threat these drivers face to their lives and livelihoods."

A federal court has separately suspended the Department of Transportation's proposed new restrictions that would have more explicitly defined which foreign nationals could obtain commercial driver's licenses.

## Background: the Florida accident

The federal focus on commercial driver licensing escalated after an August incident in Florida in which a trucker without proper authorization to be in the United States made an illegal U-turn and caused a crash that killed three people. A separate fatal crash occurred in California in October. According to the Associated Press, the drivers in both crashes were Sikh.

The Department of Transportation said it has prioritized the issue since the Florida crash. It has also moved against commercial driving schools with questionable licensing practices.

## State response and blocked resumption

California authorities said they are working to satisfy federal officials with reforms already implemented. Steve Gordon, director of the state Department of Motor Vehicles, said commercial drivers are central to the economy, and that supply chains and community connections depend on them.

The state had planned to resume issuing commercial driver's licenses in mid-December, but the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration blocked that resumption.

## Scale of the affected workforce

Immigrant workers represent approximately 20% of all truck drivers in the United States, according to industry figures cited by the AP. Non-resident immigrant permits account for about 5% of all commercial driver's licenses — roughly 200,000 drivers nationwide.

Trucking industry associations have supported removing from the roads drivers who do not meet legal qualifications or English proficiency standards, and praised the Department of Transportation's actions against substandard commercial driving schools.
