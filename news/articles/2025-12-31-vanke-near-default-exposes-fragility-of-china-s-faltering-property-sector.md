HONG KONG — China Vanke, a state-backed homebuilder once ranked as the country's largest property developer by sales, narrowly avoided defaulting on a 2 billion yuan ($284 million) bond last week, the Associated Press reported Wednesday. The developer is also seeking to delay repayment of a separate 3.7 billion yuan ($530 million) of onshore debt that came due Dec. 28, with bondholders agreeing to extend the deadline to February.

The near-default signals that state support for China's major quasi-government-backed property developers may be approaching its limits. Both S&P Global and Fitch Ratings have downgraded Vanke to a form of default status, and analysts warn that a full collapse could spread financial distress across China's wider real estate sector, further dimming prospects for a housing recovery.

MSI reported Wednesday on how <a href="/articles/2025-12-31-china-vanke-skirts-default-as-property-slump-drags-into-2026">Vanke skirted an outright default as China's property slump extends into 2026</a>. The latest reporting from the AP provides additional detail on what the maneuver signals about the limits of government rescue efforts.

## Vanke's financial position

About a third-owned by Shenzhen Metro, a state-owned railway controlled by the Shenzhen city government, Vanke posted a 27% year-on-year revenue decline in the July-September 2025 quarter, according to the AP. Several of its onshore bonds were suspended from trading after prices fell sharply.

Shenzhen Metro Group has provided more than 29 billion yuan ($4 billion) in shareholder loans to Vanke during 2025 to help cover debt repayments, according to S&P Global. Those loans have not bridged the gap: Vanke reported 60 billion yuan ($8 billion) of cash at the end of September 2025 against short-term debts of about 151 billion yuan ($21 billion), Fitch Ratings said.

"This is one of the most significant, quasi state-backed developers that may be defaulting on their repayment," said Foreky Wong, a founding partner at Fortune Ark Restructuring.

S&P Global downgraded Vanke to "selective default," saying it viewed the bond repayment extension as a distressed debt restructuring "tantamount to a default." Fitch Ratings also downgraded the company to "restricted default."

Vanke owes more than $50 billion in total — far less than the more than $300 billion accumulated by China Evergrande before it defaulted in 2021, but large enough that its troubles have unsettled markets. The company still faces more than 9.4 billion yuan of bonds maturing over the next six months, according to S&P, and employed more than 120,000 people as of last year.

## A sector still in the doldrums

More than four years after China's housing downturn began, overall home prices have fallen 20% or more from their 2021 peak, the AP reported. New home sales fell 11.2% by value year-on-year in the first 11 months of 2025, according to official statistics. Property investments fell nearly 16% from a year earlier.

The prolonged slump has caused massive layoffs and weighed on consumer confidence and spending. What was once a sector accounting for more than a quarter of total economic activity in China has become a drag on growth.

"The continued slide in the property market remains one of the most significant risks to China's efforts to shift to a domestically demand-driven growth model," wrote Lynn Song, chief economist for Greater China at ING Bank, in a recent commentary.

Restoring confidence may take years, economists at Morgan Stanley said. Economists at Morningstar said home prices are unlikely to rebound until 2027 because of excess supply, despite repeated pledges by regulators to stabilize the market.

## Contagion risk

Analysts warn that a Vanke default could ripple into the broader real estate sector. Jeff Zhang, an analyst at Morningstar, said a collapse could make it more difficult for non-state-owned developers to obtain financing or government assistance.

"Without a strong commitment by the Shenzhen government on the bailout, we think Vanke's liquidity profile should remain fragile," Zhang said.

Vanke, founded in the 1980s in the southern city of Shenzhen, may be testing the outer limits of Beijing's willingness to prop up distressed developers, analysts said. The question has broad implications: the company had been considered one of China's financially sounder real estate developers before its troubles deepened, and its struggles undercut confidence that the government's support mechanisms can stabilize the sector.
