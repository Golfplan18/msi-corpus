## European leaders held emergency talks on breaking with America

- European leaders held a secret emergency meeting in Brussels in January 2026 to discuss managing a possible breakup with America after President Trump's actions, according to a Wall Street Journal investigation based on interviews with heads of government and classified intelligence assessments.
- French President Emmanuel Macron told the gathering "There is no going back," arguing that Europe's overreliance on the United States was a security risk, as French soldiers were already deployed in Greenland alongside Danish special forces.
- Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, communicating regularly with European leaders, argued that "the old America isn't coming back" and pushed for a coordinated de-Americanization effort that gained momentum after a speech at the Davos forum.
- NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte employed a strategy of public praise and private cajoling to keep Trump committed to the alliance, securing agreement on a 5% of GDP defense spending goal by 2035 at the June NATO summit in The Hague.
- European intelligence agencies offered stark assessments of the Trump administration; one from Southern Europe described it as "a single volatile individual," while Britain's MI6 warned the White House atmosphere resembled "'The Crucible' meets 'Wolf Hall.'"

## Canada's Carney pushed de-Americanization strategy

The emergency meeting, which ran past midnight at the European Council headquarters known as "The Space Egg," lasted five hours with no cameras, recordings, or phones allowed. Nearly 30 leaders vented about the 47th president so emotionally that several later called the session "therapy night," according to multiple leaders present and their senior aides.

Macron opened the discussion, according to several participants, by declaring that Europe had reached a point of no return. For a year, America's closest allies had tried to placate Trump with flattery and concessions on trade and mutual-defense issues. The French president had argued for years that Europe's overreliance on the United States was a security risk, and the deployment of French soldiers to Greenland alongside Danish special forces equipped for potential conflict with American forces underscored the gravity of the moment, the Journal reported.

A clutch of European leaders joined the criticism. The Belgian prime minister complained that Europe risked becoming "a miserable slave" to the United States. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni dissented, telling the roomful of more-liberal leaders that Trump could still be reasoned with, according to people present.

Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen, seated to Meloni's left, appeared shaken after a week of brinkmanship with Trump. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz asked her if she was all right, according to accounts of the meeting.

Several participants mentioned Mark Carney, the newly elected Canadian prime minister, who was not in the room but had been regularly messaging European leaders using a British phone number from his time in London. Carney argued that "the old America isn't coming back," a thesis he had developed after the 2008-09 financial crisis about Western overreliance on a single, increasingly unpredictable country. Spain's prime minister told the room that "Canada is openly saying what we should do," the Journal reported.

Carney's approach contrasted with that of NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte, who tried to salvage the American-led alliance by keeping Trump engaged through sustained public praise. Rutte, whose strategy the European press dubbed "flattery diplomacy," urged allies to give Trump a win by agreeing to a 3.5% GDP defense spending target — later raised to 5% by 2035 after Trump's new NATO ambassador, Matthew Whitaker, arrived in Brussels with the demand. Rutte told holdouts that the headline number was the "win" Trump needed, and that no one would enforce the 10-year goal against fiscally constrained governments.

The flattery strategy showed early signs of fraying. Britain's MI6 assessed that the approach was "subject to the law of diminishing returns." An intelligence report from Southern Europe told leaders: "You are not dealing with an administration that has processes, you are dealing with a single volatile individual." MI6 instructed its staff not to broach the subject of the president with their CIA counterparts, according to the Journal's reporting.

By the time European Council leaders met again in March, Trump's airstrikes on Iran had spiked fuel prices across the continent. Merz was furious, saying Russia would be the only winner of the Middle East's newest war. Several participants began a wry debate over whether a JD Vance presidency would be preferable. Even Meloni conceded she was revising her view, lamenting that Trump "is not reasonable," according to leaders present.

At the NATO summit in The Hague on June 24, Trump landed what the Journal described as a huge foreign-policy win. The alliance agreed on a new defense spending goal, and one after another, the West's most powerful politicians took turns praising Trump in a closed-door session. Carney was more restrained — Trump would see through the praise, his aides reasoned, and think less of them for it. Bulgaria's then-prime minister, Rosen Zhelyazkov, said the forced atmosphere masked deep anxiety: "European leaders still clung to the belief that they could manage Donald Trump through diplomatic flattery and personal charm."

The sole holdout was Spain, whose socialist Prime Minister Sanchez insisted the 5% target was an arbitrary number. After 54 hours of negotiations, Rutte agreed in a letter that Spain could follow "its own sovereign path" and would be assessed in 2029.

European authorities are now quietly removing American technology from their systems, adopting European open-source software and urging civil servants to stop using Microsoft Teams or Office. Governments are spending hundreds of billions of dollars to boost European private space firms, AI companies, and data centers, and running studies on how their American-made weaponry would operate without Washington's authorization. The Journal reported that nations whose empires once spanned the globe are now trying to extricate themselves from what they see as a humbling dependency on American technology and military power, without provoking the United States.

The White House said Trump is looking forward to "constructive and frank conversations" at the NATO summit in Turkey, and White House spokeswoman Anna Kelly said Trump "has effectively restored America's standing on the world stage" and has done more for NATO than anyone else, while also believing that some members should do more to fulfill their obligations.
