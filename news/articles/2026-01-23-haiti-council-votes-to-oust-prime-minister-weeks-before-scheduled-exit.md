Haiti's transitional presidential council voted to fire Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé, council members announced Friday, just two weeks before the panel is scheduled to step down Feb. 7. Council members Edgard Leblanc Fils and Leslie Voltaire made the announcement without elaborating on specific reasons but said the change was necessary because the population was not getting what it needed.

The dismissal threatens to complicate Haiti's fragile transition to elected government and compounds instability in a nation already reeling from gang violence that has killed thousands since the 2021 assassination of President Jovenel Moïse.

The council selected Fils-Aimé for the position in November 2024. Voltaire said the council recognized Fils-Aimé's "capacity and enthusiasm," but that the arrangement was failing to serve the country's needs. Leblanc said the council would identify a replacement within 30 days "to find the way to fully restore security and stability and enter a cycle of development, correct the mistakes of the past and look ahead."

The U.S. State Department opposed the dismissal. In a statement Thursday, the State Department said maintaining Fils-Aimé in office was "integral" to Haiti's efforts to overcome the gangs terrorizing the country. Secretary of State Marco Rubio reaffirmed U.S. support for Fils-Aimé and said that "ongoing gang violence can only be stopped with consistent, strong leadership, with the full support of the Haitian people," according to a State Department statement.

## Haitian Autonomy

Council members framed the decision as an assertion of Haitian autonomy. Voltaire said the council itself had chosen Fils-Aimé—"it wasn't the whites who chose Didier"—and would select his successor without foreign interference. "Everyone is looking for a Haitian solution to the crisis," Voltaire said, "but when we start to find a Haitian solution to the crisis, the international community comes in with all its claws."

## The Security Crisis

Gang violence has surged since Moïse's assassination. Armed groups control an estimated 90% of the capital, Port-au-Prince, and swaths of the country's central region. More than 8,100 killings were reported across Haiti's nearly 11 million people from January to November last year, according to the United Nations, which warned those figures are underreported.

The council faces pressure to hold general elections for the first time in a decade, with tentative dates set for August and December 2026 to elect a president. Leblanc said the council wants political organizations, civil society and the religious sector to decide how Haiti should be governed after Feb. 7.

## Transition Uncertainties

Michael Deibert, author of two books on Haiti, warned that if the council does not step down by Feb. 7, it "could add another element of volatility and uncertainty in the political arena in a country already struggling with the rupture of constitutional order and incredibly severe crises of violence and insecurity."

It was unclear whether Laurent Saint-Cyr, the council's leader, supported the dismissal. Saint-Cyr said earlier in the week that he opposes any push to undermine government stability ahead of Feb. 7.
