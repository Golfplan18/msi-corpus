A Seoul court sentenced former South Korean President Yoon Suk Yeol to five years in prison Friday for charges stemming from his short-lived martial law decree, delivering the first verdict in eight criminal trials against him.

The sentence marks the opening judgment in a sweeping legal reckoning that could yield a far longer term: a separate rebellion trial in which an independent counsel has sought the death penalty is scheduled for a ruling on Feb. 19.

Judge Baek Dae-hyun said in the televised ruling that imposing "a heavy punishment" was necessary because Yoon had shown no remorse and had only repeated "hard-to-comprehend excuses." The judge also cited the need to restore legal systems damaged by Yoon's actions.

Yoon's defense team said they would appeal, describing the ruling as "politicized" and as reflecting "the unilateral arguments by the independent counsel." The defense argued the decision "oversimplified the boundary between the exercise of the president's constitutional powers and criminal liability."

## The charges

The Seoul Central District Court found Yoon guilty of defying attempts to detain him and of fabricating the martial law proclamation. He was also convicted of bypassing a legally mandated full Cabinet meeting, which denied some ministers who were not summoned their right to deliberate on his decree.

## Rebellion trial

The more consequential proceeding is a separate rebellion case for which the Seoul Central District Court is set to rule on Feb. 19. An independent counsel has sought the death penalty in that case.

Park SungBae, a criminal law specialist, said there was little chance the court would impose capital punishment, predicting instead a life sentence or a term of 30 or more years in prison. South Korea has maintained a de facto moratorium on executions since 1997, and courts rarely hand down death sentences. Park said the court would take into account that Yoon's decree caused no casualties and did not last long, though Yoon has not shown genuine remorse.

## Background

On the night of Dec. 3, 2024, Yoon declared martial law in a televised speech, saying he would eliminate "anti-state forces" and protect "the constitutional democratic order." He deployed troops and police to surround the National Assembly, but enough lawmakers were able to enter the chamber and vote down the decree before it could take hold.

The declaration triggered South Korea's most severe political crisis in decades. For many South Koreans it evoked memories of military-backed governments in the 1970s and 1980s that used martial law and emergency decrees to deploy soldiers and tanks against demonstrators. No major violence occurred during Yoon's decree.

Yoon was subsequently impeached, arrested and removed from office. His liberal rival Lee Jae Myung won a snap election and took office last June. Lee has since appointed three independent counsels to investigate Yoon, his wife and their associates.

## Pardon precedent

South Korea has a history of pardoning former presidents jailed over crimes in the name of promoting national unity. Chun Doo-hwan, who received the death penalty at a district court for his 1979 coup and the violent 1980 suppression of pro-democracy protests that killed about 200 people, was among those later pardoned.

Some observers said Yoon may be maintaining a defiant posture in the ongoing trials to retain his support base, on the calculation that a lengthy sentence could eventually be followed by a pardon.

Even if Yoon avoids the death penalty or life imprisonment in the rebellion case, he may still face additional prison terms from the multiple smaller trials he confronts, which include charges that he ordered drone flights over North Korea to manufacture a pretext for martial law and that he manipulated an investigation into a marine's drowning in 2023.
