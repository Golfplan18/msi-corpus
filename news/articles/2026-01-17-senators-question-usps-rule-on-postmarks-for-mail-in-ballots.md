PORTLAND, Ore. — A group of mostly Democratic U.S. senators said Thursday that Postal Service changes could affect the postmark dates used to count mail-in ballots in states that vote entirely or largely by mail. The senators wrote to Postmaster General David Steiner, arguing that updated USPS policy and transportation adjustments could delay postmarks during an election year that will determine control of Congress.

In the letter, the senators said the Postal Service’s updated agency policy indicates that postmarks might reflect the day mail was handled at one of its processing centers rather than the first day the USPS received the mail. They warned that recent USPS consolidations could shift processing centers farther from some communities, increasing the chance that postmarks would arrive late.

The senators wrote that “Postmark delays are especially problematic in states that vote entirely or largely by mail.” They also said many states use postmark dates to determine whether a mail ballot can be counted, adding that “These changes will only increase the likelihood of voter disenfranchisement.”

The lawmakers said the impact could be especially acute in rural areas, where mail must travel farther to reach regional processing centers. They argued that, in theory, “a rural voter could submit their ballot in time according to their state law, but due to the changes you are implementing, their legally-cast ballot would not be counted as it sits in a local post office.”

The senators said they were urging Steiner to restore “timely postmarks” and fully stand up an election mail task force. The lawmakers who signed the letter represented California, Nevada, Oregon, Washington, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Illinois, Maine, Connecticut, New Jersey and Maryland. All are Democrats but one, an independent who typically aligns with the Democratic Party.

A USPS spokesperson, Martha Johnson, said the agency had received the letter and would respond directly to the senators. She said USPS language in the final rule “does not change any existing postal operations or postmarking practices,” and she said the agency looked forward to “clarifying the senators’ misunderstanding.”

USPS’s website, Johnson said, has described adjustments to transportation operations that could affect when mailpieces reach originating processing facilities. The site says the USPS has “made adjustments to our transportation operations” that may mean some mailpieces do not arrive at their originating processing facilities on the same day they are mailed, so “the date on the postmarks applied at our processing facilities” will not necessarily match when a letter carrier collects the mail or when it is dropped off at a retail location.

The agency also said people dropping off mail at a post office can request that a postmark be applied manually to match the mailing date, and that manual postmarks are free of charge. The Postal Service said the “lack of alignment” between mailing date and postmark date will become more common as it implements an initiative to overhaul processing and transportation networks with an emphasis on regional hubs aimed at cutting costs.

In comments referenced by the senators, they said the Postal Service has gotten rid of twice-daily mail dispatches from local post offices to regional processing centers. They said that means mail received after the only transfer truck leaves will sit overnight until the next daily transfer.

Election officials in states that rely heavily on mail voting raised concerns about the change. California Secretary of State Shirley Weber said in a statement that “Not being able to have faith that the Postal Service will mark ballots on the day they are submitted and mail them in a timely manner undermines vote-by-mail voting, in turn undermining California and other elections,” and she said her office will “amplify messaging to voters” to return ballots early if they plan to use the post office.

In Washington state, where election officials said voting is done almost entirely by mail, officials recommended that those who return their ballot within a week of Election Day use a drop box or voting center. They said, “Given the operational and logistical priorities recently set by the USPS, there is no guarantee that ballots returned via mail will be postmarked by the USPS the same day they are mailed.”

The senators’ concerns centered on how postmark timing could interact with state rules for counting ballots and whether USPS operational changes could raise the risk that ballots submitted on time are not counted.
