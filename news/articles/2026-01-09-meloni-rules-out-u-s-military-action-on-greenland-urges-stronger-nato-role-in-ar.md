Italian Premier Giorgia Meloni said Friday she did not believe the United States would resort to military force to seize control of Greenland, declaring that such a move would not be in anyone's interest and would carry serious consequences for the NATO alliance.

"I still don't believe in the possibility of the U.S. initiating military action to take control of Greenland," Meloni said at her traditional New Year press conference in Rome. She added that Italy would not support such a move.

Meloni, widely regarded as one of President Donald Trump's closest European allies, called for a reinforced NATO presence in the Arctic as an alternative path to addressing Washington's security concerns. A stronger alliance role in the region, she said, would help smooth U.S. worries about rival foreign actors gaining influence there.

The White House said earlier in the week that the administration was weighing "options" that could include military action to take control of the island. Greenland is a mineral-rich, strategically located semi-autonomous territory belonging to NATO ally Denmark, whose right to the island the U.S. government recognized at the beginning of the 20th century.

Trump first raised the idea of purchasing Greenland from Denmark during his first term. He renewed calls for U.S. control of the island after recent U.S. military action in Venezuela, citing strategic reasons.

Meloni framed the Trump administration's posture as a signal about Arctic strategic priorities rather than a genuine military threat. "I think the Trump administration, with its somewhat assertive methods, is primarily focusing attention on the strategic importance of Greenland and the Arctic region for its security," she said. "It's an area where many foreign actors operate, and I continue to believe the message from the U.S. is that it will not accept actions by foreign powers."

Six European leaders — from Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Poland and Spain — have defended Greenland's sovereignty alongside Denmark.

Meloni characterized her position as consistent with Italy's standing foreign policy commitments rather than a break with Washington. "I think international law must be fully defended," she said, while pushing back on suggestions that Italy must distance itself from the United States. "I don't understand what you're asking when you say Italy must distance itself from the United States," she said.

She described Italy's foreign policy as resting on two pillars — Europe and the Atlantic Alliance — while acknowledging that alliance relationships carry inherent tensions. "Of course, I don't always agree with everything my allies say. The interests of nations don't always perfectly overlap," Meloni said.
