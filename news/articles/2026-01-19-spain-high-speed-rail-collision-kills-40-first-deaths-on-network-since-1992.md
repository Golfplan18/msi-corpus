The collision struck at Spain's identity as the operator of the largest high-speed rail network in Europe — a system that had logged more than three decades without a fatal accident. Investigators said the cause of the crash remained unknown Monday, and an official investigation could take a month.

At least 40 people died Sunday evening in southern Spain when the rear cars of a high-speed train jumped the track and struck an oncoming train traveling in the opposite direction, regional and national officials said Monday. The crash near Adamuz, in Andalusia, is the first fatal accident on Spain's high-speed rail network since the country opened its first such line in 1992.

The two trains together carried more than 480 passengers. Forty-one survivors remained hospitalized Monday, 12 of them in intensive care, while 81 others had been discharged by late afternoon, Andalusian regional emergency services said. Authorities said all survivors had been rescued by early Monday morning.

"Here at ground zero, when you look at this mass of twisted iron, you see the violence of the impact," said Juanma Moreno, the president of Andalusia. "The impact was so incredibly violent that we have found bodies hundreds of meters away."

## What happened

The crash took place Sunday at 7:45 p.m., according to rail operator Adif. The tail end of a train carrying 289 passengers on the route from Malaga to Madrid went off the rails and slammed into an incoming train traveling from Madrid to Huelva, which was carrying nearly 200 passengers.

The collision knocked the first two carriages of the second train off the track, sending them down a 4-meter (13-foot) slope. Spanish Transport Minister Óscar Puente said the largest number of deaths appeared to occur in those carriages.

Video released by the Civil Guard showed the worst-hit carriages shredded open, with train seats scattered on the gravel beneath the tracks. One carriage lay on its side, bent around a large concrete pillar.

Passengers reported climbing out of smashed windows, some using emergency hammers to break the glass. Francisco Carmona, the firefighter chief of Cordoba, told Onda Cero radio: "There were moments when we had to remove the dead to get to the living."

Authorities opened DNA collection centers in Cordoba, Madrid, Malaga, Huelva and Seville to help identify victims.

## Three days of national mourning

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez declared three days of national mourning. On a visit to Adamuz, the village nearest the crash, Sánchez said: "Today is a day of pain for all of Spain."

Residents of Adamuz helped emergency services manage an influx of injured and distraught passengers overnight, officials said. The town's sports center was turned into a makeshift hospital. The Spanish Red Cross set up a help center nearby.

Rafael Moreno, the mayor of Adamuz, said: "The scene was horrific. It was terrible. People asking and begging for help. Those leaving the wreckage. Images that will always stay in my mind."

Train services between Madrid and Andalusian cities were canceled Monday. Spanish airline Iberia added flights to Seville and Malaga to accommodate stranded travelers, and bus companies reinforced service in the south.

## Cause unknown; investigation under way

The cause of the crash remained unknown Monday. Puente called it "a truly strange" incident — it happened on a flat stretch of track that had been renovated in May, and the train that derailed was less than four years old.

The derailed train belonged to Iryo, the Italian-owned private company that became the first private competitor to Renfe on the high-speed network when it launched service in 2022. Iryo said in a statement that the train was manufactured in 2022 and had passed its most recent safety check on Jan. 15. The other train was operated by Renfe, Spain's public rail company.

Renfe president Álvaro Fernández told Spanish public radio RNE that both trains were traveling below the 250 kph (155 mph) speed limit — one at 205 kph (127 mph), the other at 210 kph (130 mph). Fernández said that "human error could be ruled out" and that the incident "must be related to the moving equipment of Iryo or the infrastructure." Officials have not independently confirmed either assertion, and an investigation could take a month, Puente said.

The Spanish Union of Railway Drivers told the Associated Press that in August it sent a letter to Spain's national railway operator asking it to investigate flaws on train lines across the country and to reduce speeds at certain points until tracks were fully repaired. The union said those recommendations covered high-speed lines, including the one where Sunday's accident occurred.

## Safety record of a flagship network

Spain has more than 3,900 kilometers (2,400 miles) of high-speed track — the largest such network in Europe for trains traveling above 250 kph — according to the International Union of Railways. Renfe said more than 25 million passengers used its high-speed trains in 2024.

Sunday's collision was the first fatal accident on the high-speed network since it opened in 1992. Spain's worst train disaster this century occurred in 2013, when 80 people died after a train derailed in the country's northwest; an investigation concluded the train was traveling 179 kph (111 mph) on a stretch with an 80 kph (50 mph) limit. That stretch was not part of the high-speed network.
