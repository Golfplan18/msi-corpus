President Donald Trump published a private text message from French President Emmanuel Macron on Tuesday, revealing a more deferential diplomatic tone behind closed doors than France has publicly displayed in opposing Trump's push to acquire Greenland. By releasing messages from Macron, NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, and Norway's prime minister, Trump has broken with diplomatic convention and exposed confidential exchanges that typically remain private.

The discrepancy between private and public diplomatic language underscores the complex calculation European leaders face in responding to Trump's unconventional approach to international relations, where personal communications are treated as fodder for public disclosure.

## Macron's Softer Tone

Macron began his message with "My friend" and emphasized common ground before broaching the disputed territory. "We are totally in line on Syria. We can do great things on Iran," he wrote.

Only then did Macron address the central dispute. "I do not understand what you are doing on Greenland," he wrote, immediately followed by: "Let us try to build great things." That was his only mention of Greenland in the two sections Trump published.

## Breaking Diplomatic Silence

Trump has made a practice of releasing private communications that world leaders typically keep confidential. The Norwegian government released a message Trump sent to Norway's prime minister, which the White House confirmed as authentic. In it, Trump linked his Greenland demands to Norway's decision not to award him the Nobel Peace Prize.

"Considering your Country decided not to give me the Nobel Peace Prize for having stopped 8 Wars PLUS, I no longer feel an obligation to think purely of Peace," Trump wrote. He concluded: "The World is not secure unless we have Complete and Total Control of Greenland."

NATO confirmed the authenticity of a message Trump published from Secretary General Mark Rutte. "I am committed to finding a way forward on Greenland. Can't wait to see you. Yours, Mark," Rutte wrote.

## The Private-Public Divide

World leaders' private messages to each other rarely become public verbatim, enabling them to project different positions in private than they voice publicly. Trump, breaking with diplomatic tradition, has exposed these confidential exchanges.

Macron's office confirmed that the message Trump published was genuine. The French leader said Tuesday he had "no particular reaction" when asked about the publication. "I take responsibility for everything that I do. It's my habit to be coherent between what I say on the outside and what I do in a private manner," he said.

## The Paris Meeting and Peace Board

In his message, Macron proposed hosting representatives from both Ukraine and Russia at a meeting in Paris later that week—an idea he had not mentioned publicly. The Russians could be hosted "in the margins" of the gathering, Macron suggested.

The meeting could also include "the danish, the syrians" and the G7 nations, Macron wrote. He proposed: "let us have a dinner together in Paris together on thursday before you go back to the us."

Trump bristled Monday when told Macron has no plans to join his Board of Peace on Gaza despite an invitation. "Well, nobody wants him because he's going to be out of office very soon," Trump told reporters.

Macron, however, has more than a year left in office before his second and final term ends in 2027.

Trump responded with a threat. "I'll put a 200% tariff on his wines and champagnes and he'll join," he said.
