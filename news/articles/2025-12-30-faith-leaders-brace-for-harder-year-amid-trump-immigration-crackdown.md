Religious leaders from Catholic, evangelical, Lutheran, Jewish, and Muslim traditions said they expect 2026 to bring worsening conditions for the immigrant communities they serve, as enforcement actions and rhetoric from the Trump administration intensify. Communities of Haitian, Afghan, and Somali immigrants face specific policy threats — including the February expiration of Temporary Protected Status for thousands of Haitians and the suspension of the federal refugee resettlement program, which took effect on President Donald Trump's first day in office for his second term.

The warnings come as faith-based organizations serving refugees absorb significant funding losses tied to the refugee program suspension, and as Haitian and Somali community leaders describe an atmosphere of fear that has already altered daily life in cities including Springfield, Ohio, and the Twin Cities.

As [MSI previously reported](/articles/2025-12-30-faith-leaders-supporting-immigrants-brace-for-harder-year-under-trump-policies), faith leaders supporting immigrant communities have been tracking these pressures throughout 2025, with multiple religious traditions signaling they will expand support rather than withdraw it.

## Haitians in limbo in Springfield

More than 15,000 Haitians live and work in Springfield, Ohio — a city of about 59,000 — most having arrived in recent years under the Temporary Protected Status program. That program expires in early February, and the Rev. Carl Ruby, pastor of Central Christian Church and one of several Springfield clergy supporting the Haitian community, said the consequences could be severe.

"It's going to be an economic and humanitarian disaster," Ruby said.

Ruby and Viles Dorsainvil, a Haitian community leader in Springfield, traveled to Washington to seek help from members of Congress. Ruby said the response offered little encouragement.

"Every single legislator we've talked to has said nothing is going to happen legislatively. Trump's rhetoric keeps getting harsher," Ruby said. "It just doesn't feel like anything is going our way."

Many Haitians in Springfield fear for their lives if they are returned to their homeland, which has been plagued by gang violence. In 2024, Trump falsely accused Haitians in Springfield of eating their neighbors' cats and dogs, according to the Associated Press — a claim that deepened fear in the community. Ruby said churches are responding by expanding, not contracting, their commitments.

"There are more and more churches in Springfield saying we will provide sanctuary," he said. "We will do whatever it takes to protect our members."

## Afghan refugees face tightened restrictions

The suspension of the federal refugee resettlement program has had immediate financial consequences for faith-based organizations. Lutheran Social Services of the National Capital Area, which serves the Washington, D.C., region, lost 68% of its budget this year and laid off two-thirds of its staff — shrinking from nearly 300 employees to 100, according to CEO Kristyn Peck. Many of the organization's employees and clients are Afghans who assisted U.S. forces before the Taliban's 2021 takeover of the Afghan government.

The Trump administration announced additional immigration restrictions following the November 26 shooting of two National Guard members in Washington, in which an Afghan national became the suspect. Peck said the incident was used to justify sweeping action against the broader Afghan community.

"A whole group of people have now been targeted and blamed for this senseless act of violence," she said.

Peck said volunteers have stepped in to provide services previously funded by the resettlement program, including English-language and job-skills training for Afghan women.

"We continue to do the good work," Peck said. "Even in challenging moments, we just continue to see people putting their faith into action."

World Relief, a Christian humanitarian organization overseen by the National Association of Evangelicals, has joined the criticism. CEO Myal Greene said the administration's decision to halt processing of all Afghan applicants because of one person's alleged actions puts tens of thousands of Afghan allies at risk.

"When President Trump announces his intention to 'permanently halt' all migration from 'Third World countries,' he's insulting the majority of the global Church," Greene said. "When his administration halts processing for all Afghans on account of the evil actions of one person, he risks abandoning tens of thousands of others who risked their lives alongside the U.S. military."

## Somali Americans establish task force in Minnesota

In mid-December, imams and other leaders of Minnesota's Somali community established a task force to address the fallout from fraud scandals, a surge in immigration enforcement, and Trump's use of the word "garbage" to describe Somali Americans — the largest Somali refugee population in the United States.

Imam Yusuf Abdulle, who directs the Islamic Association of North America — a network of more than three dozen mostly East African mosques, about half of them in Minnesota — said the community has faced unjust generalization.

"We're not minimizing the crime, but we're amplifying the successes," Abdulle said. "For the number of years here, Somali is a very resilient, very successful community."

Abdulle said that even Somalis in Minnesota who are U.S. citizens or lawfully present have deserted local businesses and mosques when immigration enforcement surged. Community leader Abdullahi Farah said the task force is focused on both addressing immediate fears and building advocacy capacity ahead of the 2026 midterm elections.

"Every election year the rhetoric goes up. And so we want to push back against these hateful rhetorics, but also bring our community together," Farah said.

## Bishops, denominations, and Jewish groups respond

In mid-November, U.S. Catholic bishops voted to issue a "special message" — the first time in 12 years the bishops had invoked that collective mechanism — decrying developments causing fear and anxiety among immigrants.

"We are concerned about the conditions in detention centers and the lack of access to pastoral care," the bishops said in the message. "We oppose the indiscriminate mass deportation of people. We pray for an end to dehumanizing rhetoric and violence, whether directed at immigrants or at law enforcement."

Bishop Daniel Garcia of Austin, Texas, chair of the Catholic bishops conference's subcommittee on racial justice, responded directly to Trump's remarks targeting Somali Americans.

"Each child of God has value and dignity," Garcia said. "Language that denigrates a person or community based on his or her ethnicity or country of origin is incompatible with this truth."

The presiding bishop of the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America, Yehiel Curry, issued a pastoral message thanking ELCA congregations for supporting immigrants amid what he described as "aggressive and indiscriminate immigration enforcement."

"The racial profiling and harm to our immigrant neighbors show no signs of diminishing, so we will heed God's call to show up alongside these neighbors," Curry wrote.

HIAS, an international Jewish nonprofit serving refugees and asylum-seekers, condemned recent Trump administration moves and expressed solidarity with targeted communities.

"As a Jewish organization, we also know all too well what it means for an entire community to be targeted because of the actions of one person," HIAS said in a statement. "We will always stand in solidarity with people seeking the opportunity to rebuild their lives in safety, including those being targeted now by harmful policies and hateful rhetoric in the Afghan American and Somali American communities."
