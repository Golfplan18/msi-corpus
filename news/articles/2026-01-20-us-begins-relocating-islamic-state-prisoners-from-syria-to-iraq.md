The United States military has begun transferring members of the Islamic State group held in northeast Syria to Iraqi detention facilities as Syrian government forces have taken control of major detention centers in the region. About 150 detainees have already been moved, according to U.S. Central Command, with up to 7,000 more potentially being transferred.

The transfers mark a significant shift in Syria's detention landscape following Syrian President Ahmad al-Sharaa's military consolidation of Kurdish-held areas, raising questions about the security and management of facilities holding thousands of IS members and their families.

The transfer process began Wednesday, with U.S. Central Command indicating the operation is ongoing. An Iraqi intelligence official told The Associated Press that 144 detainees arrived in Iraq on Wednesday night and will be held in specially designated secure units.

The transferred IS members come from multiple nationalities. An Iraqi intelligence general said the group includes approximately 240 Tunisians and fighters from countries including Tajikistan and Kazakhstan.

"They will be interrogated and then put on trial. All of them are commanders in IS and are considered highly dangerous," the Iraqi general said on condition of anonymity because he was not authorized to speak with the media.

## Why the transfers are happening

As Syrian government forces under President Ahmad al-Sharaa have consolidated control over areas previously held by the U.S.-backed Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces, or SDF, the military shift has brought Syrian troops close to multiple detention centers holding IS members and their families.

On Monday, about 120 inmates escaped from Shaddadeh prison near the Iraqi border. Syrian authorities said most have been recaptured. The prison holds about 9,000 IS members in total. Forces loyal to al-Sharaa are now also close to the Roj camp and al-Aqtan prison in Raqqa province, which Syrian troops have encircled.

## The detention landscape

An estimated 9,000 Islamic State fighters remain in detention facilities across northeast Syria, according to a U.S. State Department report. The detainee population includes approximately 1,600 Iraqis and 1,800 fighters from countries outside Syria and Iraq.

The largest facility is Gweiran prison, now called Panorama, which holds about 4,500 IS-linked detainees in Hassakeh. Other detention facilities include Cherkin prison in Qamishli, Derik prison near the border with Iraq and Turkey, and al-Aqtan prison near Raqqa.

The al-Hol camp, where Syrian troops are now in full control, houses tens of thousands of women and children linked to IS. The camp held around 73,000 people at its peak in 2019, but the population has declined to approximately 24,000 as some countries have repatriated citizens. The facility has been a focus of international concern, with documented violence carried out by IS sleeper cells against women who were distancing themselves from the group.

The Roj camp, located where the borders of Syria, Turkey, and Iraq converge, holds about 2,500 people, including British national Shamima Begum, who traveled from the United Kingdom as a teenager to join IS nearly 11 years ago.

## Iraqi detention system challenges

An Iraqi intelligence official acknowledged a challenge in securing adequate detention facilities in Iraq but said specific centers have been designated to hold the transferred detainees in special secure units. In previous years, 3,194 Iraqi detainees and 47 French citizens have been transferred to Iraq.

Syria's government joined the U.S.-led international coalition fighting IS in late 2025. Syrian authorities have said they are ready to take over and manage the camps and prisons, vowing commitment to fighting extremists.

## IS history and threat

When IS declared a caliphate across large parts of Syria and Iraq in 2014, the group attracted extremists from around the world. From their territory, IS carried out attacks that left hundreds dead from Europe to Asia to Arab countries. The group also enslaved thousands of Yazidi women and girls in its 2014 campaign in northern Iraq and committed documented atrocities including mass executions and sexual slavery.

IS was defeated militarily in Syria in March 2019. However, the group's sleeper cells continue to carry out deadly attacks in the region. The possible escape of detainees raises international concerns that they could rejoin IS networks that remain active.
