LISBON — A record 11 candidates launched Portugal's presidential campaign on Sunday, opening a two-week sprint to the January 18 vote. The broad field makes it nearly certain that no candidate will clear the 50-percent threshold required to win outright, pointing toward a runoff ballot on February 8. Almost 11 million voters are eligible to cast ballots.

The next president — largely a figurehead with no executive power but holding the tools to veto legislation and dissolve Parliament to call snap elections — will take office amid unresolved disputes over citizenship law, labor rights, and end-of-life care.

## The leading candidates

Opinion polls place four candidates at the front of a crowded pack. Luís Marques Mendes, representing the center-right Social Democratic Party currently in government, and António José Seguro of the center-left Socialist Party — parties that have alternated in power for the past 50 years — are both regarded as frontrunners, according to the Associated Press.

They are expected to face strong challenges from André Ventura, leader of the populist Chega party, and Henrique Gouveia e Melo, a retired rear admiral running as an independent. Chega's surge in support made it the second-largest party in Portugal's Parliament last year. Gouveia e Melo gained wide public recognition for overseeing Portugal's swift COVID-19 vaccine rollout during the pandemic.

## The president's role

Portugal's head of state sits largely above the political fray, refereeing disputes and seeking to defuse tensions rather than setting executive policy. The office nonetheless carries significant levers: the ability to veto legislation passed by Parliament — though the veto can be overturned — and the power to dissolve Parliament and call snap elections.

Portugal held its third general election in three years in May — what the Associated Press described as its worst spell of political instability in decades. The next president is expected to use the office's mediating role to encourage compromise between parties.

## Key decisions ahead

Whoever wins will face a stack of contested legislation. Portugal's Constitutional Court struck down a proposed citizenship bill last month — a measure setting limits on who can obtain Portuguese citizenship and under what conditions it can be revoked — returning the bill to Parliament for revision.

A government labor-reform package that has already brought street protests and a major strike will also land on the president's desk. A euthanasia and physician-assisted suicide law that Parliament approved in 2022 but has been held up by constitutional objections remains unresolved.
