Iran's currency fell to a record low against the dollar on Sunday, setting off the country's largest street protests in three years by Monday and prompting the resignation of Central Bank Governor Mohammad Reza Farzin, the Associated Press reported.

Demonstrations erupted along Saadi Street in central Tehran and in the Shush neighborhood near the Grand Bazaar, while witnesses told the AP that similar protests broke out in Isfahan, Shiraz, and Mashhad. Police used tear gas to disperse crowds in some parts of the capital.

The unrest comes as Iranians contend with a December inflation rate of 42.2 percent year-over-year — with food prices up 72 percent and medical costs up 50 percent, according to Iran's state statistics center — pressures compounded by a recent fuel price increase and reports of planned tax hikes.

The protests were the largest since 2022, when Mahsa Jina Amini, a 22-year-old woman, died in police custody after morality police arrested her for allegedly wearing her hijab improperly, the AP reported. Her death triggered nationwide demonstrations.

## Currency collapse and resignation

The rial fell to 1.42 million per dollar on Sunday before recovering slightly to trade at 1.38 million per dollar on Monday, the AP reported. When Farzin took office in 2022, the rial stood at approximately 430,000 per dollar. State television confirmed his resignation Monday. Reports of a possible resignation had circulated in Iranian media for the preceding week.

Merchants closed their shops on Monday and urged others to follow suit, according to witnesses who spoke to the AP. The semi-official ILNA news agency reported that many businesses stopped selling, though some remained open. The official IRNA news agency confirmed the protests.

Shopkeepers at the Grand Bazaar — whose merchants played a central role in the 1979 Islamic Revolution that ended the monarchy and brought Islamists to power — were among those who shuttered their stalls, according to the AP.

## Economic pressures on households

Iran's state statistics center reported that December inflation rose to 42.2 percent compared with the same period last year, up 1.8 percentage points from November. Food prices climbed 72 percent and health and medical costs rose 50 percent over the same period, the center reported. Several critics described the figures as signs of impending hyperinflation, according to the AP.

The rial's rapid depreciation is worsening pressure on household budgets, with a recent change in fuel prices adding further strain, the AP reported. Iranian state media reports of planned tax increases in the new Iranian year beginning March 21 have added to public anxiety, the AP reported.

## Sanctions and geopolitical backdrop

The rial traded at 32,000 per dollar at the time of the 2015 nuclear deal, which lifted international sanctions on Iran in exchange for strict limits on its nuclear program. That agreement collapsed after President Donald Trump withdrew the United States from it in 2018.

The United Nations reimposed nuclear-related sanctions on Iran in September 2025, freezing Iranian assets abroad, halting arms transactions with Tehran, and imposing sanctions tied to Iran's ballistic missile program, the AP reported. The measures followed a 12-day war between Iran and Israel in June 2025. The AP reported that many Iranians fear the possibility of a broader confrontation involving the United States, adding to market anxiety.
