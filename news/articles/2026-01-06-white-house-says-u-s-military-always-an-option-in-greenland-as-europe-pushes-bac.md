The White House said Tuesday that the U.S. military remains "always an option" as President Donald Trump pursues Greenland, even as the leaders of six European nations joined Denmark in rejecting any American takeover of the self-governing Arctic island.

White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt said in a statement that Trump and his team were "discussing a range of options" to acquire the world's largest island. "President Trump has made it well known that acquiring Greenland is a national security priority of the United States, and it's vital to deter our adversaries in the Arctic region," Leavitt said. "The president and his team are discussing a range of options to pursue this important foreign policy goal, and of course, utilizing the U.S. military is always an option at the commander in chief's disposal."

The statement sharpened an international dispute that has rattled NATO allies and prompted Denmark to warn that a U.S. military move against Greenland would effectively end the Western security alliance that has underpinned European security since World War II.

## European and Canadian rejection

The leaders of France, Germany, Italy, Poland, Spain and the United Kingdom joined Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen in issuing a joint statement Tuesday reaffirming that Greenland "belongs to its people." Greenland is a self-governing territory of Denmark and, by that status, a member of the NATO alliance.

"It is for Denmark and Greenland, and them only, to decide on matters concerning Denmark and Greenland," the statement said.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney also expressed support for Denmark at a meeting with Frederiksen in Paris. Carney announced a visit to Greenland by Canada's Governor General Mary Simon, who is of Inuit descent, and Foreign Minister Anita Anand.

"The future of Greenland and Denmark are decided solely by the people of Denmark," Carney said.

## Divisions within the U.S.

Not all American officials aligned with the White House position. House Speaker Mike Johnson, a Republican, said Tuesday that he did not consider military action appropriate when asked whether he was comfortable with it. "No. I don't think it's appropriate," Johnson said.

Trump's special envoy to Greenland, Louisiana Gov. Jeff Landry, whom Trump appointed last month, said he had no interest in speaking with Danish or European officials, who he said had "absolutely made their position clear." Landry said he wanted instead to speak directly with Greenlanders.

"I want to talk to people who want an opportunity to improve the quality of life in Greenland," Landry said on a Fox News radio program. He described his outreach as "culinary diplomacy," saying Greenlanders who had emailed him told him they liked to hunt and fish.

White House deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller said Monday that Greenland should come under U.S. control, questioning Denmark's claim to the island. "The president has been clear for months now that the United States should be the nation that has Greenland as part of our overall security apparatus," Miller said on CNN. He also raised the question of Denmark's standing: "What is the basis of their territorial claim. What is their basis of having Greenland as a colony of Denmark?"

Miller said Monday that military action was not necessary to accomplish the goal. "There is no need to even think or talk about this in the context that you are asking, of a military operation. Nobody is going to fight the U.S. militarily over the future of Greenland," Miller said.

## Danish warning and Greenland's response

Frederiksen said Monday that Trump's comments about Greenland "should be taken seriously." She warned that U.S. military action against the island would have sweeping consequences.

"If the United States chooses to attack another NATO country militarily, then everything stops," Frederiksen told Danish broadcaster TV2.

Greenland Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen said his government was not anticipating an imminent takeover but emphasized the importance of maintaining good relations with Washington. "We are not in a situation where we think that there might be a takeover of the country overnight and that is why we are insisting that we want good cooperation," Nielsen said.

Bipartisan members of Congress also urged restraint. Representatives Steny H. Hoyer and Blake Moore, co-chairs of the Congressional Friends of Denmark Caucus, said in a joint statement that "sabre-rattling about annexing Greenland is needlessly dangerous." They noted that Greenland's membership in NATO meant a U.S. attack would carry significant consequences: "An attack on Greenland — a crucial part of that alliance — would tragically be an attack on NATO."

The lawmakers also said existing arrangements already served U.S. interests. "We already have access to everything we could need from Greenland," they said, noting Denmark had previously authorized the U.S. to deploy more forces or build additional missile defense infrastructure on the island.

## Strategic stakes

Trump has argued that the U.S. needs control of Greenland to counter Chinese and Russian activity in the Arctic. "Greenland is covered with Russian and Chinese ships all over the place," Trump told reporters Sunday. "We need Greenland from the standpoint of national security, and Denmark is not going to be able to do it."

Ulrik Pram Gad, a global security expert at the Danish Institute for International Studies, disputed that characterization. "There are indeed Russian and Chinese ships in the Arctic, but these vessels are too far away to see from Greenland with or without binoculars," Gad wrote.

The U.S. Department of Defense already operates Pituffik Space Base in northwestern Greenland under a 1951 defense agreement with Denmark, supporting missile warning, missile defense and space surveillance operations for the U.S. and NATO. Greenland also guards part of the GIUK Gap — the Greenland-Iceland-United Kingdom corridor — where NATO monitors Russian naval movements in the North Atlantic.

Greenland holds large deposits of rare earth minerals used in electronics, batteries and clean energy technologies. The U.S. Geological Survey has also identified potential offshore oil and natural gas deposits.

Greenland has been part of the Danish kingdom for hundreds of years, becoming an integral part of Denmark in 1953. Legislation in 2009 extended self-government to the island and recognized a right to independence under international law, an option favored by a majority of Greenlanders.

The dispute began escalating over the weekend after Katie Miller — a former Trump administration official and wife of Stephen Miller — posted an illustrated map of Greenland in the colors of the American flag on social media, accompanied by the caption: "SOON." Trump added to the speculation Sunday when he told reporters, "let's talk about Greenland in 20 days."
