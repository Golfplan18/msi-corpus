Syria's new government and the Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces signed a ceasefire Sunday, with President Ahmad al-Sharaa and SDF commander Mazloum Abdi agreeing to terms that would dissolve the armed Kurdish force and cede its northeastern territories to Damascus. The agreement was announced while government forces swept into the city of Raqqa, which the SDF had controlled since capturing it from the Islamic State in 2017.

Abdi confirmed the deal in a video statement, saying the group had accepted the agreement "to stop the bloodshed." Al-Sharaa signed the document before journalists in Damascus. "It's a victory for all Syrians of all backgrounds," al-Sharaa said. "Hopefully Syria will end its state of division and move to a state of unity and progress."

The deal marks the most significant assertion of authority by Syria's post-Assad leadership since it toppled Bashar Assad in December 2024, ending the SDF's decade-long control of the northeast and setting the stage for the country's first unified government in more than 14 years of civil war.

## Terms of the agreement

Under the agreement, the SDF would surrender Raqqa and Deir el-Zour provinces — both Arab-majority areas — along with their oil and gas fields, river dams along the Euphrates, and border crossings. SDF forces are to be disbanded and integrated into Syria's military and security forces, while senior military and civilian officials would receive high-ranking positions in state institutions.

Hassakah Province, the heartland of Syria's Kurdish population, is expected only to return its civilian administration to Damascus. The Kurdish-led agencies managing prisons and sprawling camps holding thousands of detained Islamic State fighters and their families would also be handed over to central government control.

Al-Sharaa told journalists the agreement would be implemented gradually, beginning with the cessation of hostilities. No clear timeline was announced. Abdi said he would explain the full terms to his people in the coming days and had been scheduled to meet al-Sharaa in Damascus, but al-Sharaa said Abdi could not travel due to bad weather and would visit Monday.

## Raqqa falls; Kurdish families flee

An AP reporter in the area said large military convoys swept into Raqqa city Sunday evening and were greeted by residents. Crowds celebrated in the streets late into the evening, waving Syrian flags and setting off fireworks, with some firing into the air.

"Today, everyone is born anew," said Yahya Al Ahmad, who was among the revelers.

Several thousand Kurdish families who had been living in areas captured by government forces fled to the SDF-controlled city of Qamishli during the offensive. Many had been displaced multiple times during Syria's 14-year civil war and were living in tent camps. A cultural center in Qamishli was converted into a temporary shelter.

Residents of Qamishli expressed mixed reactions to the deal. Goran Ibrahim, a doctor, said: "The Kurds have become victims of international agreements and international deception." He added that "with regards to this agreement, the positive part is the end of the fighting between Arabs and Kurds in the region."

## U.S. envoy involved in negotiations

U.S. Envoy Tom Barrack met with al-Sharaa earlier Sunday as government forces moved into Raqqa. Abdi reportedly joined the meeting by phone. Barrack praised the deal, saying it would lead to "renewed dialogue and cooperation toward a unified Syria."

"This agreement and ceasefire represent a pivotal inflection point, where former adversaries embrace partnership over division," Barrack said in a post on X.

Syria's ambassador to the United Nations, Ibrahim Olabi, told the AP: "Really this is now a moment to show that Syrians are able to put differences aside and move ahead…It's a victory for Syria."

The two warring sides are both key U.S. allies. The SDF was a principal American partner in the ground campaign that defeated Islamic State's self-declared caliphate in Syria; Syria's new government has sought to normalize relations with Washington since taking power.

## How the confrontation escalated

Syria's new leadership had struggled to extend its authority over the northeast after taking power in December 2024. An agreement reached in March to merge the SDF with Damascus did not gain traction, with both sides accusing each other of violations.

The current confrontation began earlier in January with clashes along a tense front line in eastern Aleppo province. After initial fighting, Abdi announced his forces would withdraw east of the Euphrates River, and al-Sharaa issued a presidential decree strengthening Kurdish rights. The withdrawal appeared at first to be proceeding as planned.

A senior Syrian government official, speaking on condition of anonymity because he was not authorized to speak publicly, said government forces advanced further into SDF territory because the SDF, despite agreeing to withdraw east of the Euphrates by 7 a.m., did not do so. New clashes broke out and the Syrian military seized Tabqa, then pressed into Raqqa province. Armed Arab clans in Raqqa and Deir el-Zour that largely do not support the SDF backed Damascus during the push. By Sunday evening, the SDF had lost control of large swaths of its territory and infrastructure, including dams and oil and gas fields.

## Disputed allegations from Tabqa

The Syrian government accused Kurdish-led forces of executing prisoners in Tabqa before withdrawing from the area. The SDF denied the allegations, saying it had transferred the detainees out of the prison and counter-accused government forces of firing at the facility. The competing claims were unresolved as of Sunday evening.
