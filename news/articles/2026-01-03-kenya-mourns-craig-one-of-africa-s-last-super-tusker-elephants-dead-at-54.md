Craig's death marks the loss of one of a dwindling class of bulls whose tusks grow massive enough to scrape the ground as they walk — a trait that once made such elephants prime targets for poachers. Kenya's overall elephant population has grown from 36,280 in 2021 to 42,072 in 2025, a gain conservationists credit to sustained protection efforts.

Craig, a 54-year-old bull elephant celebrated as one of Africa's last "super tuskers," died of natural causes Saturday in Kenya's Amboseli National Park, the Kenya Wildlife Service announced. The animal's decades in the wild had made him a symbol of Kenya's increasingly successful efforts to protect elephants from ivory poachers — and a patient, sought-after fixture for safari tourists drawn to his ground-sweeping tusks.

"Craig, the legendary super tusker famed for its immense, ground-sweeping tusks and calm, dignified presence, passed on at the age of 54," the wildlife service said in a statement.

The Amboseli Trust for Elephants, the conservation group that monitored Craig throughout his life, said he had died of natural causes. The group said it was grateful to everyone who worked to help the animal "live out his life naturally."

Kenya's national broadcaster NTV described Craig as "one of the last remaining elephants identified as super tuskers in Africa."

## What defines a super tusker

The term describes a bull elephant with tusks weighing over 45 kilograms each — so long and heavy that they scrape the ground as the animal walks, according to the Tsavo Trust, a Kenyan conservation nonprofit. Female elephants that grow unusually long tusks are called iconic cows.

Craig lived in Amboseli National Park, a protected area in southern Kenya whose terrain ranges from savannah woodland to open grasslands near the Tanzania border. He was known for calm behavior. The wildlife service said he was "often pausing patiently as visitors photographed and filmed him."

In 2021, beer maker East African Breweries adopted Craig through its Tusker brand, reflecting his prominence and an ongoing collaboration between conservation groups and Kenya's commercial sector.

## Kenya's conservation record

The wildlife service said Craig "fathered a number of calves, ensuring that his powerful bloodline and gentle character live on across generations."

Kenya's overall elephant population has grown significantly in recent years, rising from 36,280 in 2021 to 42,072 in 2025, according to the latest official figures. That growth has posed its own logistical challenges: in the Mwea National Reserve, east of Nairobi, the local elephant population expanded to the point of overwhelming the ecosystem, requiring the relocation of about 100 elephants in 2024.

Kenya's national parks and reserves attract millions of visitors annually, making wildlife protection both a conservation priority and a foundation of the country's tourism economy.
