The Temporary Protected Status program shielding many Haitian immigrants from deportation was set to expire in early February. Trump suspended the U.S. refugee program on the first day of his second term, cutting off federal funding to hundreds of faith-based resettlement organizations and forcing several to eliminate most of their staff.

Religious leaders across the United States who minister to Haitian, Afghan, and Somali immigrant communities said in late December 2025 that the year ahead could be worse than the one just ending, as tightened immigration enforcement and the approaching expiration of a key legal-protection program threatened the status of hundreds of thousands of people.

The Temporary Protected Status program allowing many Haitians to remain legally in the country was set to expire in early February. Trump suspended the U.S. refugee program on the first day of his second term, a move that severed federal funding to hundreds of faith-based resettlement organizations.

"It's going to be an economic and humanitarian disaster," said the Rev. Carl Ruby, pastor of Central Christian Church in Springfield, Ohio.

## Haitians in Springfield face legal limbo

More than 15,000 Haitians live and work in Springfield, a city of about 59,000 in Ohio, many of them having settled there in recent years under the Temporary Protected Status program. In 2024, the Associated Press reported, Trump falsely accused Haitians in Springfield of eating their neighbors' cats and dogs.

Ruby said he and Viles Dorsainvil, a leader of Springfield's Haitian community, had traveled to Washington to seek help from members of Congress but found no legislative path forward.

"Every single legislator we've talked to has said nothing is going to happen legislatively. Trump's rhetoric keeps getting harsher," Ruby said. "It just doesn't feel like anything is going our way."

Ruby said the pressure had deepened rather than diminished the commitment of Springfield churches to the Haitian community.

"It's increasing our resolve to oppose this," he said. "There are more and more churches in Springfield saying we will provide sanctuary. … We will do whatever it takes to protect our members."

Many Haitians in Springfield and across the country fled gang violence in Haiti and fear returning.

## Afghan resettlement organizations shed staff

The suspension of the refugee program struck faith-based resettlement groups hard. Lutheran Social Services of the National Capital Area, which serves the Washington, D.C., region, lost 68% of its budget and laid off two-thirds of its staff, shrinking from nearly 300 employees to 100, according to CEO Kristyn Peck. Many of the organization's employees and close to two-thirds of its clients are Afghans who worked alongside the U.S. military and fled after the Taliban's 2021 takeover of a U.S.-backed government.

The Trump administration announced new immigration restrictions following the alleged Nov. 26 shooting of two National Guard members in Washington by an Afghan national.

"A whole group of people have now been targeted and blamed for this senseless act of violence," Peck said.

World Relief, a global Christian humanitarian organization overseen by the National Association of Evangelicals, joined other religious groups in objecting to restrictions on Afghan immigrants.

"When President Trump announces his intention to 'permanently halt' all migration from 'Third World countries,' he's insulting the majority of the global Church," said World Relief CEO Myal Greene. "When his administration halts processing for all Afghans on account of the evil actions of one person, he risks abandoning tens of thousands of others who risked their lives alongside the U.S. military."

Peck said volunteers had stepped in to provide services that laid-off staff could no longer fund, including an English-language and job-skills training program for Afghan women.

## Somali community in Minnesota forms task force

In mid-December, imams and other leaders of Minnesota's Somali community established a task force to address the fallout from fraud scandals, a surge in immigration enforcement, and Trump's public statements about Somali Americans. Trump had referred to them as "garbage."

Imam Yusuf Abdulle, who directs the Islamic Association of North America — a network of more than three dozen mostly East African mosques, about half of them in Minnesota — said the task force would first address fear within the community, then increase advocacy ahead of the 2026 midterm elections.

"We're not minimizing the crime, but we're amplifying the successes," Abdulle said.

Minnesota has been home to growing numbers of Somali refugees since the late 1990s. Abdulle said most Somalis in the state are U.S. citizens or lawfully present, but many had avoided local businesses and mosques when immigration enforcement intensified. The task force includes more than two dozen faith and business leaders and community organizers.

Community leader Abdullahi Farah said the community planned to channel its response through political engagement.

"Every election year the rhetoric goes up. And so we want to push back against these hateful rhetorics, but also bring our community together," Farah said.

## Religious institutions respond across denominations

In mid-November, U.S. Catholic bishops voted overwhelmingly to issue a "special message" on immigration — the first time in 12 years they had invoked that form of collective address. The message opposed what the bishops called indiscriminate mass deportation and dehumanizing rhetoric.

"We are concerned about the conditions in detention centers and the lack of access to pastoral care," the message said. "We oppose the indiscriminate mass deportation of people. We pray for an end to dehumanizing rhetoric and violence, whether directed at immigrants or at law enforcement."

Bishop Daniel Garcia of Austin, Texas, who chairs the Catholic bishops conference's subcommittee on racial justice, called on public officials to stop using dehumanizing language.

"Each child of God has value and dignity," Garcia said. "Language that denigrates a person or community based on his or her ethnicity or country of origin is incompatible with this truth."

The presiding bishop of the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America, Yehiel Curry, issued a similar pastoral message in late November thanking ELCA congregations for their support of immigrant communities.

"The racial profiling and harm to our immigrant neighbors show no signs of diminishing, so we will heed God's call to show up alongside these neighbors," Curry wrote.

HIAS, an international Jewish nonprofit serving refugees and asylum-seekers, also condemned recent Trump administration moves against Afghan and Somali communities.

"As a Jewish organization, we also know all too well what it means for an entire community to be targeted because of the actions of one person," HIAS said. "We will always stand in solidarity with people seeking the opportunity to rebuild their lives in safety."
