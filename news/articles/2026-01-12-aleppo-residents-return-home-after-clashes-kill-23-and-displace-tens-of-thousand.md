ALEPPO, Syria — Hundreds of displaced residents returned Monday to a predominantly Kurdish neighborhood in Aleppo, northern Syria, after days of intense fighting between the country's new government forces and Kurdish fighters killed at least 23 people and displaced tens of thousands. Buses carried families back to the Achrafieh district as armed security forces stood guard and many shops reopened, though residents reported electricity cuts.

The clashes — the most intense since former President Bashar Assad's ouster by rebel forces in December 2024 — erupted Jan. 6 after negotiations to merge the Syrian Democratic Forces, the main Kurdish-led fighting force and a longstanding U.S. partner against the Islamic State, into Syria's national army stalled. The episode exposed the fragility of Syria's post-Assad political transition, with tensions between the new government and the SDF unresolved even as some displaced families began returning home.

## Residents return, troops stand guard

About 10 buses carrying 700 families returned to Achrafieh on Monday, said Masoud Battal, director of the Afrin region for the Syrian government. The majority of the approximately 148,000 people displaced by the fighting had fled to the district of Afrin in the northwest of Aleppo province.

"I left Achrafieh five days ago. I was in Afrin and now we're returning to our homes, thank God," said Mohammed Sheikho, who was on one of the buses.

Jamal al-Youssef, an Arab resident, said he had fled with his family for about four days because of the fighting. He said he welcomed the Syrian government's exertion of control over the neighborhood.

"We've been waiting for this to happen for a long time, not just recently," al-Youssef said. He added quickly, however, that there was no conflict between Arab and Kurdish civilians in the area. "We have three or four Kurdish families in my building and we don't feel there's any difference between us."

## Ordnance clearance, evacuated fighters

Crews were combing through the neighboring district of Sheikh Maqsoud to decommission leftover explosives and tow away destroyed vehicles blocking the roads. Security forces were also inspecting tunnels under the neighborhood that appeared to have been used by fighters.

Hundreds of Kurdish fighters had evacuated from the contested neighborhoods to northeastern Syria, which is under SDF control, over the weekend under a deal reached between the SDF and government authorities. Security forces had captured Achrafieh, Sheikh Maqsoud and Bani Zeid — the three predominantly Kurdish neighborhoods where fighting concentrated — during five days of clashes that included shelling and drone strikes.

## Tensions persist along eastern lines

Despite the return of some residents, tensions between the government and the SDF remained high Monday.

Syria's state-run news agency SANA reported Monday evening that the Syrian army sent reinforcements to the deployment line east of Aleppo after seeing what it described as a buildup of SDF forces near the towns of Maskana and Deir Hafer in the eastern Aleppo countryside.

The SDF denied the claim. "There are no military movements or troop buildups by our forces in the aforementioned areas, and that all circulating claims are entirely unfounded," the SDF said in a statement. It accused the government of an "attempt to manufacture tension and create pretexts for escalation."

## Context: A fractured new army and Kurdish-Turkish tensions

The conflict reflects broader fault lines in Syria's post-Assad order. Some of the factions that make up the new Syrian army, formed after Assad's ouster, were previously Turkey-backed insurgent groups that have a long history of clashing with Kurdish forces.

The SDF has for years served as the main U.S. partner in Syria in operations against the Islamic State group. Turkey, however, considers the SDF a terrorist organization because of its links to the Kurdistan Workers' Party, or PKK, which has waged a long-running insurgency in Turkey.

The failed army-merger deal that triggered the January clashes remains unimplemented, leaving the SDF's relationship to Syria's central government unresolved.
