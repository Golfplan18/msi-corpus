LONDON — Hundreds of demonstrators gathered Saturday near the former Royal Mint site in London to protest China's planned embassy complex, as the British government faces a Tuesday deadline to approve or reject what would be Europe's largest Chinese diplomatic facility.

Conservative Party leader Kemi Badenoch addressed the crowd, urging Prime Minister Keir Starmer's Labour government to block the plans. "We know that we have to stand up to the abuses of China. And what worries me is that we have a government right now that seems to be scared of China," Badenoch told the assembled protesters, who chanted "no China mega embassy."

Badenoch said the Chinese government had "harassed and sanctioned" members of Parliament and "abused British nationals connected to China." Politicians from several other opposition parties also addressed the rally.

The project is widely expected to be approved despite the protest pressure. Approval would pave the way for a long-anticipated trip by Starmer to China and an expansion of the UK Embassy in Beijing, according to the Associated Press.

## Seven years in the making

China has waited seven years for a decision on the complex, a delay Beijing has openly protested. China said the UK was "constantly complicating and politicizing the matter."

The proposed development would cover the former Royal Mint site near Tower Bridge, spanning 20,000 square meters — about 215,000 square feet. It would consolidate China's current seven diplomatic premises in London onto a single site.

Starmer has said protecting national security is nonnegotiable, while stressing that Britain needs to maintain diplomatic dialogue and cooperation with China.

## Security debate

Opponents say the site — near London's financial district and close to crucial data cables — would serve as a base for espionage and for the surveillance and intimidation of Chinese dissidents living in exile.

Britain's intelligence agencies have issued a series of warnings about Chinese espionage activity. In November, MI5 issued an alert to lawmakers warning that Chinese agents were making "targeted and widespread" efforts to recruit and cultivate them through LinkedIn profiles or front companies.

UK security services are thought to have approved the development, however, and some security experts say the risks are manageable given what consolidating Beijing's seven existing London premises onto one site would enable in terms of counterintelligence.

Ciaran Martin, the former head of Britain's National Cyber Security Centre — part of the GCHQ intelligence agency — said the government's options are constrained by the realities of diplomacy. "Unless we want to sever diplomatic relations with China, the location of their embassy becomes an issue of practicalities, security assessments and counterintelligence operations," he wrote in The Times of London.
