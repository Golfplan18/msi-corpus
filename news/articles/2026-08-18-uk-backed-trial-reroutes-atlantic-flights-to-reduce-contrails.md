## Google AI forecasts will identify contrail-prone airspace

A British government-backed trial announced Tuesday will instruct hundreds of commercial flights to change their paths over the northeastern Atlantic Ocean during the next two winters, the first airspace-scale test of whether minor altitude adjustments can reduce aviation's climate impact.

The trial will operate inside the Shanwick Oceanic Control Area, the eastern half of the North Atlantic corridor that serves as a busy gateway for air traffic between Europe and North America. The Shanwick region is also a hot spot for where contrails form, making it a natural proving ground for the routing approach the trial is designed to test.

Contrails form when aircraft fly through cold and humid areas. Ice crystals develop around the soot particles emitted from the engine, creating clouds that trap heat and warm the planet. Avoiding those atmospheric pockets, rather than the flight's emissions themselves, is the lever the trial is meant to pull.

Google UK is providing the forecasts that identify where contrails are likely to form. Its artificial-intelligence-powered predictions will tell flight planners which slices of airspace to route around, allowing aircraft to make the minor altitude adjustments the trial is designed to measure.

British authorities framed the Tuesday-launched effort as a "landmark airspace-scale trial" intended to avoid creating condensation trails. The two-winter window gives operators and air-traffic controllers two consecutive cold seasons to work with the new forecasts and test whether the rerouting approach is operationally feasible at scale.

The Shanwick zone carries a large share of transatlantic flights between Europe and North America, making the trial a high-stakes experiment in whether climate-focused rerouting can coexist with normal traffic flows. Any operational gains — or disruptions — will play out across one of the world's busiest international corridors.
