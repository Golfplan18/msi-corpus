## DSA's São Paulo Forum and Cuba ties cited in commentary

United Press International published the commentary by Osvaldo Silva, a Chilean journalist and communications specialist who serves as director of Legacy of the Americas, a hemispheric initiative promoting civic and ethical renewal. Silva writes that while the Democratic Socialists of America probably seems to most Latin Americans like a distant feature of domestic U.S. politics, that view may be a mistake.

DSA announced on July 4 that it had reached 120,000 members, an all-time high. Silva notes the figure remains small compared with America's two major political parties, but argues that the organization's growing membership and electoral ambitions make it increasingly difficult to dismiss as marginal.

For Silva, the American debate over democratic socialism warrants attention south of the Rio Grande because many of the political and economic arguments gaining traction on the American left "sound remarkably familiar" to those who have lived through similar debates for generations. He cites his own country, Chile, which has spent more than half a century wrestling with questions about markets, equality, state power and the limits of political change — experiencing profound ideological polarization, attempts at rapid left-wing transformation and authoritarian rule from the right.

"There is no simple lesson from that history favoring either left or right," Silva writes. "But there is a warning: democratic institutions can become fragile when politics turns into a struggle to reconstruct society according to an ideological blueprint."

The commentary describes DSA's program as calling for public ownership of major corporations and essential industries, greater democratic control over economic activity and aggressive taxation of wealth to finance public services. Silva acknowledges the problems the proposals target — healthcare that can be prohibitively expensive, housing that is increasingly unaffordable and widespread economic insecurity. But he argues that Latin America has repeatedly seen governments increase state power in the name of equality or social justice, sometimes producing legitimate reforms and other times allowing economic and political power to become dangerously intertwined. The real question, he writes, is whether institutions remain strong enough to prevent any government from using economic power to reward allies, disadvantage opponents or weaken democratic restraints.

Silva highlights DSA's reach beyond U.S. domestic policy. The organization's International Committee identifies DSA as an associate member of the São Paulo Forum, the network of left-wing parties and political movements across Latin America and the Caribbean. An American socialist organization seeking greater electoral influence at home is therefore also building relationships with political forces throughout the hemisphere, Silva writes, treating its project as part of a broader international movement rather than a purely domestic debate about healthcare and taxation.

The commentary also addresses DSA's Cuba Solidarity Working Group, which campaigns for an end to U.S. sanctions and the economic embargo. Silva allows that there are legitimate arguments against the embargo and that criticism of the policy is hardly confined to socialists, but adds that Cubans have lived for decades under a system that does not permit normal democratic competition for national political power. "The same standard applies throughout Latin America," he writes, "where authoritarianism does not become acceptable merely because the government practicing it belongs to a favored ideological camp."

Silva stresses that DSA does not control the Democratic Party and that the existence of 120,000 democratic socialists does not mean the United States is on the verge of becoming socialist. American democracy, he writes, has strong institutions, competing political traditions and an electorate that frequently defies ideological predictions. Even so, he warns that political ideas that begin outside the mainstream do not necessarily remain there, and that if DSA succeeds even partially in building a mass socialist movement and expanding its electoral presence, its ideas could increasingly influence debates within one of America's two major political parties.

"And what happens in Washington rarely remains in Washington," Silva writes. For much of modern Latin American history, he notes, political and economic ideas have flowed southward from the United States; the present moment is unusual in that an American political movement is also reaching southward, building relationships with forces in the region while promoting ideas Latin America has already debated and sometimes rejected.

Silva does not argue that every form of socialism leads to authoritarianism, any more than markets by themselves produce justice or stable democracy. "What it does teach is that societies should judge political movements not only by the attractiveness of their promises, but also by the institutions they create and the limits they place upon their own power," he writes. "Economic justice matters. Political freedom does too."
