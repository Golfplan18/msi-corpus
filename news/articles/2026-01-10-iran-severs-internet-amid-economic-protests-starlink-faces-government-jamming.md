DUBAI — Iran's government severed the internet and telephone connections linking its 85 million people to the outside world just after 8 p.m. Thursday, cutting off contact with a worldwide diaspora as nationwide economic protests posed what observers described as the gravest challenge to the Islamic Republic in years, the Associated Press reported.

The blackout — Iran's third full internet cutoff since 2019 — limits the flow of protest images and witness accounts abroad. Iran's attorney general warned on Saturday that anyone taking part in demonstrations will be considered an "enemy of God," a charge that carries the death penalty under Iranian law.

"You can't understand our feelings. My brothers, my cousins, they will go on the street. You can't imagine the anxiety of the Iranian diaspora," said Azam Jangravi, a cybersecurity expert in Toronto who opposes Iran's government.

Her voice cracked as she added: "A lot of people are being killed and injured by the Islamic Republic of Iran, and we don't know who."

The Trump administration has warned Iran's government of consequences for further deaths among demonstrators, according to the AP.

## Starlink becomes the last link — and is being jammed

Satellite internet from Starlink has become the primary means of transmitting footage and accounts from inside Iran since Thursday's shutdown, according to Mehdi Yahyanejad, a Los Angeles-based internet freedom activist. The government has never authorized Starlink to operate in Iran and has made possession of receivers illegal. An Iranian official estimated tens of thousands of such receivers in use a year ago, a figure Yahyanejad said sounded plausible.

"In this case, because all those things have been disrupted, Starlink is playing the key for getting all these videos out," Yahyanejad said.

But Starlink connections are under mounting pressure. Amir Rashidi, director of digital rights and security at the Miaan Group, said he had measured about a 30% loss in packets — the discrete units of data that travel across the internet — sent by Starlink devices since Thursday. In some parts of Iran, Rashidi said the packet loss reached 80%.

"I believe the Iranian government is doing something beyond GPS jamming, like in Ukraine where Russia tried to jam Starlink," Rashidi said. He suggested Iran may be deploying mobile jammers, a tactic the government used in earlier decades to disrupt satellite television reception.

Starlink receivers depend on GPS signals to orient themselves toward a constellation of low-orbit satellites. According to the AP, Iran has been disrupting GPS signals since its 12-day war with Israel last June, a measure initially aimed at limiting drone effectiveness. The International Telecommunication Union, a United Nations agency, has previously called on Iran to stop jamming. Iran has also sought at the ITU to have Starlink service to its territory shut off entirely.

## Third shutdown follows two deadly crackdowns

Iran has severed its people from the global internet twice before. The first cutoff came in 2019, when demonstrators protested a government-imposed spike in subsidized gasoline prices; over 300 people reportedly were killed in the crackdown that followed. The second came after the 2022 death of Mahsa Amini following her arrest by Iran's morality police over allegations that she had not worn her hijab to the satisfaction of authorities; a monthslong crackdown killed more than 500 people.

Until the current shutdown, Iranians could still reach restricted applications and blocked websites through virtual private networks, even while under international sanctions related to the country's nuclear program.

## 'Help needs to come soon'

The dangers facing those who continue to use Starlink to send information out of Iran are severe, Jangravi said.

"It's really hard to use it because if they arrest a person, they can execute the person and say this person is working for Israel or the United States," she said.

Not using it, however, leaves the world with even less information about conditions inside the country. Yahyanejad said the durability of the protests was uncertain absent some change in the situation.

"This sort of nonviolent protest is not sustainable when the violence (by security forces) is so extreme," Yahyanejad said. "Unless something changes in the next two or three days, these protests can die down, too. If there's any help, it needs to come soon."
