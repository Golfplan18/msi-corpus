Flu cases are rising sharply across the United States, driven by a new variant called subclade K, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported Tuesday. The agency said high or very high levels of influenza illness had been detected in more than half the country's states as of Dec. 20 — before major holiday gatherings that health officials expect to push case counts higher.

The CDC estimated at least 7.5 million illnesses, 81,000 hospitalizations, and 3,100 deaths from flu so far this season, including at least eight child deaths. Those figures are based on data through Dec. 20.

The early and active season is being shaped by subclade K, a mutation of the H3N2 influenza A subtype that drove early outbreaks in the United Kingdom, Japan, and Canada. Health officials say flu shots remain the best available protection even though this season's vaccine is not a perfect match to the new strain.

## What subclade K is

The influenza virus mutates continuously and circulates in multiple forms. Subclade K is a mutated version of H3N2, one of two Type A influenza subtypes. H3N2 strains tend to produce more severe flu seasons, particularly for older adults.

Subclade K's mutations are not substantial enough to be classified as an entirely new type of flu, according to the AP. But they are significant enough to reduce some of the protection the current vaccine provides.

"They're different enough to evade some of the protection from this year's vaccine," said Andrew Pekosz, a virus expert at Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

## How severe this season may be

The CDC said it is too early to determine how the season will ultimately compare to prior years. Flu seasons dominated by H3N2 variants tend to produce higher infection rates and more serious illness overall, but Pekosz cautioned it will take time to determine whether subclade K spreads more easily, causes more severe disease, or both.

New York's health department said the week ending Dec. 20 was the most active flu week the state had recorded since 2004, with 71,000 cases counted.

## Vaccine guidance

A preliminary analysis from the United Kingdom found the current flu vaccine offers at least partial protection against subclade K, lowering the risk of hospitalization, according to the AP. It is not a perfect match, but health experts say vaccination can still prevent severe illness even if a vaccinated person becomes infected.

The CDC and major medical societies recommend a flu vaccine for virtually everyone age 6 months and older. About 42% of adults and children have been vaccinated so far this season, the CDC said.

Vaccines protect against three influenza strains. Despite the H3N2 variant's spread, the shots appear to remain a good match against H1N1 and Type B flu strains that may also circulate this season, Pekosz said.

FluMist, a nasal spray vaccine, is available for people ages 2 to 49. For the first time this season, some people may be eligible to self-administer FluMist at home.

## Treatment options

Prescription antiviral medicines are available to treat flu and are typically recommended for people at high risk of complications, the CDC noted. These medications generally must be started within one to two days of symptoms beginning to be most effective.

People at elevated risk of severe flu illness include adults 65 and older, pregnant women, young children, and people of any age with chronic conditions such as asthma, diabetes, heart disease, or weakened immune systems.
