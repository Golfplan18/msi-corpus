Americans are increasingly rejecting the Democratic and Republican parties, according to Gallup polling reported by the Associated Press.

Just under half of U.S. adults, 45%, now identify as independents, Gallup found. AP said that figure represents a substantial shift from about 20 years ago, when closer to one-third of Americans identified as independents.

Gallup’s analysis, as described by AP, said independents appear increasingly driven by unhappiness with the party in power. AP reported that independents have leaned more toward the Democrats over the past year, but that attitudes toward the party have not gotten warmer—suggesting Democratic gains are more connected to independents’ views of President Donald Trump than to improved perceptions of the Democratic Party itself.

AP reported that Trump’s approval among independents has fallen steadily over the year. It also said Democrats’ favorability remains historically low.

Younger Americans, AP reported, are rejecting the parties at higher rates than older generations. More than half of Generation Z and Millennials identify as political independents, AP said, with independent identity softer among older age groups.

Independents have long been the largest political group in the U.S., and AP reported that their numbers have increased over the last 15 years. Even so, AP said independents often lean toward one party or the other when asked which party they align with.

This year, AP reported, the Democratic Party gained the partisanship edge among independents when respondents were asked whether they lean more toward the Democratic or Republican Party. Nearly half of U.S. adults, 47%, identify as Democrats or lean toward the Democratic Party, while 42% are Republicans or lean Republican, AP said. AP added that the shift may not be reflected in voters’ actual registration.

AP said the change shifted a three-year party affiliation advantage that the Republican Party held while President Joe Biden was in office, reverting to where Democrats stood during Trump’s first term. AP described it as signaling how Americans feel about their political affiliations, even as it cautioned it may not predict registration patterns.

AP also reported that younger Americans are driving the recent rise in the share of U.S. adults identifying as independents. It said majorities of Gen Z and Millennial adults—people born between 1981 and 2007—now identify as independents.

In Gallup’s analysis described by AP, independent identity is softer in older generations, where about 4 in 10 in Gen X call themselves independents and roughly 3 in 10 older adults do. AP said 56% of Gen Z adults now identify as independents, higher than in earlier Gallup comparisons: it cited 47% of Millennials in 2012 and 40% of Gen X adults in 1992.

Gallup’s findings also pointed to a change in how people who identify as independents describe their political positioning. AP reported that more independents have described their views as “moderate” over the last decade while Democrats and Republicans have grown less likely to identify as moderates.

AP said about half of independents—47%—called themselves moderates in 2025, compared with about 3 in 10 Democrats and about 2 in 10 Republicans. At the same time, AP reported that Democrats and Republicans have become more polarized in ideological self-description, with about 6 in 10 Democrats calling themselves liberal and 77% of Republicans calling themselves conservative.

With moderate identification low among both parties, AP said appeals to the center to win the growing pool of independents could risk alienating the most committed voters in each party’s base.
