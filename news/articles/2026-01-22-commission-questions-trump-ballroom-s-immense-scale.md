The Commission of Fine Arts reviewed Donald Trump's proposed White House ballroom expansion Thursday and broadly endorsed the project, even as commissioners questioned whether its "immense" scale would overshadow the existing structure.

On the same day, a U.S. District judge heard arguments from historic preservationists seeking a court order to suspend the $400 million project.

The review revealed the central tension around the proposal: Trump-appointed commissioners overseeing federal construction want the ballroom, but question whether the addition can be executed at the proposed scale without dominating the White House. A legal challenge introduces a separate question: whether the president has the authority to undertake such an expansion without congressional approval.

The Commission of Fine Arts held its first public hearing Thursday on Trump's proposal to demolish the White House East Wing and construct a ballroom addition covering almost 90,000 square feet, with the ballroom itself accounting for 22,000 square feet.

## Commissioners Question Scale and Design

Commission chairman Rodney Mims Cook Jr., appointed by Trump to lead the panel, said the expansion addresses a long-standing problem for administrations. "You can't have the United States of America entertaining people in tents," he said, noting that administrations long before Trump have complained about hosting State Dinners and major events in temporary structures.

Commissioners asked lead architect Shalom Baranes to return to a future in-person session with 3D scale models of the White House complex, the U.S. Treasury Department building, and the Eisenhower Executive Office Building. Thomas Luebke, the commission's executive director, reported that online public comments received on the proposal were "almost all" negative, criticizing the process, design, or both.

Even the comment Luebke described as "more positive" raised scale concerns. That commenter praised the design style but wrote that "the scale appears oversized, making the main structure dominated," Luebke said.

Baranes emphasized that current plans set the addition's north boundary back from the North Portico and align the top of the new structure with the existing White House facade to preserve the view from Pennsylvania Avenue. The White House was about 55,000 square feet before the East Wing, first built in 1902 and expanded in the 1940s, was demolished.

But commissioners said that approach does not address the south side of the expansion, where renderings show a 10-column, multistory porch. Cook asked whether the president would accept toning down that element.

"If the president just wants cover, do you think you might be able to tone down that element?" Cook said.

Baranes answered that architects examined various options but that the design reflects the president's stated desires. "We looked at ways of covering it at different scales with different numbers of columns, and there's a president's desire to proceed with this one," he said.

## Court Challenge Tests Presidential Authority

The same day, U.S. District Judge Richard Leon heard arguments on a lawsuit filed by the National Trust for Historic Preservation seeking a preliminary injunction to suspend construction of the $400 million project.

The plaintiffs argue the president, as a temporary occupant of the White House, lacks authority to undertake a project of this magnitude without congressional approval. "He isn't the landlord," attorney Thad Heuer argued for the historic preservationists. "He is a steward."

Government attorney Jacob Roth countered that the president has broad statutory authority to modify the White House. Stopping construction mid-project would create security problems, Roth told the judge. "I don't think there's any question that this modernization is in the public's interest," he said.

Judge Leon, nominated to the federal bench by Republican President George W. Bush, said he expects to issue a decision on the injunction request sometime next month. He acknowledged that his ruling will likely be appealed regardless of the outcome.

The Commission of Fine Arts and the National Capital Planning Commission both have roles in assessing and approving federal construction projects in Washington.
