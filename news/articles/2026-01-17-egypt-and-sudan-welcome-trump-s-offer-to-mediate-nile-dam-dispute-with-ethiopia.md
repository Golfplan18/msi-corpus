CAIRO — Egypt and Sudan on Saturday welcomed President Donald Trump's offer to revive United States-led mediation with Ethiopia over the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, a $5 billion hydroelectric project on the Blue Nile that has strained relations among the three countries for more than a decade.

Egyptian President Abdel-Fattah el-Sissi said on social media that he valued Trump's attention to the Nile issue and that Egypt was committed to serious and constructive cooperation with Nile Basin countries grounded in international law principles. El-Sissi said Cairo supports Washington's efforts to resolve the dispute with Ethiopia.

Sudan's Sovereign Council head, General Abdel-Fattah Burhan, praised Trump's initiative in a post on X as a step toward finding sustainable solutions that would preserve the rights of all parties, according to the Associated Press. Ethiopian authorities made no comment.

Trump posted on social media Friday a letter he sent to el-Sissi saying he was ready to restart U.S. mediation to responsibly resolve the Nile water-sharing dispute once and for all.

## The dam and the dispute

The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, inaugurated last fall on the Blue Nile near the Ethiopia-Sudan border, is Africa's largest hydroelectric facility. It is designed to produce more than 5,000 megawatts — more than double Ethiopia's previous generation capacity. Ethiopia, where the vast majority of the population lacks electricity, has called the $5 billion project essential to the country's development.

Egypt, a mostly desert country of 110 million people, depends on the Nile for its drinking water supply and has called the dam a "grave violation of international law" and an existential threat to its water security. El-Sissi has described Egypt's Nile water allocation as untouchable while favoring a negotiated resolution. Sudan, for its part, wants to coordinate the dam's filling and operation schedule to avoid downstream effects on its own dams.

## A second attempt at mediation

U.S. mediation efforts in the dispute began during Trump's first term but collapsed in 2020 after Ethiopia withdrew from negotiations. Subsequent discussions through the African Union produced no agreement.

The core disagreements involve the pace and volume of the dam's annual filling, the amount of water Ethiopia would release downstream during prolonged drought, and the mechanism by which the three countries would resolve future disputes. Egypt and Sudan have pressed for a legally binding agreement governing the dam's filling and operation. Ethiopia has insisted on voluntary guidelines rather than binding commitments.
