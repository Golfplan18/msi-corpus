Erich von Däniken, the Swiss author whose bestselling claim that ancient civilizations were guided by extraterrestrial visitors made him a publishing phenomenon and a persistent target of scientific ridicule, died Saturday at a hospital in central Switzerland. He was 90.

Von Däniken's representatives announced his death Sunday on his official website. His daughter, Cornelia, confirmed the death to the Swiss news agency SDA, according to the Associated Press.

Von Däniken's 1968 debut, *Chariots of the Gods?*, ignited a global industry of ancient-astronaut speculation that he sustained across more than two dozen books — selling 60 million copies in 32 languages — and that shaped popular culture from semi-serious archaeological documentaries to *The X-Files*.

## From Schaffhausen to global publishing

Born in 1935 in Schaffhausen, Switzerland, the son of a clothing manufacturer, von Däniken is said to have rebelled early against the strict Catholicism of his father and the priests who instructed him at boarding school, developing what he framed as personal alternatives to the biblical account of human origins.

He left school in 1954 and worked for several years as a waiter and bartender. During that period, he was repeatedly accused of fraud and served brief prison terms, according to the Associated Press.

In 1964, he was appointed manager of a hotel in the Swiss resort city of Davos and began writing his first book. *Chariots of the Gods?* argued that the Mayans and ancient Egyptians had been visited by alien astronauts who instructed them in the advanced technology required to build giant pyramids. It arrived at a propitious cultural moment: mainstream science was on the verge of landing humans on the Moon, and public appetite for explanations of the unexplained was growing.

The book's rapid commercial success was quickly followed by charges of tax evasion and financial impropriety, for which von Däniken spent additional time in custody. By the time he was released, the book was generating a substantial income; a second book, *Gods from Outer Space*, was ready for publication, and he turned his attention full time to paranormal research and worldwide fieldwork.

## A career built on contested claims

Throughout the 1970s, von Däniken conducted extensive field trips to Egypt, India, and Latin America, whose ancient cultures held particular fascination for the self-described amateur archaeologist. He gave lectures, founded societies to promote his theories, and later pioneered the use of video and multimedia to reach audiences seeking an alternative version of history.

Scientific communities responded with consistent rejection. A British television documentary confronted him with evidence that pottery he had presented as ancient artifacts was nearly new; he acknowledged minor discrepancies while maintaining that the foundations of his theories remained sound, according to the Associated Press.

In 1991, he became the first recipient of the Ig Nobel Prize in literature — an annual award for work that first makes people laugh, then makes them think — in recognition of his role in raising public awareness of science through questionable claims.

Von Däniken's popularity in the English-speaking world declined through the 1980s, but his influence on popular media proved durable. His books and films are credited by the Associated Press with helping inspire a wave of semi-serious archaeological documentaries and popular television programs, including *The X-Files*, which followed two FBI agents investigating paranormal mysteries.

## A failed park and an enduring imprint

His last major commercial venture, a theme park based on his books called Mystery Park, failed after only a few years due to insufficient visitor interest. The park still stands near the Swiss city of Interlaken, its artificial pyramids and otherworldly domes deteriorating as tourists continue to favor the nearby Alps.

Von Däniken is survived by his wife of 65 years, Elisabeth Skaja; his daughter, Cornelia; and two grandchildren.
