Guatemala President Bernardo Arévalo declared a 30-day state of siege Sunday after suspected gang members killed seven national police officers in and around Guatemala City, Interior Minister Marco Antonio Villeda said. The killings came hours after authorities stormed a maximum-security prison in Escuintla to free nine guards taken hostage during a coordinated uprising across three prisons the previous day. By Sunday evening, security forces had retaken control of all three facilities, freeing a total of 43 guards.

The prison riots and coordinated street attacks mark one of the sharpest confrontations between the Guatemalan state and organized crime in recent years, forcing Arévalo to invoke emergency powers that can curtail constitutional rights — and drawing regional attention to whether Central American governments can contain gang violence without replicating El Salvador's mass-incarceration approach.

Guatemala's President Bernardo Arévalo declared a 30-day state of siege Sunday after suspected gang members killed seven national police officers in a series of assaults across Guatemala City, Interior Minister Marco Antonio Villeda said. Ten other officers were wounded in the attacks, which Arévalo described as retaliation for authorities recapturing control of three maximum-security prisons where inmates had rioted and taken guards hostage.

The emergency declaration, which can take effect before congressional approval, invokes constitutional authority for cases of serious violence or organized crime activity that exceeds civilian capacity to contain. It can restrict freedoms of movement, gathering, and protest. The Ministry of Education announced it would suspend classes nationwide on Monday to prioritize student and teacher safety.

## Prison retaking

The confrontation began Saturday when inmates at three prisons launched a coordinated uprising to protest authorities' decision to strip privileges from some incarcerated gang leaders. The Interior Ministry reported 46 guards held hostage.

On Sunday, hundreds of anti-riot police stormed Renovación prison in Escuintla, about 47 miles southwest of Guatemala City, freeing nine guards. An Associated Press journalist at the scene saw the freed guards escorted from the facility approximately 15 minutes after riot squads entered; they appeared unharmed, and no injuries or deaths inside the prison were immediately reported. Authorities retook the two remaining prisons in the capital later Sunday, freeing six guards at one and 28 at the other, according to the National Civil Police. Arévalo said all hostage guards were freed by day's end.

## Gang retaliation

As security forces moved on the prisons, armed gangs carried out strikes in and around the capital. Villeda said seven officers were killed and 10 wounded in the assaults. Police arrested seven gang members, confiscated two rifles, and seized two vehicles. One gang member was killed in the clashes.

"They rioted in the prisons and took hostages with the intention of making the state accept their demands, which for decades were granted," Arévalo said on national television. The street attacks that followed were, he said, "an attempt to terrorize security forces and the population so that the government relents in its head-on fight against the gangs."

Villeda credited police restraint and discipline, calling the outcome "the result of not negotiating with criminals." He added: "The state will not kneel before these criminals," framing the attacks on officers and the coordinated prison uprising as a direct response to the government's intensifying pressure on organized crime.

The Barrio 18 and Mara Salvatrucha gangs are the dominant organized crime groups in Guatemala, as they are across much of Central America.

## Regional context

The episode renews debate over how Central American governments should confront entrenched gang power. El Salvador's President Nayib Bukele declared a state of emergency in March 2022 after gangsters killed dozens in a single weekend; that declaration, which suspends rights including access to legal counsel, remains in effect, and more than 90,000 people have been arrested under it. Last week, Bukele visited Costa Rica for a groundbreaking ceremony for a new prison modeled on facilities he constructed in El Salvador.

Other leaders in the region have faced mounting pressure to adopt comparably aggressive tactics. Arévalo portrayed Sunday's events differently — as evidence that his administration's security strategy was producing results. The audacity of the gang challenge, he said, was a sign that the government's approach was working.

Police reinforced guards at additional facilities and increased joint patrols with the military following the day's violence.
