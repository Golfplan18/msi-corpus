## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- The New York Times sued the Pentagon again, saying a requirement that journalists be escorted while on Pentagon grounds violates the First Amendment.
- The paper and reporter Julian E. Barnes filed the latest lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia.

- The escort policy remained in place through appeals after U.S. District Court Judge Paul L. Friedman ruled against earlier media-access restrictions tied to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

The New York Times took the Pentagon back to court for a second time in five months, arguing that a Pentagon policy requiring journalists to be escorted on its grounds runs into First Amendment limits. The dispute follows earlier litigation over media-access rules implemented under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, which also led to rulings by U.S. District Court Judge Paul L. Friedman and an ongoing appeals process.

In the new complaint filed in the District of Columbia federal court on Monday, the Times and its reporter Julian E. Barnes asked the court to address the escort requirement directly on constitutional grounds, according to the Associated Press. The paper also argued that the escort rule and other Pentagon restrictions share an aim: limiting reporting to what Pentagon officials approve, framing that outcome as unconstitutional.

The escort requirement became part of the Pentagon’s media-access approach after a series of court decisions that shifted the rules for access and then left the escorted approach in place while the government appealed parts of Friedman’s rulings. The policy was implemented in March after Friedman struck down earlier restrictions on media access that he said violated the rights of Barnes and the paper.

Friedman later ruled that an interim policy violated his March order, but the escorted access policy stayed in effect when an appeals court stayed part of his ruling while the government pursued its challenge. The appeals process remains ongoing, with the escort rule continuing to govern reporter access during that litigation.

A Times spokesman, Charlie Stadtlander, told the Associated Press by email that the escort policy amounts to “an unconstitutional attempt by the Pentagon to prevent independent reporting on military affairs.” Stadtlander added: “As we have said before: Americans deserve visibility into how their government is being run, and the actions the military is taking in their name and with their tax dollars.”

Defense Department spokesperson Sean Parnell responded on X, saying the Times’ latest lawsuit was “nothing more than an attempt to remove the barriers to them getting their hands on classified information.” Parnell characterized the Department's policy as lawful and designed to protect national security information.

The new lawsuit also comes after the Times’ earlier challenge in December, when it sued over Hegseth-imposed media access rules that included conditions for reporting from within the Pentagon. In that earlier dispute, multiple outlets—including the Times—contended they could not accept the rules as a condition for press credentials, with coverage continuing from outside the Pentagon while a new press corps approved by the department occupied Pentagon space.

The latest case is the newest step in a broader pattern of legal and public conflict between the media and the second Trump administration, including fights over access rules and court orders. As the litigation continues, the question for the courts remains whether the escort requirement can coexist with constitutional protections for press access to how the Pentagon operates.
