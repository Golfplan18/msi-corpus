Iran's leadership, weakened by a June war with Israel and U.S. airstrikes on its nuclear facilities, faces the largest protests in years against the Islamic Republic, as a government crackdown has killed hundreds, the Associated Press reported Sunday.

The unrest erupted after the Iranian rial fell to a record low of 1.42 million to the U.S. dollar in late December, driving traders and shopkeepers in major Tehran markets into the streets before spreading into a broader challenge to the theocracy, now in its 47th year.

Analysts say the U.S. has the chance to apply pressure on Iran's leadership at the weakest point in the Islamic Republic's 47-year history — military capacity degraded, regional proxies decimated, and the 86-year-old Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei without a named successor.

## A leadership hollowed out by war

The June war with Israel — and accompanying U.S. airstrikes on Iran's nuclear facilities — killed several military commanders, nearly wiped out Iran's air defenses, and shrank its missile stockpile, the AP reported. Khamenei, who has ruled since 1989 and holds ultimate power, was out of public sight for days during and after the fighting.

"The U.S. now has the chance to apply pressure on Iran's leadership at the weakest point in the Islamic Republic's 47-year history," said Kamran Matin, associate professor of International Relations at the University of Sussex.

Reformist President Masoud Pezeshkian holds the presidency but has little power to make the sweeping economic or other changes that protesters are demanding, according to the AP.

Iran's network of regional armed proxies — Hezbollah in Lebanon, Hamas in Gaza, Houthi rebels in Yemen, and other armed groups in Syria and Iraq — was also targeted by Israel during the fighting in Gaza and the June war, further diminishing Tehran's regional standing.

## Isolated on the world stage

Iran's closest allies offer limited support. Russia, a partner, is occupied with its war in Ukraine. China, which purchases Iranian oil, issued a statement Monday expressing hope that the Iranian government and people "are able to overcome the current difficulties and maintain national stability."

After Iran's negotiations with the United States deadlocked, the United Nations reimposed sanctions in September, freezing Iranian assets abroad, halting arms deals, and penalizing any development of the country's ballistic missile program.

Siavush Randjbar-Daemi, senior lecturer at the University of St Andrews, said pragmatists within Iran's establishment who might be willing to concede ground to Washington exist but "are really marginalized." Finding a figure capable of serving as an interlocutor with the U.S. — analogous to Venezuelan Vice President Delcy Rodríguez, who emerged as interim leader after the U.S. removal of Nicolás Maduro — is difficult within the current Iranian establishment, he said. "The problem again remains that finding a Delcy Rodríguez-like figure within the Iranian establishment is very hard," Randjbar-Daemi said.

## Economic pressure behind the protests

The sanctions compounded an economy already under strain. In late December, Iranians saw the rial plunge to a record low of 1.42 million to the dollar, sending prices of food and other necessities sharply upward. Merchants and shopkeepers in major Tehran markets took to the streets.

That anger then broadened into a challenge to the theocracy itself, and leaderless protests spread to other cities, the AP reported.

Iranians' history of taking to the streets when accumulated grievances cross a threshold — the harsh enforcement of headscarf rules, or inflation — runs across decades, the AP noted, despite state repression that has limited organized opposition inside the country.

## Washington's posture

President Donald Trump has pledged to strike Iran if protesters are killed and said Iran has signaled that it wants to negotiate with Washington. U.S. lawmakers sought Sunday to temper expectations of direct military intervention.

Sen. Mark Warner, vice chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee, said on CNN that popular discontent over the U.S. role in the 1953 coup in Iran contributed to the 1979 Islamic revolution that brought the current government to power.

Matin said Trump's threats are aimed at extracting concessions rather than toppling the government. "Trump's primary aim is to change the behavior of the Iranian regime, not necessarily the regime itself," Matin said, with issues such as nuclear enrichment levels and missile range the likely targets.

The AP reported no signs of a Venezuela-style U.S. military intervention on the horizon.

## No visible cracks in the government

Despite the scale of the protests and the economic strain, no significant fractures have appeared publicly within the Iranian government, the AP reported. No major domestic political challengers have emerged. In the diaspora, opposition organizations remain divided.

Signs of support for exiled Crown Prince Reza Pahlavi, son of the shah who was forced from power in 1979, have appeared among Iranians at home and abroad, the AP reported, though how strong or widespread such sentiment may be is not clear.

Ellie Geranmayeh, deputy program director with the European Council on Foreign Relations, said the government has a long record of surviving extreme pressure. "Iran's leaders face a perilous moment, but they are no strangers to chaos. The regime has survived wars, sanctions and political upheaval through ruthless force, pragmatism and leadership unity. However, the off-ramps have now significantly narrowed," Geranmayeh said.

Analysts broadly agreed that any durable change in Iran would best come from Iranians themselves.
