The sudden détente exposed the deep mutual stakes in the U.S.-Colombia relationship: Washington has provided Bogotá with roughly $14 billion in security assistance over the past two decades, and the United States relies on Colombia as the cornerstone of its counternarcotics strategy in Latin America — a partnership both governments concluded was too valuable to sacrifice.

BOGOTÁ, Colombia — Colombian President Gustavo Petro surprised thousands of protesters he had summoned Wednesday when he stepped to the microphone not with a promised sharp rebuke of President Donald Trump, but with word of a friendly phone call and an open door to the White House.

"I had one speech prepared for today, but I have to give another one," Petro told the crowd in Bogotá. "The first speech was quite harsh. I had to change it."

Just minutes before the speech, Petro said, he had spoken directly with Trump. The sudden de-escalation came after days in which Petro had been the most outspoken world leader criticizing the Trump administration's capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro, calling it an "abhorrent" violation of Latin American sovereignty and comparing U.S. actions to Nazi Germany's 1937 carpet bombing of Guernica, Spain.

Trump confirmed the call on social media, calling it a "Great Honor" to speak with Petro and extending a White House invitation. "I appreciated his call and tone, and look forward to meeting him in the near future," Trump wrote.

## Stakes on both sides

The détente exposed how much both governments stood to lose. The United States has worked closely with Colombia for roughly 30 years on drug trafficking interdiction, counterinsurgency, and rural economic development, providing Bogotá with roughly $14 billion over the past two decades, according to the Associated Press. For the United States, Colombia is the cornerstone of its counternarcotics strategy in the region, providing intelligence used to interdict drugs in the Caribbean.

"The Colombians are extremely effective in taking advantage of their contacts in Washington, on the Hill and elsewhere, and the private sector is mobilized," said Michael Shifter, a Latin America expert at the Inter-American Dialogue think tank in Washington. "People were trying to tell Trump: 'Look, you can punish Petro to the extent possible, but you don't want to punish the country. That undermines the fight against drugs and is going to be harmful for the United States.'"

## Weeks of escalation

The confrontation had been building for months, accelerating sharply after the U.S. captured Maduro. Trump called Petro a "sick man who likes making cocaine and selling it to the United States" and warned of a possible military operation on Colombian soil. Previously, Trump had called Petro a "lunatic" and "international drug leader" — characterizations Petro disputed, telling Trump in their Wednesday call that his only connection to drug trafficking was his commitment to fighting against it.

Trump also revoked Petro's U.S. visa and the visas of his top officials and diplomats, imposed sanctions on Petro, his relatives, and his interior minister on drug-related grounds, threatened punitive tariffs on Colombian exports, and vowed to end all U.S. aid to Colombia.

Petro, a former leftist guerrilla and Colombia's first left-wing president, responded with escalating rhetoric. He convened emergency meetings before the United Nations and the Organization of American States, organized nationwide demonstrations, and threatened to take up arms to defend Colombian sovereignty.

On Wednesday, thousands gathered in public squares across Colombia chanting "Long live free and sovereign Colombia!" Banners read "The U.S. is the biggest threat to world peace."

## Political calculation

Analysts said Petro, who is constitutionally barred from seeking another term in May's presidential election, found in Trump a useful political foil at a moment when his domestic standing was weakening. His coalition faces legislative elections in March, his "total peace" agenda with armed groups has stalled, and his reform program has met congressional resistance.

"He wants this stage where he is the clearest adversary, rhetorically or politically, to the U.S.," said Sergio Guzman, a political risk analyst based in Bogotá.

The dual track inside Petro's own government illustrated the bind. While the president wielded social media to hammer Trump, his interior and justice ministers separately reassured U.S. intelligence agencies that Colombia would "continue to coordinate and cooperate in the fight against drug trafficking," the government said. Defense Minister Pedro Sánchez declared the standoff a "golden moment" for both countries to move away from confrontation.

"Whereas the Colombian institutions still maintain cooperation and have a lot to lose, Petro personally felt like that bridge has already burned," said Elizabeth Dickinson, a senior analyst at the International Crisis Group.

## "Colombia can sleep soundly"

Foreign Minister Rosa Villavicencio had warned reporters Tuesday that Colombian authorities were preparing for "the possibility of aggression against our country by the United States." "For this, we have a highly trained, very well prepared army," she said. By Wednesday evening, Villavicencio was instead preparing for a trip to Washington to arrange Petro's White House visit — a trip that first required her to obtain a U.S. visa, after her government's had been revoked.

Petro framed the de-escalation to the crowd as both necessary and conditional. "I asked (Trump) to re-establish direct communication between our governments," he said. "If there is no dialogue, there will be war."

He then offered reassurance. "The priority is peace, and peace is achieved through dialogue," Petro said. "Colombia can sleep soundly."

Experts had questioned the likelihood of a U.S. military operation against Petro, who unlike Maduro was democratically elected. But Trump's increasingly militaristic language — lumping Colombia with Venezuela as a source of narcotics and migrants — had alarmed Colombian officials facing an acute calculation: how to stand up to Washington without destroying the security partnership that four administrations had built across three decades.
