The overlap between personal finance goals and environmental impact gives households a practical entry point into lower-emissions living without requiring major changes to lifestyle or spending.

Common New Year's resolutions — eating healthier, saving money and getting organized — also reduce household carbon emissions, according to energy, food and fashion experts. The habits require little upfront cost and fit into routines most people already plan to change.

As MSI previously reported, [common resolutions can also shrink household emissions](/articles/2025-12-30-common-new-year-s-resolutions-can-also-shrink-household-emissions). The following draws on expert guidance in three specific areas: home energy use, food waste and clothing consumption.

## Cutting energy costs at home

Unplugging chargers and entertainment systems — or using a power strip with an on-off switch — can cut phantom energy draw from appliances that continue drawing power when turned off, said Matthew Gonzales, vice chairman of the National Hispanic Energy Council, which advocates for affordable energy for communities of color.

The U.S. Department of Energy said adjusting a thermostat 7 to 10 degrees Fahrenheit (4 to 6 degrees Celsius) for eight hours a day can save up to 10% a year on heating and cooling costs.

Gonzales said other low-cost options include switching to LED bulbs, sealing drafty windows, replacing dirty air filters and managing natural light strategically during the day to limit heat gain or loss.

"Don't let perfect be the enemy of good enough," Gonzales said.

Washing clothes in cold water and air-drying them can also reduce energy use. Heating water is the biggest energy draw in laundry, and clothes dryers are among the most energy-intensive household appliances, Gonzales said. The approach can also reduce wear and tear on clothing, he added. Savings vary depending on a household's location, the age of its home and local energy prices.

## Food waste

The Environmental Protection Agency estimates food waste costs U.S. consumers $728 every year and generates annual greenhouse gas emissions equivalent to 42 coal-fired power plants.

One starting point is using what is already in the pantry before going to the grocery store, said cookbook author Anne-Marie Bonneau.

"If you have fat and an onion and a couple of random vegetables — or even one random vegetable or some leftover cooked proteins or grains — you've got soup in the making," Bonneau said. "If you've cleared out so much food that you don't even have an onion and fat, it's probably time to go shopping."

She also recommended preserving food before it spoils through fermentation — noting sauerkraut requires only cabbage, salt and a glass jar — and making sauces from seasonal vegetables to freeze for use later in the year.

Eating fewer ultra-processed foods, such as sugary cereals, hot dogs and packaged meals, can benefit both health and the environment, Bonneau said. Those foods are often packaged in plastic, which can shed into food and contribute to pollution.

"You'll improve your diet, you'll reduce your waste and your food will come in less contact with plastic," she said.

## Decluttering and clothing

Getting organized does not require buying storage bins or discarding items, said Katrina Caspelich, communications director for Remake, an advocacy group focused on human rights and climate justice in fashion. Spending an hour spotting duplicates and rediscovering forgotten possessions can reduce impulse spending by clarifying what someone actually uses, she said.

A "one-in, one-out" rule — buying a new item only when it replaces an existing one — can prevent wardrobes from growing and encourage more deliberate purchases, Caspelich said.

Unsubscribing from brand emails and sale alerts can also reduce unplanned spending. "Most impulse purchases start with a notification, not a need," she said, adding that waiting 24 hours before buying often turns a perceived must-have into a pass.

Remake estimates that someone who completes a 90-day pause on new clothing purchases can save about 3,900 liters (1,030 gallons) of water, avoid roughly 300 kilograms (about 661 pounds) of carbon dioxide emissions, keep around 9 kilograms (about 20 pounds) of clothing out of landfills and save an average of nearly $300.

The EPA reported that Americans generated about 17 million tons of textile waste in 2018, most of it clothing.

"The key is flexibility," Caspelich said. "Framing it as an experiment rather than a strict rule makes it feel empowering instead of restrictive."
