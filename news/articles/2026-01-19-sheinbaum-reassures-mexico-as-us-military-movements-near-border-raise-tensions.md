Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum said Monday that two recent U.S. military movements near Mexico posed no threat to the country's sovereignty, after each incident prompted demands for explanation from opposition legislators and raised public alarm. Sheinbaum said her administration secured written assurance from Washington that no American military aircraft would fly over Mexican territory and described a separate U.S. military transport plane landing near Mexico City as a routine training transfer authorized by Mexico's defense secretary.

The incidents unsettled Mexico against the backdrop of the Trump administration's capture of then-Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro earlier this month and renewed Trump administration threats to take direct military action against drug cartels operating inside Mexico.

## The FAA advisory

The U.S. Federal Aviation Administration issued an advisory Friday urging American aircraft operators to "exercise caution" when flying over the eastern Pacific Ocean near Mexico, Central America, and parts of South America, citing "military activities."

Sheinbaum said her administration waited a couple of hours before the U.S. government provided "written" assurance that no military flights would enter Mexican airspace. She said the United States had not given Mexico advance notice of any military operations.

Once the U.S. government provided precise coordinates for where it was operating, Mexican authorities issued a statement saying the FAA advisory had no implications for Mexico.

## The Toluca landing

Images of a U.S. military transport airplane on the tarmac at Toluca's airport, approximately 39 miles (63 kilometers) west of Mexico City, circulated on social media over the weekend and drew calls for a government explanation.

Sen. Clemente Castañeda of the opposition Citizen Movement party posted on X asking for an accounting from the government. Castañeda noted that Mexico's senate is required to approve sending Mexican troops abroad or allowing foreign troops into Mexico.

Sheinbaum said the Toluca landing was a "logistical" operation that did not require senate approval. "The United States plane landed, public servants got on that plane and they went (to the U.S.) for training," Sheinbaum said. "Who authorized this? The Secretary of Defense."

Mexico's security cabinet posted on X Sunday night that such training operations "occur in conformity with established protocols and in adherence to the bilateral collaboration agreements."

## Sovereignty concerns and next steps

Both incidents highlighted the sensitivity created by the Trump administration's unilateral action to capture Maduro earlier in January and by subsequent offers and threats to deploy U.S. forces directly against drug cartels in Mexico.

Sheinbaum and Trump spoke again last week on the subject. Both governments have said they intend to continue collaborating on security, with Mexico insisting it will not accept violations of its sovereignty.

Security officials from both countries are scheduled to meet Friday, according to a joint statement released Thursday. The statement said the talks would aim to "continue delivering tangible actions to strengthen security cooperation and meaningful outcomes to counter cartels, and stop the illicit flow of fentanyl and weapons from crossing our shared border."
