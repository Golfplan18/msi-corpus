Child development and civic engagement advocates say the desire to volunteer as a family is widespread, and the benefits to children — confidence, social skills, a window onto lives different from their own — are real. But the organizational barriers are real too, making persistence, creativity, and sometimes a willingness to start something from scratch the price of entry.

NEW YORK — When Cami Teacoach set out to find volunteer work she could do with her 3-year-old son, most organizations said no. So she started her own.

As MSI has reported, [parents find ways to volunteer with young children despite nonprofit age limits](/articles/2025-12-26-parents-find-ways-to-volunteer-with-young-children-despite-nonprofit-age-limits) — a recurring challenge that pushes families toward improvisation. Many nonprofit organizations require volunteers to be at least 18, according to civic engagement advocates who work to match families with service opportunities.

"There's a million different ways to help people and volunteer. So many families want to do this and literally just don't know where to start," said Polly Lagana, executive director of Volunteer New York!, which connects people with service opportunities. "In a turbulent time in our world, families — and parents in particular — are very interested in showing their kids how they can help out and how they can give back."

## What children can do

Sapreet Saluja, executive director of New York Cares, which works with organizations to develop volunteer programs in New York City, said children excel at activities such as sorting coats, packing groceries and following through on tasks.

"I've been very impressed with the detail-orientation and the precision and the following of directions and the care that some kids I've seen, as young as six, take to the tasks that they're doing," Saluja said. "It's been explained to them that this is to benefit someone, and it's important, and they're following the directions and they want to get it just right. And in many cases, they're more detail-oriented than the adults, which I think is very inspiring."

Saluja said volunteering also opens a larger dialogue. "Kids are unbelievably curious," she said. "It helps you see even at a young age some of the challenges that society is facing and it gives you agency to know that you can be a part of the solution."

## When organizations say no, families build their own

Teacoach had reached out to many organizations in Pittsburgh looking for projects she could do with her young child, but most places refused.

"Everyone was like, 'A 3-year-old can't do that,'" Teacoach said. "And I was like, 'No, I swear, he really can if you would just give him a chance.'"

She eventually started VolunTOTs, a Pittsburgh group that creates service opportunities for children as young as 3. Participants and their parents pack 500 boxes of groceries to distribute to families in need, play bingo with seniors in nursing homes, and make dog treats for an animal rescue center, among other projects. Parents have told Teacoach their children's conduct improves after volunteering. "They feel so good about themselves, they were a helper, and that translates into better behavior," she said.

A Denver family took a similar path through an existing program. Aviva Davis was about eight years old when she and her brother Brendan began helping her parents deliver Meals on Wheels to senior citizens and medically frail individuals. Initially, they rode in the car with their parents and helped bring food to the door. Now 17, Davis still does monthly deliveries with her parents.

"It definitely opened my eyes to what the world is like outside of our bubble. We saw all sorts of different things and I saw not everyone lives the same way," Davis said. "But even at such a young age I could realize it's amazing what we're doing that we could help people that aren't as lucky as we were."

Her father, Seth Davis, said the deliveries created something harder to come by as children age. "It's a great chance for us to catch up as a family," he said. "When we're not all on our phones, you get some pretty cool quality time."

## Teen-led projects and social skills

Stephanie Bernaba's family started "Tough Cookies," a project where they bake and deliver cookies to veterans, when her son Matthew was in eighth grade and had to complete a service project for school.

Michael Bernaba, 14, a freshman at The Prout School in Wakefield, Rhode Island, said the visits mattered beyond the treats. "Going up to the houses, it was very nice, because they're mostly living alone. A lot of their family or friends died," he said. "It's just nice to be there and bring it to them ... They were very happy, especially for someone to bring them treats like that."

For Matthew Bernaba, now 15, the project helped him overcome shyness. "For the first couple of deliveries I was more to myself, and as we kept going, we talked more with the veterans and got to hear great stories from them," he said.

## Getting started

Lagana advised families to give children agency in choosing an activity. "Let them know what problem you're trying to fix in your community, and maybe one or two options of how that problem can be fixed," she said. For example, a parent might explain that some people don't have enough food, while other children in hospitals might lack blankets, and ask which cause the child wants to address.

Families can also organize projects collaboratively. Bonnie Davis — whose daughter Aviva and son Brendan began their Meals on Wheels work in childhood — organized a drive for menstrual supplies with neighboring families gathering in her backyard to assemble baskets. She also turned half of her son's graduation party into an assembly project for bean soup kits.

"It's a win for everybody. You get quality time, you're raising your children with what feels like good values, and people benefit," Davis said.
