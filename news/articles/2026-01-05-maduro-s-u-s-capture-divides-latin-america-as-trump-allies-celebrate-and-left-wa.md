The reactions laid bare how Trump's Latin America policy — which the administration calls the "Donroe Doctrine" — has divided the hemisphere into competing ideological camps, while raising immediate questions about whether Washington intends to support democratic outcomes in Venezuela or advance its own strategic priorities.

The U.S. capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro drew sharply divided reactions across Latin America on Saturday, with right-wing governments celebrating and left-leaning presidents condemning the operation as a dangerous revival of U.S. interventionism. President Donald Trump proclaimed at a news conference that "American dominance in the Western Hemisphere will never be questioned again," hours before Maduro was walked through the offices of the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration in New York.

Trump also vowed that Washington would "run" Venezuela and seize control of its oil sector. The episode exposed deep political fault lines from Mexico to Argentina as Trump-aligned governments have grown in number and influence across the region.

## Allied leaders hail the operation

Argentine President Javier Milei framed the divide in stark ideological terms. Supporters of Maduro's removal, he said, stood for "democracy, the defense of life, freedom and property." Those opposing it, he added, were "accomplices of a narco-terrorist and bloody dictatorship that has been a cancer for our region."

Ecuador's conservative President Daniel Noboa issued a direct warning to followers of the late Hugo Chávez, who founded Venezuela's Bolivarian revolution: "Your structure will completely collapse across the entire continent," Noboa said.

Chile's President-elect José Antonio Kast, who won a December election in which fears over Venezuelan immigration were prominent, called the U.S. operation "great news for the region."

## Left-wing governments condemn the action

Four left-leaning heads of state — Brazil's Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Mexico's Claudia Sheinbaum, Chile's Gabriel Boric, and Colombia's Gustavo Petro — issued sharp condemnations.

Lula said the operation set "an extremely dangerous precedent" and recalled, he said, "the worst moments of interference in the politics of Latin America." Sheinbaum warned it "jeopardizes regional stability." Boric said it "violated an essential pillar of international law." Petro called it "aggression against the sovereignty of Venezuela and of Latin America."

Trump has previously threatened or punished all four leaders for failing to comply with his administration's demands.

## Analysts describe a hemisphere being reshaped

Gimena Sanchez, Andes director for the Washington Office on Latin America, said the operation was part of a sustained push.

"The Trump administration in multiple different ways has been trying to reshape Latin American politics," Sanchez said. "They're showing their teeth in the whole region."

Trump has labeled this approach the "Donroe Doctrine" — a reference to President James Monroe's 19th-century assertion that the United States should dominate its sphere of influence. According to the Associated Press, the administration has since taking office launched naval strikes against alleged drug traffickers in the Caribbean, ordered a blockade on Venezuelan oil exports, and applied tariffs, sanctions, and military force to pressure regional governments on immigration, drug trafficking, strategic resources, and countering Russian and Chinese influence.

Alexander Gray, a senior fellow at the Atlantic Council, said the operation was consistent with that strategy.

"The president is going to be looking for allied and partner nations in the hemisphere who share his kind of broader ideological affinity," Gray said.

Not all of Trump's regional allies responded with enthusiasm. El Salvador's President Nayib Bukele, usually among Trump's most vocal supporters, posted a meme mocking Maduro after his capture but offered none of the jubilation seen from other regional counterparts, according to the Associated Press. El Salvador remains scarred by a civil war in which a U.S.-backed government fought leftist guerrillas.

## Questions over Venezuela's democratic future

The operation immediately raised questions about what political transition Washington envisions. Venezuela's 2024 election was widely seen as fraudulent, with the opposition claiming victory over Maduro. Trump announced he would work instead with Maduro's loyalist Vice President Delcy Rodríguez — bypassing those opposition results.

Bolivia's new conservative President Rodrigo Paz praised Maduro's removal insofar as it fulfilled, he said, "the true popular will" of Venezuelans. "Bolivia reaffirms that the way out for Venezuela is to respect the vote," Paz said.

Hours later, Trump moved in a different direction. Asked on Sunday when Venezuela would hold democratic elections, Trump replied: "I think we're looking more at getting it fixed."

Kevin Whitaker, a former deputy chief of mission for the State Department in Caracas, said the administration's posture raised a fundamental concern.

"The Trump administration, it appears at this point, is making decisions about the democratic future of Venezuela without referring back to the democratic result," Whitaker said.

## Broader warnings to the region

Beyond Venezuela, Trump used the moment to put other governments on notice. He said Cuba's Communist government "looks like it's ready to fall" and said "something's going to have to be done with Mexico," criticizing Sheinbaum's handling of drug cartels.

Trump also repeated allegations that Colombian President Petro "likes making cocaine" and warned "he's not going to be doing it very long." The allegations were not independently verified.

"We're in the business of having countries around us that are viable and successful, where the oil is allowed to really come out," Trump told reporters aboard Air Force One on Sunday. "It's our hemisphere."
