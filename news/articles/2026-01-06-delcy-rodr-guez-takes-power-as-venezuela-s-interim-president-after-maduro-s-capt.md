Venezuela's Delcy Rodríguez assumed the role of interim president Saturday after the United States seized Nicolás Maduro in a nighttime military operation, becoming the constitutional successor to her former boss after Venezuela's loyalist Supreme Court ordered her to take power and the country's military backed her ascension. Rodríguez, a 56-year-old lawyer and veteran of the socialist governments of Hugo Chávez and Maduro, had served as vice president since 2018.

Her first hours in power were marked by a sharp contradiction: a defiant televised address in which she called the Trump administration "extremists" was followed within a day by a conciliatory Instagram post inviting the United States to cooperate with Caracas, a reversal that left her precise intentions toward Washington unclear.

Whether Rodríguez will align with or resist the Trump administration remains an open question as she faces simultaneous pressure from Washington and from hardliners within Venezuela's ruling socialist movement, whose support she needs to consolidate her grip on power.

Venezuela's Delcy Rodríguez, the longtime vice president of the government of Nicolás Maduro, became interim president Saturday after Maduro was seized by U.S. forces in a nighttime military operation that left the South American country's political future uncertain. Venezuela's loyalist Supreme Court ordered her to assume the role, and the country's military backed her, according to the Associated Press.

Rodríguez, a 56-year-old lawyer educated in Britain and France, is a senior figure in the socialist movement that has governed Venezuela for more than two decades. She served as economic minister, foreign affairs minister, and petroleum minister before becoming vice president in 2018, and was next in the presidential line of succession at the time of Maduro's capture.

## A sharp shift in tone

Rodríguez's first public statements as interim leader placed her in direct confrontation with Washington. In a televised address surrounded by civilian officials and military leaders, she called the Trump administration "extremists" and maintained that Maduro remained Venezuela's rightful president.

"What is being done to Venezuela is an atrocity that violates international law," she said.

Trump announced Saturday morning that Secretary of State Marco Rubio had been in contact with Rodríguez and that she was "gracious" and would work with the American government. Rubio said she was someone the administration could work with, unlike Maduro. But by Sunday, Rubio told interviewers he did not see Rodríguez or her government as "legitimate" because Venezuela had never held free and fair elections.

Trump issued a direct threat on Sunday. If Rodríguez did not fall in line, he said, "she is going to pay a very big price, probably bigger than Maduro." He said he wanted her to provide "total access" to oil facilities and basic infrastructure.

Within hours, Rodríguez's tone shifted. In statements posted to Instagram, she said she hoped to build "respectful relations" with Trump and invited the U.S. to cooperate with Venezuela.

"We invite the US government to collaborate with us on an agenda of cooperation oriented towards shared development within the framework of international law to strengthen lasting community coexistence," she wrote.

## Her relationship with the military and Washington

Ronal Rodríguez, a spokesperson for the Venezuela Observatory of Rosario University in Bogotá, Colombia, said the interim president had cultivated an unusually durable position inside the Venezuelan government by building ties with the military.

"She has a very particular relationship with power," he said. "She has developed very strong ties with elements of the armed forces and has managed to establish lines of dialogue with them, largely on a transactional basis."

Rodríguez has avoided criminal indictment in the United States, though she faced U.S. sanctions during Trump's first term for her role in what the U.S. described as undermining Venezuelan democracy. Unlike many in Maduro's inner circle, her brother Jorge Rodríguez, who heads the Maduro-aligned National Assembly, also avoided U.S. prosecution.

The siblings' leftist credentials are rooted in personal history. Their father, a socialist leader, was arrested for his involvement in the 1976 kidnapping of American business owner William Niehous and later died in police custody, according to AP.

Rodríguez also developed working relationships with Republicans in the oil industry and on Wall Street who opposed U.S.-led regime change in Venezuela, the AP reported.

## Constitutional mechanics and an uncertain timeline

Venezuela's constitution requires an election within 30 days when a president becomes "permanently unavailable" — a provision that was followed after Hugo Chávez died of cancer in 2013.

The loyalist Supreme Court, however, classified Maduro's absence as "temporary," invoking a separate constitutional provision that sidesteps the election requirement. Under that provision, the vice president assumes power for up to 90 days, a period extendable to six months with a National Assembly vote. The court's order made no mention of the 180-day limit, leaving the duration of Rodríguez's tenure unclear.

Geoff Ramsey, a senior nonresident fellow at the Atlantic Council, a Washington research institute, said Rodríguez's initially firm posture toward the Trump administration may have been about maintaining credibility within Venezuela's fractured socialist ruling party rather than signaling genuine confrontation.

"She can't exactly expect to score points with her revolutionary peers if she presents herself as a patsy for U.S. interests," Ramsey said.
