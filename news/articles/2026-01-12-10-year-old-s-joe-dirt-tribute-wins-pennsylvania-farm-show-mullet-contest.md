A 10-year-old dressed in tribute to movie character Joe Dirt took top honors Monday at the Pennsylvania Farm Show's annual mullet contest in Harrisburg, where about 150 competitors vied before a crowd of more than a thousand spectators. Drew Fleschut of Dallas, Pennsylvania — wearing a red-and-black shirt and carrying the character's signature mop — claimed the top prize, the rear bumper of a Corvette, at the event billed as the day's "mane" attraction.

The contest, now in its third year and one of the few Farm Show events open to out-of-state entrants, has become a recurring celebration of a hairstyle the Oxford English Dictionary describes as slang that is "humorous and frequently derogatory" — the short-in-front, long-in-back cut once associated with Canadian hockey players and hair metal bands.

HARRISBURG, Pa. — Drew Fleschut, 10, of Dallas, Pennsylvania, took top honors Monday at the Pennsylvania Farm Show's annual mullet contest, claiming the event's prize — the rear bumper of a Corvette — with a tribute to movie character Joe Dirt that featured a red-and-black shirt and the character's signature mop.

About 150 competitors entered the contest in Harrisburg, drawing a crowd of more than a thousand spectators for what organizers called the day's "mane" attraction. The event, now in its third year, is one of the few at the Farm Show open to competitors from outside Pennsylvania.

Contestants were scored on the style of their cut, any props or accessories, their presentation, and their overall sense of commitment, said judge Brittany Goldberg, owner of Heavy Metal Hair Salon in Philadelphia.

"This is for fun," Goldberg said. "It's about the camaraderie and everyone having a laugh and a good time."

The competition included magic tricks, customized T-shirts, and at least one "skullet" — a mullet worn by a balding man. Some contestants danced the worm, some dabbed, and a few ripped off their shirts, pro-wrestling style.

Among the competitors was Ben Barley, 7, of Red Lion, who arrived wearing a T-shirt featuring his name and the words "MULLET LIFE 6-7." He said he had been working on his mullet for two years.

Brayden Shaner, 14, of Lancaster finished third in the teenager category. He said it had taken about four years to grow his mullet.

"I like it because it's different," Shaner said. "You don't see, walking through the grocery store, people with a mullet. I think the girls like it."

Kyle Wertman, 43, of Murrysville, who works in sales and service of industrial air compressors, said he was inspired to grow a mullet while watching old professional wrestling footage of Hacksaw Jim Duggan. He said he gets a lot of comments about it in his hometown.

"They like to fluff the curls in the back, 'Look at this thing, it's got a mind of its own,'" Wertman said.

Brittni Williamson of Harvey's Lake brought her 3-year-old son Mason, whose mullet formed naturally when the hair on the back of his head grew more quickly than the rest when he was a baby.

"We just clean it up in the front and keep the party going," Williamson said. Mason did not win but, Williamson said, he accomplished his New Year's resolution by feeding a cow at the show.

Though mullets have likely been around longer than there have been barbers, the Oxford English Dictionary credits hip-hop group the Beastie Boys with helping popularize the term with the song "Mullet Head" on their 1994 recording "Ill Communication." The dictionary describes the word as slang that is "humorous and frequently derogatory."
