## Houston's East End rallies in grief and defiance after ICE killing

**Subtype:** fact

- Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 52-year-old Mexican immigrant and construction employer, was fatally shot by an ICE agent on July 7 while driving to work in Houston's Magnolia Park neighborhood.
- The vigil on Canal Street has grown nightly since the shooting; hundreds of mourners have attended a public viewing and packed city council meetings demanding an independent investigation.

## Magnolia Park residents demand investigation as gentrification reshapes barrio

Ten days after Lorenzo Salgado Araujo was shot and killed on Canal Street, a wooden cross ringed with photographs stands at the corner, surrounded by votive candles painted with the Virgin of Guadalupe and Mexican flags alongside Texas ones. The vigil has grown every night since July 7, when two neighbors first nailed white roses to a utility pole.

"It could have been anybody," said Jesse E. Rodriguez, an artist who has lived in the neighborhood his whole life. "It could have been me or a sibling. My little brother buys breakfast right there before he goes to work."

Salgado Araujo, a Mexican national who had lived in the United States for 35 years, was driving to work with three others when the shooting occurred. He employed more than a dozen workers building homes across the region. The Department of Homeland Security and the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas confirmed that ICE officers conducting the operation were targeting two Guatemalan nationals — not Salgado Araujo.

As MSI previously reported, the disclosure that ICE agents were seeking other men has complicated the government's account of the incident. The officers involved were not wearing body cameras and have not provided evidence to corroborate their account that Salgado Araujo tried to run over an agent, according to DHS.

"Magnolia Park is a tight-knit community. Neighbors know each other and they help each other," said JoAnna Rodriguez, co-founder of the nonprofit Magnolia Park Arts and Community, located two blocks from the shooting site. "So when we heard what had happened, our first reaction was, 'Who was it?'"

The neighborhood, once called Houston's "Little Mexico," took shape in the early 1900s as Mexican families settled along the channel to build the docks and refineries that powered the city's growth. Thousands more arrived fleeing the Mexican Revolution. Jose Aranda, who teaches Mexican American literature at Rice University and grew up in Magnolia Park, said the area's identity was forged by Houston's economic geography — the shipping channel and oil refineries that demanded labor.

"This is why people continue to immigrate to these neighborhoods," Aranda said. "They're low-cost neighborhoods, but they're also situated economically in the places that Houston is most strong economically."

In recent years, the neighborhood has begun to change. Century-old homes built by immigrant families have increasingly been sold to developers who demolish them and erect townhomes costing half a million dollars or more. Between 2018 and 2023, Magnolia Park's Hispanic population shrank by 15%, according to Adela Cedillo, a historian at the University of Houston who has lived in the East End for 15 years. Cedillo said she has watched rents climb steadily even as services and infrastructure lag behind.

"Houston has this reputation that anything that's built can be easily unbuilt, destroyed," Aranda said. "It's very hard to hold on to historic homes and buildings because it's always been a developer's landscape. They control the politics."

At a public viewing held Thursday at a funeral home in the East End, hundreds of mourners filed past Salgado Araujo's casket for hours. His sons, 29-year-old Ronaldo and 27-year-old Lorenzo Jr., stood beside the casket the entire evening.

Activists have vowed to seek accountability through an independent investigation, holding rallies at city hall and "know your rights" trainings. The broader community has responded with protests featuring art and music as forms of healing.

Racial tensions between authorities and Magnolia Park residents are not new. In 1977, Joe Campos Torres, a 23-year-old Vietnam War veteran, was beaten by Houston police officers and drowned in the Buffalo Bayou, not far from where Salgado Araujo was killed. The officers received only probation, a sentence so light it sparked the Moody Park riot the following year, when simmering tensions boiled over into a violent clash between residents and police.

"Whether it's Joe Campos Torres at 23 or Araujo at 52, it's just senseless and so damaging to everybody," Aranda said.

He said growing up in Magnolia Park meant absorbing a wariness toward law enforcement passed down through generations. "You needed to be always strait-laced. You cannot really mess around with them because there was a way that barrios were intensely patrolled, and we just didn't have any faith that law enforcement would follow the rules."

Cedillo said the community's collective memory of Campos Torres' killing shaped how quickly residents responded to Salgado Araujo's death. "These stories are marked by segregation, racial profiling, police harassment," she said.

A neighbor who declined to share his name for fear of retaliation said: "Everybody knows that what happened is wrong. How will we not stand with our people, stand behind them, especially when something so vicious and ugly happened?"

Throughout the day, neighbors continue to gather at the corner where Salgado Araujo died, lighting candles, playing music, and trading phone numbers so they can check on one another. It is the same impulse, Cedillo said, that has always defined the neighborhood, whether the threat came from a squad car in 1977 or an unmarked SUV in 2026.

"We're not asking for much," Cedillo said. "We just want to be treated like humans, with due process and within the framework of the rule of law."
