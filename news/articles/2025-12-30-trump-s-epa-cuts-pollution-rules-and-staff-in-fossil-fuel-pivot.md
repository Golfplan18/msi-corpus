The Trump administration overhauled the Environmental Protection Agency in its first year, rolling back federal limits on air and water pollution, cutting the agency's workforce by roughly 20 percent and reorienting the agency toward fossil fuel promotion, the Associated Press reported Monday.

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin proposed overturning the landmark finding that climate change is a threat to human health, pledged to roll back dozens of environmental regulations and froze billions of dollars for clean energy programs. The agency's new direction marks a break from the EPA's founding mission under President Richard Nixon, who created it in 1970 after pollution was disrupting American life, with cities suffocating in smog and rivers turned into wastelands by industrial chemicals.

"It just constantly wants to pat the fossil fuel business on the back and turn back the clock to a pre-Richard Nixon era," said historian Douglas Brinkley.

Zeldin, a former New York congressman, has defended the changes as necessary to relieve an excessive economic burden. "We should not be causing … extreme economic pain for an individual or a family" because of policies aimed at "saving the planet," he told reporters at EPA headquarters in December.

EPA spokeswoman Brigit Hirsch said in a statement: "While the Biden EPA repeatedly attempted to usurp the U.S. Constitution and the rule of law to impose its 'Green New Scam,' the Trump EPA is laser-focused on achieving results for the American people while operating within the limits of the laws passed by Congress."

## Specific rollbacks and new priorities

Zeldin has announced plans to abandon soot pollution rules, loosen rules around harmful refrigerants, limit wetlands protections and weaken gas mileage rules. He would also exempt polluting industries and plants from federal emissions-reduction requirements.

Zeldin announced five pillars to guide EPA's work; four are economic goals, including energy dominance — the Trump administration's shorthand for expanded fossil fuel production — and boosting the auto industry.

Much of the EPA's new direction aligns with Project 2025, the Heritage Foundation road map that argued the agency should cut staffing, reduce regulations and end what it called a war on coal and other fossil fuels. Diana Furchtgott-Roth, director of Heritage's Center for Energy, Climate, and Environment, said many Biden-era regulations were "more harmful and restrictive than in any other period."

## Enforcement and staffing

The number of new civil environmental enforcement actions under Trump's EPA was roughly one-fifth the number recorded in the first eight months of the Biden administration, according to the nonprofit Environmental Integrity Project.

"You can effectively do a lot of deregulation if you just don't do enforcement," said Leif Fredrickson, visiting assistant professor of history at the University of Montana.

Hirsch disputed enforcement-count comparisons, saying they require work outside of the EPA and can bog staff down with burdensome legal agreements. She said the agency is focused on resolving violations and achieving compliance efficiently.

Zeldin cut EPA staffing by about 20 percent, to levels last seen in the mid-1980s. Justin Chen, president of the EPA's largest union, called the cuts "devastating," citing the dismantling of research and development offices at labs across the country and the firing of employees who signed a letter of dissent opposing the reductions.

## Green bank and environmental justice

Zeldin canceled a $20 billion green bank established under the Biden administration's landmark climate law to fund clean energy projects. He argued the fund was a scheme to funnel money to Democrat-aligned organizations with little oversight — allegations a federal judge rejected.

He also dismissed staff and canceled billions in grants for projects that fell under the diversity, equity and inclusion umbrella, eliminating programs Biden had prioritized to address chronic pollution that falls disproportionately on minority and low-income communities.

Matthew Tejada, a former EPA official who now works at the Natural Resources Defense Council, said: "I think it would be hard for them to make it any clearer to polluters in this country that they can go on about their business and not worry about EPA getting in their way."

## Reactions from health and legal experts

Scientists and public health experts say the rollbacks will lead to more pollutants in the environment, including mercury, lead and tiny airborne particles that can lodge in lungs, and will worsen atmospheric warming driving more frequent and costly extreme weather.

Christine Todd Whitman, who led the EPA under President George W. Bush, said watching Zeldin roll back laws protecting air and water has been "just depressing."

"It's tragic for our country. I worry about my grandchildren, of which I have seven. I worry about what their future is going to be if they don't have clean air, if they don't have clean water to drink," said Whitman, who joined a centrist third party in recent years.

Chris Frey, an EPA official under Biden, said the regulations Zeldin has targeted "offered benefits of avoided premature deaths, of avoided chronic illness … bad things that would not happen because of these rules."

Pat Parenteau, an environmental law expert and former director of the Environmental Law School at Vermont Law & Graduate School, said he sees little hope for what he called "the two most awful crises in the 21st century" — biodiversity loss and climate disruption.

"I don't see any hope for either one," he said. "I really don't. And I'll be long gone, but I think the world is in just for absolute catastrophe."
