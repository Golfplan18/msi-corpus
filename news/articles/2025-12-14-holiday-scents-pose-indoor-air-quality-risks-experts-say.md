Holiday candles, incense, and fireplaces release air pollutants that can trigger allergy-like symptoms and contribute to long-term respiratory harm, a Johns Hopkins pulmonologist said. Dr. Meredith McCormack, director of the pulmonary and critical care medicine division at Johns Hopkins University's medical school, said the risk depends on the source, duration, and intensity of exposure — and urged people who enjoy seasonal fragrances to practice moderation and improve ventilation.

People in the Northern Hemisphere spend more time indoors during the end-of-year holidays, when closed windows allow pollutants to concentrate at levels significantly above outdoor air, according to the American Lung Association. Children, older adults, and people with heart or lung conditions face the greatest risk.

"Clean air is fragrance free," said McCormack, who has studied air quality and lung health for more than 20 years. "If having seasonal scents is part of your tradition or evokes feelings of nostalgia, maybe think about it in moderation."

## What goes into indoor air

All flames release chemicals that may cause allergy-like symptoms or contribute to long-term respiratory problems if inhaled in sufficient quantities. Active fireplaces and gas appliances release tiny airborne particles that can penetrate the lungs and chemicals like nitrogen dioxide — a major component of smog — according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. Cleaning products, air fresheners, and candles also emit pollutants at varying concentrations.

As MSI previously reported, [holiday candles and fireplaces can worsen indoor air quality](/articles/2025-12-14-holiday-candles-and-fireplaces-can-worsen-indoor-air-quality) across a range of common seasonal products. Experts add that some pollutants carry no detectable odor, meaning unscented products can still degrade air quality indoors.

## Who is most vulnerable

Polluted air affects everyone, but not equally. Children, older adults, minority populations, and people of low socioeconomic status are more likely to be affected by poor air quality because of either physiological vulnerabilities or higher exposure, according to the EPA.

Children are more susceptible because of their lung size, which means they get a greater dose of exposure relative to their body size, McCormack said. Pollutants inside the home also pose a greater hazard to people with heart or lung conditions, including asthma, she said.

"The more risk factors you have, the more harmful air pollution or poor air quality indoors can be," McCormack said.

Signs of respiratory irritation include coughing, shortness of breath, headaches, a runny nose, and sneezing. Experts advise stopping use of pollutant-releasing products or immediately ventilating rooms if symptoms occur.

## Practical steps to reduce exposure

Buying products with fewer ingredients, opening windows when temperatures allow, and using air purifiers with HEPA filters are ways to reduce exposure to pollutants from indoor fireplaces, appliances, and candle displays, McCormack said. She also recommends switching on kitchen exhaust fans before starting a gas-powered stovetop and using the back burners so the vent can more easily capture pollutants. Setting polite limits with guests who smoke cigarettes or other tobacco products is also advisable, she said.

All lit candles give off air pollutants regardless of what they are made of, experts note — whether soy-based, beeswax, or petroleum-based paraffin.

Rachael Lewis-Abbott, a member of the Indoor Air Quality Association — an organization for professionals who identify and address air quality problems — said most people pay little attention to what they breathe indoors until a visible problem such as a gas leak or mold develops.

"It is out of sight, out of mind," she said.

McCormack offered a simple frame for why incremental changes carry real weight.

"Small improvements in air quality can have measurable health benefits," she said. "Similarly to if we exercise and eat a little better, we can be healthier."
