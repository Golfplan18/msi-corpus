Hundreds of Indigenous people representing 14 groups have blocked a Cargill shipping terminal in Santarem, northern Brazil, for nearly a week, protesting President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's decree allowing private operators to control waterway maintenance and dredging. Indigenous leaders say the government violated Brazilian law and international conventions by failing to consult affected communities.

The blockade highlights a conflict between Brazil's environmental pledges and its economic development strategy. Government officials frame waterway privatization as necessary to move agricultural exports; Indigenous peoples warn the policy threatens the Amazon and their sovereignty.

Hundreds of Indigenous people representing 14 ethnic groups maintained a blockade of a Cargill shipping terminal in Santarem, northern Brazil, demanding President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva revoke an August decree that would allow private operators to manage waterway maintenance and dredging on the Tapajos River.

The Tapajos and Arapiuns Indigenous Council, which leads the protest, says the government violated Brazilian law and international conventions by failing to consult affected communities. "All we want is awareness and for the government to acknowledge that it made a mistake and must respect our rights," said Indigenous leader Auricelia Arapiun.

## The decree and the dispute

Lula's decree shifts responsibility for waterway maintenance and vessel traffic management to private operators through federal concessions. Indigenous leaders warn that dredging projects could devastate their territories and the Amazon rainforest's environmental balance.

Arapiun pointed to what she described as a contradiction in the government's position. "There is no point in talking about a climate plan or pledging to defend the environment, the Amazon or the climate while attacking them more than protecting them. The rhetoric is one thing. The practice is another," she said, referencing commitments Brazil made at the U.N. climate conference held in Belem last year.

The blockade has disrupted operations at the Cargill terminal. Cargill said it respects the right to protest but stated the company "has no authority or control" over the waterway concession policy.

## Government response and planned next steps

Brazil's Presidential Secretariat-General, the office responsible for dialogue with social movements, said it is committed to conducting consultation with affected communities before any action on the Tapajos waterway concession and that the consultation process is ongoing.

Indigenous groups said they held a virtual meeting with government officials and expect them to attend an in-person assembly at the protest site on Friday. The Presidential Secretariat did not confirm whether it would attend.

## Economic development pressures

The Tapajos River moves approximately 41 million metric tons of cargo annually, according to a statement from the office of Lula's Chief of Staff. Infrastructure analyst Renata Utsunomiya said the proposed waterway concession is tied to broader Amazon development, including a planned railway, driven by demand from soy and corn producers seeking an export corridor.

"A waterway concession with expanded dredging and shipping capacity, combined with the Ferrograo railway, would increase pressure on Indigenous territories and protected areas, fueling deforestation, land grabbing and other long-standing impacts in the region," Utsunomiya said.
