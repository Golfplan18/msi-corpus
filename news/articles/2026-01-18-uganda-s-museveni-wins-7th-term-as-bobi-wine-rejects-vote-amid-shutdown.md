Uganda’s President Yoweri Museveni won a seventh term with 71.65% of the vote, according to official results published Saturday, as opposition leader Bobi Wine rejected the outcome and called for peaceful protests.

Wine, who is also known as Kyagulanyi Ssentamu, received 24.72% of the vote, the final results showed. In comments reported by the Associated Press, Wine condemned what he described as an unfair electoral process and alleged abductions of his polling agents before voting started in parts of the country.

Wine rejected the “fake” results and urged Ugandans to peacefully protest until the “rightful results are announced.” He said he had to escape to avoid arrest after security forces stormed his house Friday night. Police spokesperson Kituuma Rusoke said Wine was “not under arrest” and was free to leave his house, but that there was “controlled access” for others trying to enter the property to prevent people from using the premises to incite violence.

The election took place amid a days-long internet shutdown and also featured failures of biometric voter identification machines used to verify voters. Electoral officials faced questions after the machines failed on Thursday, causing delays in the start of voting in urban areas including Kampala, opposition strongholds, and polling officials then used hard-copy registers of voters.

Wine and others pointed to those problems as part of their case against the result. The Associated Press reported that the failure of the biometric machines is likely to be the basis for any legal challenges to the official outcome. Museveni said he agreed with the electoral commission’s plan to revert to paper records of voters after the biometric machines failed, while Wine alleged fraud, including “massive ballot stuffing,” and said his party’s polling agents were abducted to give an unfair advantage to the ruling party.

An African Union observer mission led by former Nigerian President Goodluck Jonathan said it did not find evidence supporting the ballot-stuffing allegation. Jonathan told journalists Saturday that the mission did not find “any evidence of ballot stuffing” in the polling stations the team observed, and he urged electoral authorities to test biometric machines in advance to prevent failures and delays.

Local observers raised concerns about the environment around voting. Livingstone Sewanyana, head of the Foundation for Human Rights Initiative in Kampala, said the election climate was characterized by “fear and tension among the electorate,” and that some people chose not to participate in the process.

Voter turnout was 52%, the lowest since Uganda returned in 2006 to multi-party politics. Museveni, 81, has stayed in power over the years by rewriting election and governance rules, including the removal of term limits and age restrictions from the constitution, according to the report.

The Associated Press also noted that veteran opposition figure Kizza Besigye remained in prison after treason charges he said were politically motivated. Separately, academic and Observer newspaper columnist Yusuf Serunkuma told AP that Wine “didn’t stand a chance” against Museveni and said Museveni has “quite successfully emasculated the opposition.”

The Associated Press reported that to implement the internet shutdown, the Uganda Communications Commission directed internet service providers to suspend access over an unspecified threat to national security; service providers complied even though the directive lacked legal weight without a declared state of emergency. The shutdown stayed in effect from Tuesday to late Saturday and disrupted businesses including sports betting shops and Uber drivers.

Wine said security forces followed him during the campaign and harassed his supporters, using tear gas against them. The report said he campaigned wearing a flak jacket and helmet due to security fears, and that Uganda has not seen a peaceful transfer of presidential power since independence from British colonial rule six decades ago.
