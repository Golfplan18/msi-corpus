The episode has revived questions among foreign policy analysts about whether Washington and Moscow may be tacitly renegotiating their spheres of influence — with Venezuela ceded to the United States in exchange for a freer hand for Russia in Ukraine — at a moment when U.S.-led peace talks over the conflict enter what officials describe as a pivotal stage.

The U.S. military operation that captured Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro deals Russia a significant strategic loss in the Western Hemisphere while simultaneously handing Moscow fresh arguments to justify its war in Ukraine, analysts and Russian officials told the Associated Press.

Russian President Vladimir Putin had not commented publicly on the operation as of Wednesday. His diplomats condemned it as a blatant act of aggression. Dmitry Medvedev, Russia's former president and deputy chairman of the presidential Security Council, offered a more layered response — denouncing the action as a violation of international law while complimenting President Donald Trump's assertiveness.

"Even though Trump's action is completely unlawful, he cannot be denied a certain consistency — he and his team are very aggressively upholding their country's national interests," Medvedev said.

Medvedev also noted that after its action in Venezuela, the U.S. has "nothing to formally reproach our country for."

## Russia's strategic losses

Russia stands to lose both strategic positioning and economic investment as a result of the Maduro ouster. The Kremlin had invested heavily in Venezuela's oil industry and used the country as a platform for projecting military force in the Western Hemisphere, dispatching nuclear-capable Tu-160 bombers to Venezuela as recently as 2018. On Wednesday, the U.S. said it seized two sanctioned oil tankers linked to Venezuela, including one flagged to Russia, in the North Atlantic.

The Maduro ouster follows what analysts have described as a pattern of Kremlin setbacks: Syria's former President Bashar Assad fell in 2024, and Iran sustained significant U.S. and Israeli military strikes last year. In each case, Moscow proved unable to protect an allied government.

Military experts have said any attempt by Russia to establish a permanent military foothold in the Western Hemisphere would face overwhelming logistical challenges — suggesting that whatever influence Moscow had built in Venezuela was always more symbolic than operational.

## A sphere-of-influence question

The operation has revived questions about whether Washington and Moscow may have quietly negotiated boundaries between their respective zones of influence — and whether Venezuela was ceded as part of any such understanding.

Fiona Hill, who oversaw Russia and Europe on Trump's National Security Council during his first term, testified before Congress in 2019 that Russia had signaled its willingness to trade its Venezuela influence for a freer hand in Europe. She told the AP that Russia's then-ambassador to Washington, Anatoly Antonov, "hinted ... many times" to her that Russia could cede its influence in Venezuela to the U.S. in exchange for a sphere of influence in Europe.

Hill said she was sent to Moscow in April 2019 to convey that "nobody's interested. ... Ukraine and Venezuela are not related to each other."

Hill said she did not know whether the calculus had changed under the second Trump administration. She noted that officials involved in "restraining" Trump in his first term are no longer in place.

"Who knows what Witkoff and others have been chatting about recently?" Hill said, referring to Trump envoy Steve Witkoff.

## Advance notice?

Before Maduro was captured, the AP reported that Russia had started evacuating families of diplomats from Venezuela. Hill said it would not be "implausible" that Witkoff gave Moscow a "courtesy heads-up" before the operation.

Sam Greene, a Russia expert at King's College London, warned that the episode may point to a broader arrangement among the major powers. "My worry is that it may be part of a tacit agreement, by which Washington, Moscow and Beijing agree not to deter one another against interventions in their putative spheres of influence," Greene wrote on X.

## Moscow's narrative gains

Kremlin-connected analysts said the U.S. action in Venezuela bolsters Russia's framing of its invasion of Ukraine. Fyodor Lukyanov, a Moscow-based foreign policy expert with Kremlin connections, argued that the episode creates a favorable precedent for Russia. "If we consider what's happening from the perspective of setting a precedent, then we couldn't ask for anything better, and this includes Trump's conviction that the authorities in Venezuela must be approved by Washington," Lukyanov said.

Hill noted that Maduro's capture makes it harder for countries to condemn Russia's action in Ukraine because "we've just had a situation where the U.S. has taken over — or at least decapitated the government of another country — using fiction." A federal indictment accuses Maduro and others of allegedly working with drug cartels to facilitate the shipment of thousands of tons of cocaine into the U.S.

Hard-line nationalist ideologue Alexander Dugin argued the U.S. action created new urgency for Moscow to accelerate its Ukraine offensive. "Ukraine under our full control is our pass to the Great Powers club," Dugin wrote.

## NATO and the Greenland factor

Trump's stated interest in acquiring Greenland from NATO ally Denmark adds a further complication. That ambition, the AP reported, threatens to distract alliance members from their efforts to support Ukraine and provide it with security guarantees at the moment when U.S.-led peace negotiations enter a pivotal stage.

Putin himself had cited NATO's eastward expansion as a core justification for sending troops into Ukraine. "We have made it clear and unambiguous that further eastward expansion of NATO is unacceptable," Putin said shortly before ordering the full-scale invasion on Feb. 24, 2022.
