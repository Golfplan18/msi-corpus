## Trump signs vaccine order; CDC guidance remains, pediatricians report confusion

**Subtype:** fact

- President Donald Trump's executive order on vaccines cannot change federal vaccine recommendations and is instead sowing confusion, according to public health experts.
- The order attempts to reinstate childhood vaccine schedule changes that HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. made earlier in 2026 and that a federal judge later halted.
- Pediatricians warn that parental confusion spurred by the order could delay vaccinations and increase illness among children.
- Experts say the order's religious-freedom language could pressure states to widen exemptions for school vaccine requirements.
- Forty-six states and the District of Columbia already provide religious exemptions for school vaccine requirements; all states provide medical exemptions.

## Pediatricians warn order could delay childhood vaccinations

The order was framed by the president as an effort to revisit the childhood vaccine schedule — a stance long shared by Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., a vaccine opponent whom the president has publicly backed. But an executive order can only offer guidance to agencies; it does not change laws or set recommendations. Recommendations from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the federal agency that sets vaccine guidance, remain the same.

"Nothing's changed," said Noele Nelson, a public health professor at Cornell University and former pediatrician. The primary effect of the order has been "adding unnecessary confusion" and "undermining trust in vaccines without any change in the scientific evidence," she said.

While the science and the recommendations are unchanged, "what is going to change is more people are going to be confused and scared," said Vincent Iannelli, a pediatrician near Dallas and founder of Vaxopedia, a resource on vaccines.

The president is an influential figure, and his inaccurate comments about the dangers of vaccines and a long-disproven connection to autism can have resounding effects, said Wendy Parmet, faculty co-director of the Center for Health Policy and Law at Northeastern University.

"People hear Trump is doing this and they think, 'My God, he's powerful. He's doing it. Nobody will be able to have hepatitis B vaccines.' That isn't true, but it's confusing, and it can sow more distrust," Parmet said.

Trump also lacks the authority to recommend spacing doses, Parmet said: "The president can't tell doctors to say to parents: 'Why don't you come back in a week and have the second shot?' But saying it can certainly lead some parents, maybe many parents, to start asking their doctors for multiple visits."

Adding four or five visits for each round of vaccination would make pediatricians' schedules busier, and insurance frequently does not cover additional vaccination visits. The added appointments also raise the possibility of errors as pediatricians track which vaccines have and have not been administered.

"It also means that they have to come back to the office more, which means they might be exposed to more illnesses," Iannelli said. "The biggest risk is they're going to delay getting protected from that illness that the vaccine protects them from."

Vaccines are spaced the way they are based on decades of evidence, Nelson said: "It provides the best immune response to the vaccine, and the spacing is not arbitrary. For some vaccines that are considered to have lifelong immunity — like hepatitis A, hepatitis B, measles — spacing is critical."

Trump's rhetoric also increases stigma around autism and may encourage families to seek out unproven treatments.

"Nobody talks enough about how much it hurts kids with autism," Iannelli said. "Their whole framing about how vaccines cause autism — it makes people think that their kids are damaged. It pushes them into trying unproven and dangerous therapies."

Trump also invoked religious freedom in the order, indicating the administration may put pressure on states to reduce vaccination mandates for school attendance. The administration has previously pushed its priorities around "parental authority" on vaccines in communication with states, and it has placed religious freedom at the heart of its health policies.

There are no federal mandates on vaccination outside of the military, and states set vaccination requirements for school attendance. Forty-six states and the District of Columbia already have religious exemptions for vaccine requirements; all states have medical exemptions.

"This potentially could put pressure on states that don't have [religious] exemptions right now," Nelson said. But there would likely be pushback and legal challenges if the administration tried to push states to change their vaccine laws, she said.

The few states that don't have religious exemptions have already litigated the issue. "New York and California and Connecticut, in particular, have been litigating and litigating and litigating, and they've been winning and winning and winning on these vaccine cases," Parmet said. "And I don't see those states suddenly folding. If Trump tries to coerce the states more, it will get litigated again."

Concerns about vaccines cycle through the population, Iannelli said. As soon as a disease is brought under control through vaccination, public concern fades, immunization rates drop, the disease resurges, and parents again seek vaccination. "It happens every time," he said. Children fall ill during the gap before coverage recovers, he said.

With announcements like Trump's, "people are primed to listen because they're already scared," Iannelli said. "Then they hear something like this, and it reinforces the idea that they should be scared." Frequently, he said, parents don't even know why they are scared; they just know that they are.

Recently, he said, parents visiting his practice said they didn't think they would get their child vaccinated that day. He asked what their concerns were; they worried about information they had seen online and thought it might be better to split up the shots. Iannelli listened and told them he could do that, but they would need to schedule several follow-up visits. Tetanus is all around, he added, and there has been an increase in measles. The parents said: "Oh — let's just get them."

"It helps you to address it and reassure them, and you just keep asking questions and talking," Iannelli said. Pediatricians shouldn't lecture hesitant families or kick them out of their practices, he said; they should use "motivational interviewing," where they determine the reasons for a patient's hesitation and help patients find the reasons to change.

"As pediatricians, that's our lane," he said, before adding: "Parents don't need a lecture; they just want to talk about their concerns." Concerns, he said, that are being amplified by misinformation spread at the highest levels of US government. And it doesn't take much time to listen and respond, he said. "You don't want to spend a half hour on it. You want to spend a couple minutes on it. And then, after a couple visits, it works."
