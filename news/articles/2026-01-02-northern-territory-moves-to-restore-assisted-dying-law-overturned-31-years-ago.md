Australia's Northern Territory government announced Friday that lawmakers would consider a bill legalizing doctor-assisted dying by mid-2026, a move that would make the territory the last Australian jurisdiction to adopt a law it first enacted — and then lost — three decades ago.

The announcement comes 31 years after the Northern Territory enacted the world's first voluntary euthanasia law in 1995, only to have the Australian Parliament overturn it two years later. Every Australian state has since enacted assisted dying legislation, and the Australian Capital Territory passed its own law in 2024. The Northern Territory now stands as the final holdout among Australia's eight states and territories.

## The bill and how it would proceed

Northern Territory Attorney-General Marie-Clare Boothby said lawmakers would consider a bill by mid-2026 under a conscience vote, meaning legislators would not be required to follow a party line.

"Legislating for the rights of the terminally ill is one of the most sensitive and complex reforms any government can undertake," Boothby said in a statement. "We're taking the time to get this right. We're working carefully and consultatively — not rushing it — and we are committed to getting the balance right."

Boothby said drafting of the bill was underway. "We won't shy away from difficult issues and we are committed to progressing these reforms carefully, thoughtfully and responsibly," she said.

A Northern Territory parliamentary committee recommended in September 2025 that a doctor-assisted suicide law be adopted, and also recommended a public education campaign to raise awareness and counter misinformation, particularly in remote and Indigenous communities.

## Historical context

The original Northern Territory law, enacted in 1995, was the first of its kind anywhere in the world. Four terminally ill patients were legally helped to die under its provisions before the Australian Parliament overturned the law in 1997. The federal parliament later lifted the ban on territories passing their own assisted dying laws, clearing the legal path for both the Northern Territory and the Australian Capital Territory to legislate.

## Distinct challenges in a vast, diverse territory

The Northern Territory presents challenges that set it apart from Australia's states. Its population of approximately 260,000 people is the smallest of any Australian jurisdiction, scattered across an area nearly the size of France. Indigenous people account for more than a quarter of the territory's population, compared with 3.8% of Australia's overall population at the 2021 census.

Indigenous views on voluntary euthanasia vary. Some Indigenous people express suspicion of the medical system and fear that they could be killed against their wishes. Others advocate for equitable access to assisted dying with strong cultural safeguards. The territory also contends with conflicting cultural beliefs about death and a large population whose first language is not English.

Northern Territory Legislative Assembly Speaker Robyn Lambley said she did not believe the territory was prepared to move forward. She said establishing adequate education about an assisted dying law for Indigenous people was "probably unrealistic."

"I don't think we're ready," Lambley said in an online post. "Maybe we'll never be ready in the Territory. Aboriginal people have reluctance around accessing health services for anything, even having babies."

"It will be a disaster. I don't think it will work. We just need to take it at our own pace," she added.
