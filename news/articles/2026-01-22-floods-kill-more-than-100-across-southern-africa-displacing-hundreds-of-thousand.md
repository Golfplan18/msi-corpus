Torrential rains and severe flooding across southern Africa have killed more than 100 people in Mozambique, South Africa, and Zimbabwe since late last year, destroyed thousands of homes, and caused tens of millions of dollars in damage to one of Africa's premier wildlife parks. More than half a million people across Mozambique have been affected by the flooding, with authorities expecting the death toll to rise as rescue operations continue.

The disaster has exposed the region's vulnerability to extreme weather, while authorities work to prevent disease outbreaks and food shortages among the millions displaced.

## Deaths and Displacement

South Africa reported more than 30 deaths from the flooding, while Zimbabwe reported at least 70. In Mozambique, 13 deaths have been directly attributed to flooding over the past two weeks, though authorities believe the actual toll is higher.

The floods have displaced more than 300,000 people in Mozambique's Gaza province alone, with more than half a million affected across the country. The Gaza provincial capital of Xai-Xai and the nearby agricultural town of Chokwe have been submerged by the floods, with only the tips of rooftops visible in some areas. The tourist town of Marracuene, north of the capital Maputo, is surrounded by water and effectively cut off.

## Kruger National Park Under Siege

In South Africa, Kruger National Park — one of Africa's top wildlife reserves covering nearly 20,000 square kilometers — has sustained damage amounting to tens of millions of dollars. The flooding washed away roads and bridges, destroyed staff accommodation and tourist facilities, and parts of the park have been totally cut off, Environment Minister Willie Aucamp said.

The damage is so severe that it will likely take years to fully recover, Aucamp said. A special fund has been set up to rebuild the park, which often attracts more than 1 million visitors a year.

No deaths were reported in the park itself, though hundreds of tourists and staff were evacuated from flood-stricken areas, some by helicopter. While animals generally move to higher areas during flooding, the extent of the impact on the park's wildlife — including critically endangered black rhinos — is not yet clear.

## Rescue and Long-Term Threats

The South African Air Force has deployed helicopters to rescue people stranded in trees and on building rooftops across the northern provinces of Mpumalanga and Limpopo. In cross-border operations, the military said it has rescued nearly 500 stranded people in Mozambique.

South Africa has declared a national disaster, and authorities are concerned about the stability of a dam in the northeast that is under pressure from the flooding. Safety engineers have been sent to the site and evacuations have been ordered in case it breaches.

Humanitarian agencies warn of increased hunger and disease from the flooding. The extreme weather has wiped out crops that millions of small-scale farmers depend on for survival. Water-borne cholera poses a threat following major flooding.

The UN Children's Fund said children are especially vulnerable among the more than half a million people affected in Mozambique, with access to clean water, food and healthcare uncertain for those in displaced communities.
