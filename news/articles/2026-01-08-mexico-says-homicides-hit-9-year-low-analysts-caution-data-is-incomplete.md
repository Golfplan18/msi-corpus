If confirmed by independent sources, the decline would mark the sharpest sustained reduction in Mexican homicide rates since violence surged following the launch of a military-led drug war in 2006 — though analysts note that cartel consolidation, potential undercounting, and more than 133,000 officially missing persons complicate the country's true security picture.

MEXICO CITY — Mexico's government reported Thursday that the country recorded 17.5 murders per 100,000 inhabitants in 2025, the lowest homicide rate since 2016, presenting the figures as evidence that President Claudia Sheinbaum's security strategy is producing results. The rate compares with 29 killings per 100,000 people in 2018, when Mexico recorded its highest homicide level in two decades.

Independent analysts acknowledged the downward trend but warned that incomplete official data and a rising count of missing persons make any full assessment impossible.

During her daily news conference, Sheinbaum said homicides dropped 40% between September 2024 — the month before she took office — and December 2025.

"This means 34 fewer homicides every day, and it is the lowest figure since 2016," Sheinbaum said.

She attributed the decline to coordination among security forces, justice institutions, prosecutors' offices, and state governors. After taking office on Oct. 1, 2024, Sheinbaum moved away from the "hugs, not bullets" approach of her predecessor, Andrés Manuel López Obrador, prioritizing intelligence work and interagency coordination amid pressure from the United States.

The figures had not been independently confirmed as of Thursday. Mexico's public security secretariat had not yet published complete 2025 homicide data. The national statistics institute, considered a more reliable source because it bases its count on death certificates, publishes its figures annually but with a monthslong delay.

## Analysts sound caution on the data

Lisa Sánchez, director of the nongovernmental organization Mexico United Against Crime, said homicides are down but cautioned that not all of the necessary data is available to make meaningful comparisons. "We don't know how they're compiled and handled," she said.

Sánchez noted that official figures of missing people keep rising and have reached more than 133,000. She said some killings may be undercounted because some missing people may be dead or because some violent deaths are recorded under other categories — such as accidents — instead of homicides.

Security analyst David Saucedo offered a separate caution: violence may be decreasing in some areas not because of government strategy but because criminal groups have consolidated control, reducing open conflict after eliminating rivals — a pattern, he said, widely discussed by researchers.

## Historical context

Mexico's homicide rate began rising sharply in 2006, when then-President Felipe Calderón of the conservative PAN party launched a military-led campaign against drug cartels. Killings kept rising and peaked during the administration of López Obrador, who governed from 2018 to 2024.

Despite the reported decline, violence linked to organized crime remains a reality in several states, including Sinaloa, Michoacán, Jalisco, and Guanajuato, where multiple drug cartels operate.
