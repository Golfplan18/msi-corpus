BEIRUT — Lebanon's economy minister said Thursday that disarming non-state armed groups and asserting full state control are as important as financial reforms for the country's economic recovery, speaking after a cabinet session in which the Lebanese military reported completing the first phase of a plan to expand its deployment in southern Lebanon.

"You need economic reforms, but you also need security and political reforms," Economy Minister Amer Bisat told The Associated Press.

The remarks underscore the dual burden on Lebanon's reformist government: winning over foreign investors and international financial institutions while consolidating state authority after a 2024 war the World Bank estimates caused $11 billion in damages and economic losses — all against the backdrop of a financial crisis dating to 2019 that years of talks with the International Monetary Fund have failed to resolve.

## Military progress disputed

Lebanon's military said Thursday it has completed the first phase of the plan to disarm Hezbollah and other non-state groups and expand its presence in the south. Israel disputed the assertion, maintaining that Hezbollah is still present and rearming in areas the Lebanese army said it now fully controls.

Bisat said asserting full sovereignty extends beyond military deployment in the south.

"(It) is also the control of the borders, control of the airport, control over smuggling, money-laundering, terrorist activities," he said, adding that such steps are essential to rebuilding investor confidence.

## A crisis compounded by conflict

Lebanon fell into a protracted financial crisis in 2019 following decades of corruption and mismanagement. The Israel-Hezbollah war in 2024 battered large parts of the country and deepened that economic hole, with the World Bank estimating $11 billion in combined damages and losses.

Western governments and Gulf Arab monarchies that once poured large sums into Lebanon have said investment will not resume without meaningful economic and security reforms. Gulf states are slowly rebuilding ties with Beirut after previously cutting them to push back against Hezbollah's influence.

Bisat cited the fall of the Assad government in Syria and Gulf states' appetite for international investment as they diversify their economies as openings Lebanon must move quickly to exploit — but only if reforms advance.

"Waiting is not an option. Precisely because time is not our friend," he said.

## Fiscal gap law and IMF impasse

Bisat, a member of Prime Minister Nawaf Salam's reformist government, endorsed a draft fiscal gap law intended to determine the extent of losses — estimated to be in the tens of billions of dollars — suffered by Lebanese banks during the 2019 financial meltdown, and to establish a mechanism for returning depositors' funds that were wiped out at the time.

The draft law has drawn criticism from multiple directions, and it is unclear whether parliament will pass it.

"This is an extremely important piece of legislation, without which this economy just will not be able to take off," Bisat said. He described the law not as a final solution but as a framework for beginning serious discussions. "This problem is extremely complicated financially. The size of the gap is very large."

Years of IMF bailout negotiations have produced no deal. Bisat said Lebanon's credibility deficit is the central obstacle.

"We have a credibility gap, we need an international framework to help us solve our problems," he said. "The days in which people help us without us doing our homework are gone."
