## 2022 federal court order has kept gray wolves protected across most of lower 48

President Donald Trump signed an executive order on Friday directing Interior Secretary Doug Burgum to determine within three months whether gray wolves and Mexican gray wolves have recovered enough to be de-listed from the Endangered Species Act, and to consider whether the agency could make it easier to authorize killing them.

The order also directs federal authorities to urge states to remove protections for gray wolves and loosen local restrictions on wolf killing, moves that drew immediate criticism from environmental groups. "Once again, we see this administration attempting to override the checks and balances provided by bedrock environmental laws like the Endangered Species Act in order to create industry carveouts and distract from other bad policies that are proving unpopular," said Leia Barnett, New Mexico conservation lead for WildEarth Guardians. "Directing federal agencies to pressure states into rolling back their own wildlife protections isn't just bad policy, it's an attempt to bypass the very framework Congress built to keep species recovery decisions insulated from political whims."

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has already attempted to de-list the gray wolf — a decision currently tied up in litigation. A 2022 federal court order has left gray wolves protected by the ESA across most of their range in the lower 48 states. The major exception is in the northern Rocky Mountain states of Montana, Idaho and Wyoming, where states manage wolves. In Minnesota, wolves are classified as "threatened" rather than "endangered."

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has not attempted to de-list the Mexican gray wolf, a rare sub-species that was reintroduced to New Mexico and Arizona in 1998. The recovery plan requires a population average of more than 320 animals for four years in a row before federal authorities consider de-listing. Arizona and New Mexico counted a total of 319 animals at the end of 2025, triggering the first year officials can begin counting toward that threshold.

Michael Robinson, an advocate with the Center for Biological Diversity, said Mexican gray wolves face "dire threats from lack of genetic diversity" and warned that premature de-listing would likely invite challenge from environmental groups. "We're going to be fighting every step of the way, with information, with science," Robinson said. "That should be the core of the decision-making process."

The federal government systematically trapped and killed wolves in the United States through the late 19th and early 20th centuries, pushing them to the brink of extirpation from the contiguous states, largely to protect livestock from predation. Ranchers still largely view the animals as a nuisance that threatens their already slim profit margins, and Trump's order was part of a broader series of measures aimed at protecting ranchers and American beef production.

At the signing ceremony, Trump appeared to suggest his order would make it easier to shoot wolves despite the fact that most remain protected. "You can shoot them as of today?" Trump asked, referring to Mexican gray wolves while talking with an Arizona rancher. The comment drew laughter. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins interjected to say that Burgum "has to do a little magic, but yes."

Trump returned to the issue later in the ceremony. A rancher told the president that people in Washington state struggle with wolves, noting that in one case a pack of wolves caused a young girl to climb a tree to safety. "You're allowed to go and take care of it," Trump told the rancher. "If you say so, sir," the rancher replied. "We have no choice," Trump said. "I've been hearing about it for a long time."
