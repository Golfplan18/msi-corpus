YANGON, Myanmar — Myanmar held a second round of voting Sunday in its first general election since the military seized power in February 2021, with polling stations opening across 100 townships amid ongoing civil conflict.

Attacks targeting polling stations and government buildings were reported in at least four of the townships holding polls, with two administrative officials killed, independent online media including Myanmar Now reported.

Tom Andrews, a special rapporteur working with the U.N. human rights office, urged the international community Thursday to reject what he called a "sham election," saying the first round had exposed coercion, violence and political exclusion.

"You cannot have a free, fair or credible election when thousands of political prisoners are behind bars, credible opposition parties have been dissolved, journalists are muzzled, and fundamental freedoms are crushed," Andrews said.

The ballot is proceeding under a new Election Protection Law that imposes harsh penalties for virtually any public criticism of the polls. Authorities have charged more than 330 people under the law for leafleting or online activity in recent months.

## Military-backed party in dominant position

The military-backed Union Solidarity and Development Party, known as the USDP, won nearly 90 percent of contested seats in the lower house during the first phase of voting on Dec. 28. It also won a majority of seats in regional legislatures.

The election is being held in three phases because of armed conflict across the country. The first round took place Dec. 28 in 102 townships. A final round is scheduled for Jan. 25, though 65 townships will not participate because of ongoing fighting.

More than 4,800 candidates from 57 parties are competing for seats in national and regional legislatures, though only six parties are contesting seats nationwide.

Under the constitution, the military automatically holds 25 percent of seats in each house of parliament. The party winning a combined parliamentary majority selects the president, who names a cabinet and forms a government. Maj. Gen. Zaw Min Tun, the military government's spokesperson, said Sunday that the two houses of parliament will be convened in March and the new government will take up its duties in April.

## Suu Kyi and opposition absent

Aung San Suu Kyi, Myanmar's 80-year-old former leader, is not participating in the polls. She is serving a 27-year prison sentence on charges widely viewed as politically motivated. Her party, the National League for Democracy, was dissolved in 2023 after refusing to register under new military rules.

Other parties also declined to register or to run under conditions they deemed unfair, and opposition groups have called for a voter boycott.

The military government said there were more than 24 million eligible voters — about 35 percent fewer than in 2020. The government said more than 6 million people cast ballots during the election's first phase, representing about 52 percent of the more than 11 million eligible voters in that round.

## Conflict backdrop and rights toll

The Assistance Association for Political Prisoners, which keeps detailed tallies of arrests and casualties linked to the conflict, said more than 22,000 people are detained for political offenses and more than 7,600 civilians have been killed by security forces since 2021.

The military's seizure of power triggered widespread protests that gave way to armed resistance, pulling the country into civil war. Polling stations opened in parts of Sagaing, Magway, Mandalay, Bago and Tanintharyi regions, as well as Mon, Shan, Kachin, Kayah and Kayin states — many of which have recently seen clashes or remain under heightened security.

At more than 10 polling stations visited by Associated Press journalists in Yangon and Mandalay, voter turnout appeared lower than during the 2020 election, when long lines were common.

## Voices from the polls

Maung Maung Naing, who voted in Mandalay's Mahar Aung Myay township, said he hoped the election would produce a government focused on ordinary people.

"I only like a government that can make everything better for livelihoods and social welfare," he said.

Sandar Min, an independent candidate from Yangon's Latha township who cast a ballot, said she chose to contest the election despite widespread criticism because she wants to work with the government for the benefit of the country.

"We want the country to be nonviolent. We do not accept violence as part of the change of the country," Sandar Min said. "We care deeply about the people of this country."
