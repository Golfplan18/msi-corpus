Nick Saban, the former head football coach of the University of Alabama, testified Wednesday before the Senate Commerce Committee in support of a bipartisan bill that aims to overhaul the regulatory framework of college athletics, as players increasingly earn millions of dollars and move freely between schools.

The hearing, held by the leaders of the committee, focused on legislation introduced last week by Sen. Ted Cruz, R-Texas, and Sen. Maria Cantwell, D-Wash. The bill would establish a federal structure for payments to college athletes, limit them to a single "free" transfer during their collegiate careers, and create a so-called "Lane Kiffin Rule" that would restrict coaches from leaving their programs during the season.

Cruz described the proposal as "the last, best hope we have to save college sports," according to his prepared remarks at the hearing.

The bill represents the most recent effort by lawmakers to break what supporters described as congressional gridlock over how to regulate an industry in which athletes can now profit from their name, image, and likeness through endorsement deals and other compensation. The current landscape, critics have said, has created a system with few uniform rules governing athlete compensation, transfer mobility, and coach accountability.

Despite the bipartisan backing from Cruz and Cantwell, the legislation has already drawn criticism from some senators and from the Southeastern Conference (SEC) and the Big Ten — the two wealthiest and most influential conferences in college sports. Both conferences have signaled opposition to key provisions of the bill, though their specific objections were not detailed in the hearing.

Saban, who retired in January 2024 after winning seven national championships, offered his public endorsement on Capitol Hill at a moment when the sport he dominated for nearly two decades is navigating a period of rapid financial and regulatory change. His testimony carried weight as a figure whose career spanned the transition from amateurism as the governing ideal to the current environment of six- and seven-figure athlete compensation packages.
