Volcano guides in Sicily staged a strike and demonstrated Wednesday outside Mount Etna's lava flow gate, protesting new restrictions imposed by Catania authorities that ban nighttime excursions and prohibit visitors from approaching the active lava front closer than 200 meters. The work stoppage, the first in decades for the profession, followed a round of eruptions that began on Christmas Eve and has left tourists with sharply curtailed options to view one of Europe's most active volcanoes.

The dispute places guides' livelihoods in direct conflict with local officials' safety mandate, with no resolution in sight as the eruption continues and the guides vow to extend their strike in the coming days.

MOUNT ETNA, Italy — Dozens of volcano guides staged a strike and demonstrated Wednesday outside Mount Etna's lava flow gate, protesting new safety restrictions imposed by authorities in Catania that they say threaten their livelihood and exceed what the current eruption warrants.

The work stoppage — described as the first of its kind in decades — came after Catania suspended or restricted guided excursions to the lava flows following a round of eruptions at Sicily's towering volcano that began on Christmas Eve.

Under the new rules, excursions are permitted only until dusk and cannot bring visitors closer than 200 meters (660 feet) to the active lava front. A previously existing cap of 10 people per group is now being vigorously enforced, including through drone surveillance.

Guides said the restrictions strip them of their professional purpose.

"These measures effectively nullify the role of guides, stripping them of their skills, function, and professional responsibility," a statement by the guides' regional board said.

Dario Teri, 43, a member of Sicily's association of alpine and volcano guides who participated in Wednesday's demonstration, said the terrain has moderated the hazard.

"This is a lava flow that is descending very slowly on an area that is now also flat or semi-flat," Teri said.

Italy's National Institute of Geophysics and Volcanology confirmed this week that the eruption is ongoing but said the lava fronts are cooling and not advancing further. The most advanced lava front reached 1,360 meters (4,460 feet) above sea level before stopping and entering a cooling phase after traveling approximately 3.4 kilometers (about 2 miles), according to local authorities. Volcanologists said the flow poses no danger to nearby residential areas.

The guides' grievances were underscored by the experience of visitors who arrived expecting guided access to the volcano's dramatic displays.

Claudia Mancini, a 32-year-old tourist who traveled from Palermo for an excursion, said she arrived to find the situation upended.

"Unfortunately, we got the bad news of the cancelling of all activity," Mancini said, adding that the situation "is not making anyone happy."

The spectacle draws visitors from across Europe and beyond. Mount Etna is the continent's most active volcano and its largest, standing 3,350 meters (about 10,990 feet) tall and spanning 35 kilometers (21.7 miles) in width. It draws hikers to its upper slopes while others view it from the Ionian Sea. The lava flows are particularly striking after dark — precisely the hours now restricted under the new rules.

The current round of restrictions follows a more severe episode last June, when a massive eruption sent a plume of high-temperature gases, ash, and rock several kilometers into the air, forcing tourists to flee the volcano's slopes.

The guides said they expect to continue their strike in the coming days and expressed hope that negotiations with Catania authorities could produce a compromise that maintains some access while addressing safety concerns.
