NUUK, Greenland — Scores of international journalists have descended on Nuuk, Greenland's small capital, in recent weeks, packing its quiet shopping street with camera crews and correspondents seeking reaction from a population of roughly 20,000 to President Donald Trump's repeated calls to bring the Arctic island under U.S. control.

Reporters from the Associated Press, Reuters, CNN, the BBC, Al Jazeera, Scandinavian outlets and Japanese broadcasters have all arrived in Nuuk, according to AP reporting from the city. They film the capital's multicolored houses, snowcapped hills and freezing fjords — but must compress their work into roughly five hours of daylight each day, as the sun does not rise until after 11 a.m. and sets around 4 p.m.

The result, local politicians and residents said, has been an overwhelming, repetitive, and at times exhausting encounter with the global press.

## 'Round two' for an island of 57,000

Juno Berthelsen, a member of parliament for the Naleraq opposition party, which campaigns for independence in the Greenlandic parliament, described the current wave of attention as "round two" — a reference to an earlier burst of global interest following Trump's first statements in early 2025 that he wanted to control Greenland.

Berthelsen said he has done multiple interviews a day for two weeks.

"I'm getting a bit used to it," he said.

He added that residents are growing fatigued by the repetition. "We're very few people and people tend to get tired when more and more journalists ask the same questions again and again," Berthelsen said.

Greenland's total population is about 57,000, according to AP reporting. In Nuuk, the concentration of journalists has reached the point where the same business owners are approached repeatedly by different news organizations — sometimes doing up to 14 interviews in a single day, AP reported.

## Greenlanders' message: this is home

Residents who spoke to the AP said they want the world to understand that the island's future is for Greenlanders, not outside powers, to determine.

Maya Martinsen, 21, said Trump is "basically lying about what he wants out of Greenland," characterizing his national security arguments as a pretext for gaining access to the island's natural resources.

"The Americans only see what they can get out of Greenland and not what it actually is," Martinsen said. "It's just home to me. I think the Americans just see some kind of business trade."

Trump has argued repeatedly that the United States needs control of Greenland for national security, claiming that China and Russia have their own designs on the island. Greenland holds large untapped reserves of critical minerals, according to AP reporting. Trump has not provided public evidence to support his claims about Chinese or Russian intentions.

## The geopolitical stakes

Greenland is a semiautonomous territory of Denmark. Denmark's prime minister has warned that if the United States attempts to take Greenland by force, it could potentially spell the end of NATO — the transatlantic alliance that has anchored Western security since 1949.

Greenlanders and their political leaders have said consistently that the island is not for sale.

The media surge reflects the degree to which Trump's statements have elevated Greenland from a remote Arctic territory largely unknown to most Americans into a central point of contention in Western geopolitics.
