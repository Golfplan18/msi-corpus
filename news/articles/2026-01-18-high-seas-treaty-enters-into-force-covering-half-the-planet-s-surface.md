The High Seas Treaty, the world's first legally binding agreement to protect marine life in international waters, entered into force Saturday after nearly two decades of negotiations. The accord, ratified by 83 countries including major maritime powers China and Japan, will govern ocean areas beyond any country's jurisdiction — waters covering nearly half the planet's surface.

The treaty entered into force 120 days after it reached the ratification threshold of 60 countries in September. It creates the first legal framework for establishing Marine Protected Areas on the high seas, which make up about two-thirds of the world's ocean. Currently, roughly 1% of international waters are protected. Conservationists say achieving the global target of protecting 30% of the world's oceans by 2030 — which scientists describe as critical for ocean health — depends heavily on action in these vast unregulated waters.

## Immediate obligations

From Saturday, ratifying countries must begin collaborating on ocean science and technology and help developing nations build capacity to participate in ocean governance. Companies planning activities that could harm marine life must conduct environmental impact assessments meeting the treaty's standards. Those conducting research on ocean organisms with potential commercial applications, such as developing new medicines, must notify other countries and share their findings.

Countries must also promote the treaty's conservation goals when participating in other international bodies that regulate ocean activities, including regional fisheries organizations, the International Maritime Organization, and the International Seabed Authority.

Potential sites for the first Marine Protected Areas include the Emperor Seamounts in the North Pacific, the Sargasso Sea in the Atlantic, and the Salas y Gomez and Nazca Ridges off South America.

## Enforcement details unresolved

How those protected areas will be monitored and enforced remains undecided. Rebecca Hubbard, director of the High Seas Alliance, said countries are exploring options including satellite technology, coordinated patrols between nations, and using other United Nations agencies for oversight. Those details will be worked out as the first protected areas take shape, she said.

Conservation advocates warned that the treaty's promise depends on governments following through.

"The marine protected areas under the treaty will only be as strong as the governments make them," said Megan Randles, global political lead for Greenpeace's Ocean Campaign. "We can't trust big fishing industry players to simply stop fishing in these critical ecosystems. We need governments to use the treaty to force their hands."

## United States absent from voting bloc

The United States has signed but not ratified the treaty, leaving it as an observer without voting rights. Under international law, signatory countries are expected to comply with treaty objectives even before ratification.

Hubbard said U.S. non-ratification has not slowed the accord's momentum.

"The High Seas Treaty has such incredibly broad and strong political support from across all regions of the world," she said. "Whilst it's disappointing that the U.S. hasn't yet ratified, it doesn't undermine its momentum and the support that it has already."

## What comes next

Within a year, the treaty's first Conference of Parties will meet to decide key operational details, from budgets to the makeup of various committees. Countries have been working through many of those questions at preparatory meetings, with a final session scheduled for late March.

The earliest any Marine Protected Areas could win approval would be at the second Conference of Parties, since the scientific body that will review proposals has not yet been established. Key institutions including the treaty's secretariat and scientific body are still being developed.

"The treaty is a sign that in a divided world, protecting nature and protecting our global commons can still triumph over political rivalries," Randles said. "The ocean connects us all."
