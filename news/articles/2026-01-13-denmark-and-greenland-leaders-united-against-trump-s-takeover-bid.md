The prime ministers of Denmark and Greenland stood together in Copenhagen on Tuesday, offering their sharpest joint rebuke of President Donald Trump's drive to place Greenland under U.S. control, as their foreign ministers prepared for White House talks the following day with Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

The coordinated pushback deepens a rift between Washington and two NATO allies over Trump's repeated assertions that the United States will acquire Greenland — a stance Danish and Greenlandic officials warn could fracture the transatlantic military alliance.

Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenland Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen stood together in Copenhagen on Tuesday, offering their sharpest joint rejection of President Donald Trump's drive to place Greenland under U.S. control, as their foreign ministers prepared for White House talks the following day with Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

Both leaders underscored that Greenland is part of Denmark and therefore falls under NATO's collective-defense umbrella, warning that a U.S. attempt to force annexation of the island would tear apart the transatlantic alliance.

"Dear Greenlanders, you should know that we stand together today, we will do so tomorrow, and we will continue to do so," Frederiksen said at the joint press conference.

Nielsen was direct in stating his government's position. "If we have to choose between the United States and Denmark here and now, we choose Denmark. We choose NATO. We choose the Kingdom of Denmark. We choose the EU," he said.

## Trump dismisses allied concerns

Trump rejected the pushback from Washington. Asked by reporters about Nielsen's comments expressing a preference to remain with Denmark, Trump said: "I disagree with him. I don't know who he is. I don't know anything about him. But, that's going to be a big problem for him."

On concerns that a U.S. takeover attempt could fracture the NATO alliance, Trump told reporters: "That's their problem."

Trump has repeatedly stated his intention to acquire Greenland, citing what he described as the need to prevent Russia or China from gaining influence over the strategically situated Arctic island. He has said he would prefer to negotiate a deal but added that "one way or the other, we're going to have Greenland." He has said he is considering a range of options, including military force.

## 'Children are afraid,' minister says

Greenland's minister for business and mineral resources, Naaja Nathanielsen, told lawmakers at Britain's Parliament on Tuesday that the Trump administration's stated intentions have unsettled the island's population. She called it "unfathomable" that the United States was discussing the prospect of taking over a fellow NATO ally.

"People are not sleeping, children are afraid, and it just fills everything these days. And we can't really understand it," Nathanielsen said.

She said Greenlanders understand the need for greater Arctic security cooperation amid growing geopolitical tensions but drew a distinction between cooperation and annexation. Nathanielsen said she hoped the island's population would have a voice in determining its future.

"My deepest dream or hope is that the people of Greenland will get a say no matter what," she said. "For others this might be a piece of land, but for us it's home."

## NATO declines to enter dispute

NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte declined to take a position on the standoff, speaking at the European Parliament in Brussels. "I never, ever comment when there are discussions within the alliance," Rutte said. "My role has to be to make sure we solve issues."

He said the alliance should focus on Arctic security. "When it comes to the protection of the High North, that is my role," Rutte said.

## Allied diplomacy on multiple tracks

Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen and Greenland Foreign Minister Vivian Motzfeldt were due to meet Vance and Rubio at the White House on Wednesday.

Separately, a Danish government official confirmed that Denmark provided support to U.S. forces in the east Atlantic last week when they intercepted an oil tanker for alleged violations of U.S. sanctions. The official, who was not authorized to comment publicly on the matter, spoke on condition of anonymity and declined to specify what the support entailed. The interception was part of a weeks-long pursuit of the vessel that began in the Caribbean Sea, according to the Associated Press. The White House and Pentagon did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

A bipartisan U.S. congressional delegation is also set to travel to Copenhagen for meetings on Friday and Saturday.

## U.S. military presence in Greenland

The U.S. military footprint in Greenland has contracted significantly since 1945, when thousands of American soldiers were stationed across 17 bases and installations on the island. Today, roughly 200 soldiers are based at Pituffik Space Base in Greenland's northwest, where the facility supports missile warning, missile defense, and space surveillance operations for the U.S. and NATO.

Danish officials have said they are open to expanded U.S. military cooperation in Greenland but have repeatedly stated the territory is not for sale. Denmark's parliament approved a bill last June permitting U.S. military bases on Danish soil, widening a 2023 defense agreement made with the Biden administration that gave U.S. troops broad access to Danish air bases.
