Vietnam's ruling Communist Party opened its National Congress in Hanoi on Monday, convening 1,586 delegates to select the country's top leadership and set policy priorities for the next five years. General Secretary To Lam is expected to be confirmed to a full term, with diplomats and analysts closely watching whether he will also assume the state presidency — a concentration of authority that would echo political arrangements in China under Xi Jinping and neighboring Laos.

HANOI, Vietnam — Vietnam's ruling Communist Party opened its National Congress in the capital on Monday, convening 1,586 delegates from across the country to select the party's top leadership and set broad policy priorities for the next five years.

The congress, the party's highest decision-making body and one that meets every five years, will elect roughly 200 members to the Central Committee, which will in turn appoint 17 to 19 members to the powerful Politburo, the Associated Press reported.

General Secretary To Lam is expected to be confirmed to a full five-year term. A more consequential question, diplomats and analysts said, is whether he will simultaneously assume the role of state president — a move that would collapse two of the four traditional pillars of Vietnam's governing structure into a single office, in an echo of the political model of China under President Xi Jinping and neighboring Laos.

## Power at the top

Vietnam has long operated under a "four pillars" system distributing power among the party chief, president, prime minister, and National Assembly chair. Merging the party chief and presidential roles would make To Lam the most powerful leader Vietnam has seen in decades, according to the Associated Press.

To Lam spent decades in the Ministry of Public Security before becoming its minister in 2016. He led the anti-corruption campaign championed by his predecessor, Nguyen Phu Trong. During that campaign, the Politburo — then comprising 18 members — lost six, including two former presidents and the parliamentary head.

He has also overseen the most sweeping round of bureaucratic and economic reforms since Vietnam liberalized its economy in the late 1980s, including cuts to tens of thousands of public-sector jobs, a redrawing of administrative boundaries to speed decision-making, and the initiation of dozens of major infrastructure projects.

To Lam's faction within the party is aligned with the Ministry of Public Security; the other main grouping is close to the more conservative military, according to the Associated Press.

Nguyen Khac Giang, a visiting fellow at the ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute in Singapore, said conservatives within the party were uneasy about To Lam's reform agenda, fearing the "Vietnamese socialist system could go astray." He said conservative voices want to remain in the leadership as a check on power concentration and to keep Vietnam on a socialist path.

## Economic direction

Hanging over the congress is a defining national ambition: whether Vietnam can transform itself into a high-income country by 2045.

A draft resolution released in October called for average annual gross domestic product growth of 10 percent or more from 2026 to 2030, a target set in the context of the country having fallen short of an earlier goal of 6.5 to 7.0 percent average annual growth during the first half of the decade. Vietnam's economy grew 8 percent in 2025, according to the Associated Press.

To reach the new target, the draft resolution called for upgrading industry, modernizing production processes, and relying more heavily on science, technology, and digital tools to drive growth. It also described the private sector as "one of the most important driving forces of the economy," underscoring To Lam's emphasis on business-led growth and a shift away from the dominance of state-owned enterprises. Giang said To Lam may seek to deepen that shift by giving large private conglomerates a greater role in major projects across the country.

In January, military-run telecommunications company Viettel broke ground on its first semiconductor chipmaking plant. The project aims to begin trial production by 2027 and is part of Hanoi's broader strategy to build high-tech manufacturing capacity and reduce reliance on foreign suppliers.

## Geopolitics and environment

The draft resolution placed foreign affairs and international integration on the same footing as national defense and security, a formulation that reflects how dependent Vietnam's export-driven economy is on global markets and geopolitics amid intensifying U.S.–China rivalry.

The document also elevated environmental protection to a central priority alongside economic and social development — a notable shift in a country where rapid growth has fueled worsening air pollution and other environmental pressures, according to the Associated Press.
