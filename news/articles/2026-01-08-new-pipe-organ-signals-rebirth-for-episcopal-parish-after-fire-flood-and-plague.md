Manhattan's Church of the Epiphany marked the Feast of Epiphany on Jan. 6 when the bishop of New York blessed the congregation's handcrafted $2.5 million pipe organ, completing the final phase of a $70 million effort to relocate and rebuild the nearly 200-year-old Episcopal parish after years of flooding, a building fire and the COVID-19 pandemic. The organ, designed and built by Bigelow & Co. Organ Builders of American Fork, Utah, arrived in August and was installed in the church's second-floor sanctuary on East 74th Street on Manhattan's Upper East Side.

The installation points to continued investment by some theologically traditional denominations in new instruments even as overall U.S. worship attendance declines nationally, with industry representatives saying that high-quality organ builders are currently fully booked.

## A move across First Avenue

Church of the Epiphany was a space-constrained but healthy congregation in 2018 when it began planning to move from its location on York Avenue to the former Jan Hus Presbyterian Church one block west. The Rev. Matthew Dayton-Welch, who became rector in 2023, said that for the parish community at the time, "crossing First Avenue, for us, we might as well have been crossing the Red Sea."

The transition came with a series of setbacks. The church's then-rector, the Rev. Jennifer Anne Reddall, was elected bishop of Arizona, triggering an unplanned leadership search. A 2020 excavation of the new property revealed that it sat over a natural creek, requiring an engineering redesign of the building's foundation and causing flooding in the basement. In 2021, hot steel beam rafters that did not cool as expected briefly set the church ablaze. And the congregation, situated in the middle of a medical hub, was hard hit by the pandemic — parishioners recalled refrigerated morgue trucks circling the neighborhood.

"You had a flood, a fire and a plague," Dayton-Welch said.

## Built in Utah, installed by community

The congregation contracted with Bigelow & Co. in 2020 to design and build the new instrument. Michael Bigelow, the company's founder and a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, operates his workshop in a former LDS church building in American Fork, Utah, whose tall ceilings accommodate organ assembly.

The organ arrived in August on a 53-foot-long truck that double-parked on the Upper East Side. Clergy climbed onto the loading dock, donned stoles and blessed the instrument while roughly 30 parishioners fired confetti cannons. For hours afterward, parishioners spanning multiple generations hauled pipes and boxes up flights of stairs to the sanctuary. Moving the 600-pound console up an exterior staircase took more than 30 minutes.

"What has been the most beautiful part of this organ is the way it has brought our entire community together," said Denise Cruz, a vestry member. "It was all hands on deck."

The instrument uses mechanical tracker action — in which pressing a key opens a valve through physical linkages — rather than the electric-action systems found in many modern organs. Builders initially designed it with a German neo-Baroque character, but feedback from church leaders prompted changes to better serve Anglican-style liturgy, including the addition of a Flute Celeste stop.

"That decision was made basically to better serve the Anglican style of liturgy," said Conner Kunz, a woodworker on the Bigelow team. He described the Flute Celeste as producing an "ethereal, sort of wavy, shimmery effect" — "less boisterous than our shrieky little harmonic pipes that are sort of traditional in the neo-Baroque style."

## Beyond Sunday worship

Dayton-Welch said the organ is part of a broader investment in community. "So many churches make difficult decisions because they're shrinking and they're consolidating and they're trying to survive. And that wasn't the case here," he said. "This was the church that was healthy, but it was still willing to risk everything it had in order to create an even better platform in a city where churches don't get up and move."

Epiphany's 60 to 80 Sunday worshippers represent one segment of a community the parish is working to expand. The church also runs a Wednesday night dinner program serving housing-insecure neighbors, college students and others. Alex Nguyen, who became director of music in September, said he plans unconventional programming alongside traditional recitals.

"Of course we will have recitals, but I think we'd like to try some different things, unconventional pairings with the organ, doing things with the kids to help create that interest," Nguyen said.

## A signal to stay

Andrew Gingery, vice president of the Associated Pipe Organ Builders of America and executive vice president of C.B. Fisk — a pipe organ builder based in Gloucester, Massachusetts, currently developing an instrument for St. Vartan Armenian Cathedral in New York for 2027 — said Catholic, Episcopal, Lutheran and Presbyterian congregations with resources continue to commission new organs.

"There are still churches with means, and they want to have good music," Gingery said. "That's one of the things that makes them an active church. Frankly, you put on a good show and people are likely to come."

For Christian Vanderbrouk, who has attended Epiphany for about a decade and served as junior warden when organ plans were first drawn up, the instrument's presence carries weight beyond its sound.

"It's a signal to the parish and to our neighbors that after all that moving and construction, we're fully invested, and we're here to stay," Vanderbrouk said. "There's a sense of permanence."
