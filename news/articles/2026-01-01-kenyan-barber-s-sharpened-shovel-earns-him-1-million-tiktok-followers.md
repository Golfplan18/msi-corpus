KIAMBU, Kenya — Safari Martins runs a sparse roadside shack at the edge of Nairobi where the tools hanging on wooden walls — a shovel, an iron, agricultural shears, a wrench — look nothing like what a barber would reach for. Martins reaches for them anyway, sliding a razor-sharp shovel edge across a client's scalp to produce what customers describe as a precision cut.

The Rwandan-born barber, based in Kenya's Kiambu county and known online as Chief Safro, has built roughly 1 million followers on each of his Instagram and TikTok accounts by trading conventional clippers for improvised implements and documenting every session on camera. His clientele pay up to 1,500 Kenyan shillings — nearly $12 — for the experience, a price that can run ten times what a standard Nairobi barbershop charges.

Martins is among a new class of influencer barbers who have emerged as Kenya's social media landscape has expanded rapidly, with the country's user base growing nearly 50 percent between January 2023 and January 2025, according to market research firm DataReportal. His story surfaces a broader tension in Africa's creator economy: large audiences can be built, but monetization has not kept pace for barbers and others in niche craft categories.

## Origins

Martins was born in Rwanda and began barbering in high school in 2018, using borrowed clippers to offer trims outside classrooms and in cramped dormitories, the Associated Press reported. Five years later, he added a camera and dropped his conventional trimmer entirely.

He went viral initially for the novelty of his methods but has since woven traditional African folk tales into voiceovers for his videos. "I'm motivated by African culture, by African stories," he said. He said one of his tools, a sharpened iron box, was blessed by village elders.

## The premium and the platform

A helper stands to the side of every cutting session, capturing footage from multiple angles on a smartphone. The resulting videos feed social accounts that document both the haircuts and the clients' reactions.

Ian Njenga, who first visited Martins last year, said the experience set the barber apart. "If I compare him with other barbers his talent is next level," Njenga said. "When I get shaved here I get very comfortable … while walking in the streets I get very confident."

The social media growth underpinning Martins' business has been rapid. Kenya counted 10.6 million social media users in January 2023, a figure that climbed to 15.1 million by January 2025, DataReportal reported. About 15 percent of Kenyans engaged in online content creation rely on it as their primary source of income, the Kenya Institute for Public Policy Research and Analysis said in a June 2025 brief.

## Creator economy gaps

Despite his large following, Martins said barbers do not receive the same financial rewards as creators in categories like gaming, education, or lifestyle content. Brand deals in those niches draw broader applicability, according to Fundmates, a company that finances influencers.

"Barbers get viral on social media but I feel like they are not respected," Martins said. "You are not paid as a content creator, even though you have the views, even if you have the engagement."
