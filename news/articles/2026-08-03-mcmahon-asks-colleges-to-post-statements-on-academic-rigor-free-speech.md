## McMahon asks colleges to post statements on academic rigor, free speech

**Subtype:** fact

- Education Secretary Linda McMahon sent an open letter to American colleges on Monday asking administrators to commit to changes aligned with the Trump administration's higher education agenda before the end of the year.
- The letter asks schools to post statements prominently on their websites about academic rigor, free speech, and "malign" foreign influence.
- It also asks them for statements on prioritizing "American interests" and hiring faculty of "all relevant perspectives."
- Monday's letter omits the funding incentives and many of the strictures contained in a year-old compact that no college signed.

## Letter omits funding incentives that accompanied year-old compact

The open letter McMahon sent Monday comes about a year after the Education Department asked a group of colleges to sign a compact that promised federal funds to schools that embraced the administration's agenda. Monday's letter does not include the compact's connected advantages in funding and grants, which are usually merit based, and it omits many of the compact's strictures.

Instead, McMahon asked schools to post statements prominently on their websites covering academic rigor, free speech, and what she described as "malign" foreign influence, writing that the postings would improve public perception. The letter also requests statements on prioritizing "American interests" and on hiring faculty of "all relevant perspectives." The administration has previously said colleges and universities are too liberal.

McMahon wrote that "the dilution of academic standards, opaque admissions policies and a domineering monoculture have badly eroded public trust" in education, and that "only full candor and transparency can begin to strengthen the bonds between higher education and the American people."

The year-old compact, in contrast, carried a broader set of requirements: limits on international enrollment, a pledge to follow the administration's definitions of biological sex, and "consideration of transforming or abolishing institutional units that purposefully punish, belittle or even spark violence against conservative ideas." It also promoted policies "to recognize that academic freedom is not absolute."

None of the colleges signed the agreement, and several publicly rejected it.

McMahon wrote that "there is no single template for academic excellence," adding, "I hope we will witness a variety of institutional types, each with distinct missions and priorities, issue landmark declarations of purpose that will revitalize higher education for years to come."
