Behind some of 2025's most uplifting news — a papal phone call, a Nobel Prize surprise, a typhoon-flooded wedding, and a youth theater revival from wildfire ashes — were Associated Press journalists who found themselves at the center of the moments they were sent to document.

In a year marked by political conflict and natural disasters, AP correspondents described assignments that placed them inside moments of unexpected joy, and at one turn, inside the news itself.

The accounts illustrate how journalists sent to record events sometimes become witnesses to something that could not have been scripted — and how the reporting itself can carry a story further than the original assignment.

## The call from Rome

When Pope Leo XIV appeared on the balcony of St. Peter's Basilica as the first American pope, AP video journalist Obed Lamy drove three hours from Indianapolis to the suburban Chicago home of John Prevost, the new pontiff's older brother, in New Lenox, Illinois.

As Lamy interviewed Prevost, a ringing came from the basement. Prevost hurried down to a tablet and found missed calls from his brother. He returned the call on speaker, with Lamy's camera rolling.

"I was shaking because I didn't know what the pope would say," Lamy said. "Am I supposed to say something or not say anything?"

The brothers spoke as siblings do — including an exchange about where the pope's family would sleep in Rome, a detail the new pontiff apparently did not yet know. Prevost told the pope: "Well, first you need to know you're on the air right now. This is the first time I'm hearing that this thing rang."

## A Nobel announced before dawn

When the Nobel Prize in medicine was announced, AP photographer Lindsey Wasson was dispatched before dawn to the North Seattle home of Mary E. Brunkow. Brunkow, along with Fred Ramsdell and Shimon Sakaguchi, had won the prize for discoveries concerning peripheral immune tolerance.

Wasson's GPS led her first to the back of the property. She navigated through a neighbor's dark driveway before reaching the front door. No one was awake. A barking dog roused Brunkow's husband, Ross, and Wasson identified herself.

"Sir, your wife just won the Nobel Prize," Wasson told him.

When Ross brought the news to Mary, her initial response — according to Ross — was: "Don't be ridiculous."

The mood shifted once the couple worked through the overnight missed calls from Sweden and an accumulating stream of messages from family, colleagues, and other journalists seeking comment.

## The wedding that went ahead

AP photographer Aaron Favila, who has covered floods for years in the Philippines, raced to a church north of Manila after a colleague tipped him to a wedding proceeding despite typhoon flooding. The floodwaters were too deep for his vehicle; he completed the journey on a rescue truck, arriving just before the bride entered.

Groom Jade Rick Verdillo said the couple had decided to proceed regardless. "We've been through a lot. This is just one of the struggles that we've overcome," Verdillo said.

## Theater from the ashes

Reporter Jocelyn Gecker covered opening night of a Southern California youth theater group's production of "Crazy for You" staged weeks after the Palisades wildfire destroyed the group's theater and many cast members' homes. Director Lara Ganz, whose own family lost their home in the fire, said the production was about restoring togetherness and showing the young cast that life was not over.

After the story was published, members of the Gershwin family trust read it and attended a later performance. They delivered a letter to the cast and crew.

"On behalf of the families of George and Ira Gershwin, we applaud your resilience," the letter said.
