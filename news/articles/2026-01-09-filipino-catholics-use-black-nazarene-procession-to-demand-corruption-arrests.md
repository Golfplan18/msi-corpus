MANILA, Philippines — Hundreds of thousands of mostly barefoot Filipino Catholics filled Manila's streets Friday for the annual Black Nazarene procession, weaving deep religious devotion with political protest as crowds chanted demands that legislators implicated in a major corruption scandal be jailed.

The procession of a centuries-old wooden statue of Jesus Christ wound through nearly six kilometers (3.7 miles) of the capital's congested streets near the presidential palace. Manila Mayor Isko Moreno said millions of devotees were expected to participate throughout the day and into the late night.

The gathering coincided with growing public anger over allegations that dozens of members of the House of Representatives and the Senate received kickbacks from construction companies for thousands of flood control projects across the Philippines that were substandard or nonexistent. Several government engineers and construction company executives have been detained while facing corruption trials. President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. had vowed to have accused legislators jailed by Christmas, a deadline that passed without the promised prosecutions.

The alleged irregularities began during the tenure of former President Rodrigo Duterte and have sparked street protests, some organized by the Roman Catholic Church, whose members object to the lavish lifestyles — fleets of luxury cars, private jets and mansions — that implicated officials have displayed.

Bishop Rufino Sescon, delivering a homily at Manila's Rizal Park grandstand before the procession, directed pointed criticism at officials who had been named in televised congressional hearings but refused to step down.

"In our country today, there are those who refuse to step down even though they made mistakes and got caught, even though they are causing hardship to the people, even though the poor are suffering, even though the country is being flooded and destroyed," Sescon said. "Enough is enough. Have mercy on the people. Have some shame. Step down voluntarily in the name of mercy and love."

Venus Lopez, a 62-year-old devotee who carried a replica of the Nazarene statue during the procession, called for the same accountability. "I hope those corrupt government officials will go away. They don't deserve to be seated in power," she told the Associated Press, adding that she planned to pray to the Nazarene to help remove corrupt officials from government.

## Security and safety along the route

About 15,000 police officers, backed by intelligence agents, were deployed to maintain order. Authorities imposed a gun and liquor ban, prohibited drones and backpacks, and jammed cellphone signals along the route. First-aid tents staffed by government and Red Cross medics treated more than 250 worshippers for bruises and sprains by midday.

A Filipino photographer collapsed on a nearby sidewalk shortly before the procession began and died while being transported to a hospital, according to officials and two Associated Press journalists who tried to assist him.

## Devotion and the statue's history

Many devotees wore maroon shirts bearing images of the Nazarene and attempted to touch the carriage carrying the statue or threw small towels at volunteers riding the carriage to wipe the statue and cross, in the belief that the figure can cure ailments and bring good health, employment and a better life.

The life-size statue, crowned with thorns and bearing a cross, is believed to have arrived in Manila from Mexico in 1606 aboard a Spanish galleon. The ship caught fire during the voyage, but the charred statue survived. Devotees cite its endurance through subsequent earthquakes, fires and intense bombing during World War II as evidence of what they describe as its mystical powers.

The procession is one of Asia's major religious spectacles and reflects the distinct form of Catholicism practiced in the Philippines, Asia's largest Catholic nation, which blends orthodox tradition with folk belief.
