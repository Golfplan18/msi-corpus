Iran's Revolutionary Guard commander, Gen. Mohammad Pakpour, warned Friday that his force stands "more ready than ever, finger on the trigger" as U.S. military assets moved toward the region. The statement marked the latest escalation in tensions rooted in a month-long crackdown on protests triggered by Iran's currency collapse, which activist groups say has killed over 5,200 people and resulted in more than 40,000 arrests.

President Donald Trump has set two explicit red lines for military intervention: the killing of peaceful protesters or mass executions of detained demonstrators. With a U.S. Navy aircraft carrier now in the Indian Ocean and Iran's government labeling some prisoners with charges that carry the death penalty, the risk of wider escalation has intensified.

## Military Threats and Contingencies

Gen. Mohammad Pakpour, the commander of Iran's paramilitary Revolutionary Guard responsible for carrying out a month-long crackdown on nationwide protests, warned Friday that his force stands ready to act. "The Islamic Revolutionary Guard and dear Iran stand more ready than ever, finger on the trigger, to execute the orders and directives of the Commander-in-Chief," according to a statement carried by Nournews, a news outlet with ties to Iran's Supreme National Security Council.

Pakpour cautioned the United States and Israel to "avoid any miscalculation." The warning came as tensions escalated between Washington and Tehran, rooted in a violent crackdown that began Dec. 28 when Iran's currency, the rial, collapsed and triggered nationwide demonstrations.

President Donald Trump responded by moving U.S. military assets toward the region. On Thursday, aboard Air Force One, Trump said the U.S. was deploying "a massive fleet heading in that direction and maybe we won't have to use it." A U.S. Navy official confirmed Friday that the aircraft carrier USS Abraham Lincoln and other warships were in the Indian Ocean.

Trump has set two explicit conditions for military intervention: the killing of peaceful demonstrators and the mass execution of people arrested in the protests. On Thursday, the president claimed that Iran had halted executions of 800 people detained during the demonstrations without elaborating on the source of this claim. Iran's top prosecutor, Mohammad Movahedi, strongly rejected the assertion Friday, characterizing it as false.

## Death Toll and Arrests Escalate

The death toll from the crackdown remains contested. The U.S.-based Human Rights Activists News Agency, whose figures have proved accurate in previous unrest, reported Saturday that the death toll reached 5,200, with the number expected to increase as information emerges from Iran despite the most comprehensive internet blackout in the country's history.

Iran's government reported 3,117 deaths on Wednesday, identifying 2,427 as civilians and security forces, and labeling the remainder "terrorists."

Arrests have accelerated dramatically. The activist agency reported Saturday that the total number of people arrested had jumped to 40,879, a significant increase from the more than 27,700 reported previously. The rapid rise underscores the severity of the security response.

## Death Penalty Charges Raise Execution Fears

Adding to international concern, authorities have called some of those being held "mohareb"—or "enemies of God"—a charge that carries the death penalty. This designation echoes Iran's past. The same charge was used alongside other counts in mass executions in 1988 that reportedly killed at least 5,000 people.

At a United Nations Human Rights Council special session in Geneva on Friday, Volker Türk, the U.N.'s high commissioner for human rights, expressed concern over "contradictory statements from the Iranian authorities about whether those detained in connection with the protests may be executed." Türk noted that Iran "remains among the top executioner states in the world," with at least 1,500 people reportedly executed last year—a 50 percent increase over 2024.

## Airlines Suspend Service Over Regional Instability

The military buildup and threats of escalation have disrupted civilian commerce. Air France canceled two return flights from Paris to Dubai over the weekend, citing the need to ensure flight safety as it monitors the geopolitical situation. The airline said it would resume service to Dubai later Saturday.

Luxair postponed its Saturday flight from Luxembourg to Dubai by 24 hours "in light of ongoing tensions and insecurity affecting the region's airspace." Dutch carriers KLM and Transavia canceled Saturday flights from Amsterdam to Dubai, according to online flight trackers. Some KLM flights to Tel Aviv were also canceled Friday and Saturday.

The airline actions signal growing concern in Europe about the risk of military confrontation in the region and its effects on regional airspace.
