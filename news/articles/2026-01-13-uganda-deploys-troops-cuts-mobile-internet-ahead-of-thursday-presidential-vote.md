KAMPALA, Uganda — Uganda's military deployed armored vehicles and soldiers in the capital and telecommunications regulators ordered mobile internet suspended less than 48 hours before Thursday's presidential election, measures that opposition figures said reflected the authoritarianism they seek to end. President Yoweri Museveni, 81, seeks a seventh consecutive term against his main challenger, singer-turned-politician Bobi Wine, in a contest that Brussels-based International Crisis Group said Museveni "will likely" win.

The election pits Africa's third-longest-serving head of state against a 43-year-old opposition leader who has built a following among urban youth, testing whether Uganda's institutional arrangements allow for a peaceful transfer of power after nearly four decades of Museveni's rule.

## Internet suspension and troop deployment

The Uganda Communications Commission on Tuesday directed mobile internet providers to temporarily suspend services, citing misinformation, electoral fraud and incitement of violence. Restoration of services would occur only after the commission issued a separate notice to providers, according to a letter sent to internet companies.

Ugandan authorities began deploying troops on Saturday in parts of Kampala, with armored trucks spreading into different parts of the city and soldiers patrolling streets. Military spokesman Col. Chris Magezi said the deployment was intended to deter violence.

"No cause for alarm," Magezi said. "However, we do not take threats of violence during the election period by some political actors and their supporters lightly."

Eight candidates are competing for the presidency of the East African nation of roughly 45 million people. Electoral authorities say there are 21.6 million registered voters.

## Incumbent and challenger

Museveni and Wine are reprising their rivalry from the 2021 election, when Wine, whose legal name is Kyagulanyi Ssentamu, secured 35% of ballots against Museveni's 58% — the president's smallest vote share since his first electoral campaign. Voter turnout in 2021 was 59%.

Wine has maintained much of his base in parts of eastern Uganda and in the metropolitan area around Kampala, where he held rallies wearing a flak jacket and helmet. He told the Associated Press that he and his supporters have been attacked by security forces using tear gas and sometimes even bullets. Amnesty International repeated those allegations.

Museveni has called Wine unpatriotic and an agent of foreign interests. Wine rejects the charges. Members of Museveni's party, the National Resistance Movement, have suggested the president would not hand power to Wine even if the challenger won the election.

Museveni has a loyal following across northern Uganda and his western home region. His supporters credit him with restoring relative peace and stability in a country that hosts hundreds of thousands of refugees fleeing violence elsewhere.

Museveni first took power by force as leader of a guerrilla army and has been elected six times since 1996 in contests that have been repeatedly marred by allegations of rigging and interference by security forces.

## Dynastic concerns

Some critics say removing Museveni through elections remains difficult, and his authority has become increasingly dependent on the military, which is led by his son, Gen. Muhoozi Kainerugaba, a four-star general. Kainerugaba sparked controversy as the author of social media posts widely seen as offensive, including remarks about invading neighboring Kenya and offering cattle as a dowry to wed the Italian prime minister. He has also said he expects to succeed his father as president.

## Voter concerns

Voters in and around Kampala cited job creation and economic hardship as central concerns.

"I was born when Museveni was in power," said Farouk Mugaya, 37, who operates a passenger motorcycle in Kampala. "They have had enough time to protect the gains. I want to see change."

Mugaya said he plans to vote in his hometown of Iganga in eastern Uganda despite doubts about the outcome. "I don't want to say they rigged the vote when I was just there and didn't vote," he said. "I want to say they stole the vote when I also voted."

Denis Oraku, a mason at a construction site near Kampala, said economic conditions weigh on voters despite the relative calm. "Uganda is very hard. Getting money is not easy," Oraku said.

Analyst Robert Kabushenga said of Museveni on a popular podcast: "Time is not on his side. For the first time he is actually, really an outsider. Why? Because he doesn't represent the future."
