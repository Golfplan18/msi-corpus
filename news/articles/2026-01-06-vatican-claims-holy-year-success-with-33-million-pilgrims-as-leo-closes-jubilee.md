VATICAN CITY — Pope Leo XIV on Tuesday will formally close the 2025 Holy Year by sealing the Holy Door of St. Peter's Basilica, capping a Jubilee the Vatican said drew more than 33 million pilgrims — and only the second such observance in history to be opened by one pope and closed by another. The final pilgrims crossed the threshold late Monday, among them a wheelchair-using public defender from Birmingham, England, who called the crossing "wonderful" and "unique."

The Vatican estimated 33,475,369 people participated in the year's pilgrimages and events, exceeding the 31.7 million projected by a Roma Tre University study, though the Holy Year's chief organizer acknowledged the figure is an estimate that may include double counting.

The closing ends a year reshaped by Pope Francis's death in April and the election of Leo XIV — a succession that made the 2025 Jubilee only the second Holy Year since 1700 to change popes before its end. In that earlier instance, Pope Innocent XII opened the Jubilee and Pope Clement XI closed it after Innocent's death.

## Counting the pilgrims

The Vatican arrived at its 33.4 million figure by combining the number of people who registered for Jubilee events, volunteer crowd counters at Rome-area basilicas, and closed-circuit television cameras at St. Peter's that recorded roughly 25,000 to 30,000 people crossing the Holy Door each day, according to the AP. Archbishop Rino Fisichella, the Vatican's Holy Year organizer, said at a press conference that no breakdown was available between Holy Year pilgrims and Rome's general tourism numbers.

The Vatican said it recorded a steady increase in participation following Francis's death. Italy, the United States, and Spain were the top nationalities represented, according to the Vatican.

## Among the last to cross

Among the final pilgrims was Natalie Turner, a public defender from Birmingham, England, who was pushed in a wheelchair by her son, Philip, across cobblestones and up ramps along the pilgrim route. Turner, who uses a wheelchair because of severe arthritis, said it was her first visit to the Vatican.

"Wonderful. Unique. Something I will never experience again, probably," Turner said as she passed through the door.

She said the trip had grounded her. "It is a great way of grounding me, and helping me to realize that as bad as those things are that I see, God has it," Turner said. "He is in control, and I cannot take it all, I can only do as much as I can do."

## Rome's public works

For Rome, the Holy Year was associated with about 4 billion euros ($4.3 billion) in public funds for long-delayed infrastructure. Gualtieri said 110 of 117 projects initially linked to the Jubilee had been completed.

The centerpiece was Piazza Pia, a new pedestrian square at the end of Via della Conciliazione opposite St. Peter's Basilica, built by routing traffic through a newly dug underground tunnel. The Via della Conciliazione was itself carved out for the 1950 Jubilee by razing a neighborhood.

The piazza's design produced the main point of disagreement between Gualtieri and Fisichella: two round stone fountains that frame the view toward the basilica. The mayor favored them; the archbishop did not. Because the piazza sits on Italian rather than Vatican soil, Fisichella said, he had to defer.

"This was probably the only point on which we had to say, laughing and smiling, that we didn't completely agree," Fisichella said. "He liked those two fountains, I liked others, but I had to back down."

A year on, he said he has grown accustomed to them — but remains unmoved.

"I always thought they looked like foot baths," he said.
