The agreement opens the path back to power for al-Maliki, who served two terms as prime minister from 2006 to 2014, at a moment when the incoming government faces overlapping pressures: U.S. demands to disarm Iran-linked armed groups, an increasingly assertive judiciary calling for state monopoly on weapons, and powerful militias that have publicly rejected any discussion of disarmament.

BAGHDAD — Iraq's caretaker Prime Minister Mohammed Shia al-Sudani agreed to step aside in favor of former Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki in a deal struck between the two rivals, two Iraqi officials said Tuesday, resolving weeks of post-election deadlock over who would lead the country's next government.

The officials — a senior leader in al-Sudani's Reconstruction and Development Coalition and a government official — spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to discuss the matter publicly. Al-Sudani's office did not respond to a request for comment.

Al-Sudani's bloc had won 46 seats in November's elections to the 329-seat parliament, the largest share of any single coalition, while al-Maliki's coalition won 30 seats. Both men sought the backing of the Coordination Framework, a coalition of Iran-backed Shiite parties that brought al-Sudani to power in 2022. The Framework declined to choose between them and left the decision to the candidates themselves, the officials said. A meeting between al-Sudani and al-Maliki produced the agreement that al-Sudani would step aside, they said.

## The militia question

The incoming government will inherit a politically fraught debate over Iraq's armed groups, particularly the Popular Mobilization Forces — a coalition of mostly Shiite militias that formed to fight the Islamic State group as it swept across Iraq more than a decade ago. The PMF was formally placed under the control of the Iraqi military in 2016 but in practice still operates with significant autonomy, according to the Associated Press.

The United States has been pushing Iraq to disarm Iran-backed groups, the AP reported — a difficult proposition given the political power that many of those factions hold.

## Judiciary enters the debate

As politicians negotiated over government formation, Iraq's Supreme Judicial Council entered the disarmament debate. Council head Judge Faiq Zidan said in a speech earlier this month that there was no longer a need for weapons outside the Iraqi state following the defeat of terrorism, and that a functioning state depends on respect for the law, unified decision-making and "exclusive state control of weapons."

Some political forces welcomed Zidan's statement, but armed groups pushed back. The Iraqi Resistance Coordination group — which includes powerful factions Kataib Hezbollah, Kataib Sayyid al-Shuhada and Asaib Ahl al-Haq — issued a statement rejecting any discussion about their weapons. The group described its arms as "a guarantee to defend the country and its sacred sites" and said any dialogue on the issue must take place "after full sovereignty is achieved," rejecting what it called foreign interference.

A judicial official who spoke on condition of anonymity said Zidan "has laid out a comprehensive legal framework for the disarmament process." The official added that "the refusal by some factions to disarm will not prevent the judiciary from supporting the incoming Iraqi government by providing legal cover and judicial backing for the disarmament process."

The judiciary has also taken a stronger stance in the post-election period on enforcing constitutional deadlines for government formation, including naming the speaker of parliament and the president.

Ihsan Al-Shammari, a professor at the University of Baghdad, said the judiciary's posture represents one of the most assertive judicial interventions in Iraq since 2003, when the current political system was established following the U.S.-led invasion that unseated Saddam Hussein.
