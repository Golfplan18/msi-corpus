The overture revives a U.S.-led diplomatic track that collapsed in 2020 when Ethiopia withdrew from Trump-era talks, and comes after Ethiopia formally inaugurated the dam last fall — sharpening tensions with downstream nations whose agriculture and drinking water depend on Nile flow.

CAIRO — Egypt and Sudan said Saturday they welcomed President Donald Trump's offer to restart U.S. mediation with Ethiopia over a years-long dispute about Nile River water sharing, after Ethiopia completed and inaugurated Africa's largest hydroelectric dam.

Trump announced the initiative Friday in a social media post sharing a letter he sent to Egyptian President Abdel-Fattah el-Sissi. "I am ready to restart U.S. mediation between Egypt and Ethiopia to responsibly resolve the question of 'The Nile Water Sharing' once and for all," Trump wrote.

El-Sissi said in a social media post that he valued "President Trump's attention to the central importance of the Nile River issue for Egypt," adding that Egypt is committed to "serious and constructive cooperation with the Nile Basin countries, based on the principles of international law, in a manner that achieves shared interests without causing harm to any party." He said Egypt supports U.S. efforts to resolve the dispute.

Gen. Abdel-Fattah Burhan, chairman of Sudan's ruling Sovereign Council and the country's military chief, hailed the initiative on X as a step "to find sustainable and satisfactory solutions that preserve everyone's rights." Ethiopia had not issued a comment as of Saturday.

## The dam and the dispute

Ethiopia inaugurated the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam last fall. Built on the Blue Nile near Ethiopia's border with Sudan at a cost of approximately $5 billion, the dam is designed to produce more than 5,000 megawatts — more than doubling Ethiopia's electricity generation capacity. Ethiopia argues the dam is essential because the vast majority of its population lacks access to electricity.

Egypt, a mostly desert country whose population of 110 million depends on the Nile for fresh water, has called the dam a "grave violation of international law" and an "existential threat," fearing a drastic reduction in downstream water flow with severe consequences for agriculture and water supply. El-Sissi has said that Egypt's share of Nile water is "untouchable," while favoring a negotiated resolution.

Sudan wants coordination of the dam's operation and reservoir replenishment to protect its own downstream infrastructure from unexpected impacts.

## Where talks stand

U.S.-led mediation began during Trump's first term but effectively collapsed in 2020 when Ethiopia withdrew. African Union-facilitated discussions continued afterward without producing a settlement.

The unresolved questions involve how quickly the dam's reservoir will be filled each year, how much water Ethiopia will release downstream if a multi-year drought occurs, and what mechanism the three countries will use to settle future disputes. Egypt and Sudan have called for a legally binding agreement governing dam operations, while Ethiopia has insisted on non-binding guidelines.
