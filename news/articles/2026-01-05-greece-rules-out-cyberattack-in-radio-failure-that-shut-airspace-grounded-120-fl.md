Greece's government said Monday that a communications failure that shut the country's airspace for several hours Sunday is unlikely to have been a cyberattack, though investigators have yet to determine what caused noise to overwhelm air traffic radio channels — including backup systems — across the country. The outage grounded roughly 120 flights at the nation's two largest airports and stranded thousands of passengers.

"There is not the slightest indication that we are dealing with a cyberattack," government spokesman Pavlos Marinakis said. "I need to make that clear."

The incident prompted Greek authorities to launch a judicial inquiry, an internal investigation, and a new ministerial committee — and renewed long-standing calls from air traffic controllers to replace aging equipment.

## What happened

The Greek Civil Aviation Authority said that noise appeared Sunday across all air traffic communication channels, including backup systems, triggering a shutdown that lasted several hours before operations were gradually restored.

Incoming international flights were diverted to neighboring countries, creating a large backlog that lasted into early Monday morning, according to the Brussels-based European Organization for the Safety of Air Navigation, widely known as Eurocontrol. Eurocontrol said about 120 flights were grounded at the airports in Athens and Thessaloniki and assisted in redirecting aircraft during and after the outage.

## Investigations launched

Transport and Infrastructure Minister Christos Dimas called the incident "a very serious incident" but stressed that passenger safety was never at risk. He announced the formation of an investigative committee comprising representatives from civil aviation authorities, the Greek air force, Eurocontrol, and a Greek state-run cyberdefence agency.

Separately, a judicial inquiry and an internal investigation were both opened Monday to determine the cause of the failure.

## Equipment concerns

Greece's Air Traffic Controllers' Association said the outage underscored its long-standing calls to modernize and replace outdated equipment — a systemic concern the association has raised previously.
