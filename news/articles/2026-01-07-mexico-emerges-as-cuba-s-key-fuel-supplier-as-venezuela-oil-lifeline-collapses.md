Venezuela supplied approximately a quarter of Cuba's total fuel demand before Maduro's removal, and analysts say the United States is likely to press Mexico to curtail its oil flows to the island, leaving Cuba facing an intensifying energy crisis with few alternative suppliers.

Mexico City — Mexico has emerged as Cuba's most significant oil supplier following the U.S. military operation that removed Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro from power, Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum said Wednesday.

Sheinbaum acknowledged that "with the current situation in Venezuela, Mexico has become an important supplier" of crude oil to Cuba but asserted that "no more oil is being sent than has been sent historically; there is no specific shipment." She said shipments are made via contracts or as humanitarian aid but provided no production figures.

The acknowledgment places Mexico in a potentially difficult position with the Trump administration, which has tightened its posture toward Cuba as part of its broader pressure campaign following the Venezuela operation. Analysts said the United States is likely to press Mexico to limit oil flows to the island.

## Cuba's supply gap

Venezuela's Maduro government had exported an average of 35,000 barrels per day to Cuba in the three months before his removal — approximately a quarter of the island's total daily fuel demand — according to Jorge Piñón, an energy analyst at the University of Texas at Austin's Energy Institute who tracks shipments using oil tracking services and satellite technology.

Cuba faces chronic energy shortages. Blackouts lasting up to eight hours and long lines at gas stations are routine across the island, conditions driven in part by the U.S. trade embargo imposed after the 1959 revolution that overthrew dictator Fulgencio Batista.

José Martínez, a 65-year-old former construction worker and resident of Old Havana, said Venezuela's upheaval will worsen those conditions. "The blackouts are going to intensify with all this," he said.

Street vendor Yeison Gálvez, 37, said dependence on Venezuelan fuel has shaped daily life on the island for as long as he can remember. "For as long as I can remember, we've depended on Venezuela for fuel. We're going to be walking even more," he said.

## Mexico's shipment data

From January through September 30, 2025, Mexico shipped 19,200 barrels per day to Cuba — 17,200 barrels of crude oil and 2,000 barrels of refined products — according to Pemex's most recent report to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission.

Piñón's independent tracking found a comparable figure of 22,000 barrels per day for the same period. He said the volume fell to 7,000 barrels per day after U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio's visit to Mexico City in September 2025.

Pemex valued the Cuba shipments at approximately $400 million in its SEC filing, describing them as sales through contracts at market price. Oscar Ocampo of the Mexican Institute for Competitiveness said it cannot be clearly determined whether the shipments were priced at market rate, sold at a discount with some form of quid pro quo — perhaps including the deployment of Cuban medical personnel to Mexico — or provided as humanitarian aid.

Cuba exports account for 3.3% of Mexico's total oil exports, according to the AP report. Their economic profitability is uncertain, Ocampo said, given that Pemex's production has continued to fall. He said 2025 is set to become the year Pemex exported the least crude oil in recent history, with less than 600,000 barrels per day, compared to more than 1 million barrels per day a few years prior.

## Transparency concerns

Since 2023, Mexico's oil shipments to Cuba have been channeled through Gasolinas Bienestar, a private Pemex subsidiary. Ocampo said the subsidiary's financial situation "is not transparent" and that the arrangement "incentivizes opacity."

Mexico has historically used oil shipments as a form of diplomatic support for Cuba during periods of crisis. After widespread protests in Cuba in 2021, Mexico sent approximately 100,000 barrels of fuel as humanitarian aid. In October 2024, Mexico sent more than 400,000 barrels in a matter of days following serious blackouts, according to Piñón.

## U.S. pressure anticipated

Analysts said Mexico is unlikely to increase its Cuba shipments in the current political environment. "The U.S. government would go bonkers," Piñón said.

"There will be more pressure from the United States regarding Cuba," Ocampo said, adding that oil will predictably be among the areas under pressure — a view shared by other analysts. The Mexican government has not commented on its future plans for Cuba shipments.

It is unclear whether any country would step in to increase oil exports to Cuba in the absence of Venezuelan supply.
