Iran's 440.9-kilogram stockpile of uranium enriched to 60% purity — a short technical step below weapons-grade — fits in roughly 18 to 20 transport cylinders light enough for two people to carry, according to one expert. The IAEA's inability to confirm where that material is located is the central concern animating analysts as Iran's government confronts an expanding protest movement it has violently suppressed.

VIENNA — The U.N. nuclear watchdog has not received information from Iran on the whereabouts of the country's highly enriched uranium stockpile since Iranian nuclear facilities were struck in a 12-day war last June, as analysts warned Monday that Iran's worsening political crisis raises the risk of theft, diversion, or radiological catastrophe.

The International Atomic Energy Agency said in a November report that it had lost "continuity of knowledge" regarding nuclear material at Iranian facilities affected by the conflict. A diplomat close to the agency confirmed Monday that the gap remained open, telling the Associated Press that the IAEA had still not received any information from Iran on the status or whereabouts of the stockpile. The diplomat spoke on condition of anonymity in line with diplomatic protocol.

Iran maintains a stockpile of 440.9 kilograms (972 pounds) of uranium enriched to 60% purity, according to the IAEA — a short technical step below the 90% threshold considered weapons-grade. Tehran has maintained that its nuclear program is peaceful.

"The risk is real but it is difficult to assess, given the unknowns regarding the status of the materials and the whereabouts," said Kelsey Davenport, director for nonproliferation policy at the Arms Control Association in Washington.

## Theft and diversion

David Albright, a former nuclear weapons inspector in Iraq and founder of the Institute for Science and International Security, said that in a scenario of internal chaos Iran could "lose the ability to protect its nuclear assets," describing the highly enriched uranium stockpile as "the most worrisome" element.

He said the stockpile fits in roughly 18 to 20 cylinders designed for transport, each weighing about 50 kilograms (110 pounds) when full. "Two people can easily carry it," Albright said of each container.

Davenport said the stockpile "could be diverted either to a covert program or stolen by a faction of the government or the military that wanted to retain the option of weaponization." She said the risk increases as the government feels threatened or destabilized, and that in a scenario of internal chaos, some of the material could be smuggled out of Iran or sold to non-state actors.

The concern has historical grounding. Following the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, highly enriched uranium and plutonium suitable for building nuclear weapons went missing as security eroded at formerly protected sites.

## Weaponization prospects

Both Albright and Davenport noted a theoretical possibility that Iran could attempt to build a nuclear device using its 60%-enriched uranium without further enrichment. Eric Brewer, a former U.S. intelligence analyst and deputy vice president at the Nuclear Threat Initiative, said a weapon constructed directly from 60%-enriched uranium rather than the standard weapons-grade 90% would require more material, making it "much bigger and bulkier and probably not well suited to delivery" on a missile. Such a device could still "be blown up in the desert," he said.

Brewer said the possibility that the current Iranian government pursues weaponization should not be "totally dismissed," but said most information indicates the highly enriched uranium "remains buried in a tunnel as a result of the U.S. strikes and is probably not easily accessible to the regime; at least not with some major risk of detection and another strike by the U.S. or Israel." He added that recent events "have also shown that the Supreme Leader has a very high bar for any decision to weaponize."

## Reactor sabotage

Albright warned that in a scenario of civil unrest, Iran's Bushehr nuclear power plant could become a target for sabotage. Bushehr is Iran's only commercial nuclear facility, located roughly 750 kilometers (465 miles) south of Tehran and fueled by Russian-produced uranium rather than Iran's own enriched stockpile.

He pointed to the 1982 attack by the armed wing of the African National Congress on South Africa's Koeberg Nuclear Power Station near Cape Town, an act of sabotage that caused significant damage but produced no nuclear fallout.

"If the Bushehr reactor has a major accident, the winds would carry the fallout within 12 to 15 hours to the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia and Oman," Albright said.

## Political backdrop

As of Monday, there was no sign that Iran had lost command and control of its security forces. Analysts noted, however, that the risk of a proliferation incident rises with the degree to which the government feels destabilized.

President Donald Trump called Saturday for an end to Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei's nearly 40-year rule, responding to Khamenei's public characterization of Trump as a "criminal" for supporting protesters. Khamenei had blamed demonstrators for causing thousands of deaths. Trump had, in recent days, appeared to step back from the prospect of a direct U.S. military strike on Iran.

A U.S. aircraft carrier that had been operating in the South China Sea passed Singapore overnight and entered the Strait of Malacca — a route that could bring it to the Middle East.
