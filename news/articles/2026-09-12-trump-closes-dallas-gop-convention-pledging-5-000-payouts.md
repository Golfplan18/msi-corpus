## Trump closes Dallas GOP convention pledging $5,000 payouts

**Subtype:** fact

- President Donald Trump closed the first Republican midterm convention in Dallas on Thursday with a pledge of $5,000 payouts to every US adult if Republicans retain both chambers of Congress in November, and a plea that voters "pretend that I'm on the ballot."
- Trump's Wednesday-night keynote on Fox News drew 2.5 million viewers per Nielsen data, while hundreds of members of Congress and candidates stayed away from the American Airlines Center and most upper-tier seats stood empty on both nights.
- Decision Desk HQ projects Democrats to capture the House 229-206, with the Senate hanging 50-50.
- Speakers including Ted Cruz, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and House Speaker Mike Johnson used anti-communist and Christian-faith messaging, while interviews with attendees surfaced reservations about the cost of living and the war in Iran that most main-stage speeches did not address.
- Vice President JD Vance drew "JD 48!" chants during Thursday's keynote, and multiple attendees named Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Florida Governor Ron DeSantis as potential 2028 contenders.

## Decision Desk HQ projects 229-206 Democratic House edge

Before handing the microphone to an opera singer, Trump told the crowd: "We're going to have a good time listening to music, because I'm getting a little bit tired of politics now." The Guardian reported that the host committee raised $45 million in three weeks at Trump's behest, pulling together a spectacle with familiar trappings — tall state delegations on the convention floor, fiery speeches and a celebratory balloon drop at the end — but no nominations or formal business. In the corridors of the American Airlines Center, attendees wore apparel emblazoned with phrases such as "Trump tribe," "Trump has our back" and "Proud deplorable," and Trump's image dominated the decor. Speaker after speaker lavished him with effusive praise.

The Guardian reported that the convention's central pitch — that voters should treat the November elections as a referendum on Trump himself — was received by his Democratic opponents with glee. Andy Beshear, the Democratic governor of Kentucky, told reporters on Thursday: "If Donald Trump wants to hold a convention saying this election is about him, I fully support his decision. He's telling me to pretend that he is on the ballot – I want people to pretend that he is on the ballot."

Holding a convention in a midterm year was, the Guardian wrote, "an attempt to make history in order to defy history." The party that holds the White House typically suffers midterm losses: George W. Bush's Republicans took a "thumping" in 2006, while Barack Obama's Democrats endured a "shellacking" in 2010. Trump's approval rating stood at a historic low, the Guardian reported, with the president polling in the low-to-mid 30s. Decision Desk HQ projects Democrats to capture the House 229-206, with the Senate hanging on a knife-edge at 50-50.

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. recalled meeting Trump soon after Trump survived a shooting in Butler, Pennsylvania: "I discovered a self-effacing man of superior intelligence, of empathy and compassion, with an encyclopedic grasp of the issues in molecular detail. And most of all, a near reckless courage to challenge vested interests and official orthodoxies."

The Guardian reported that, unable to point to falling grocery bills or peace in the Middle East, the convention leaned heavily into fear, painting what the newspaper described as a "nightmarish portrait" of what the United States would look like with Democrats back in charge. Numerous speeches, the Guardian reported, "sought to whip up a new red scare." Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned that Democrats wanted to transform the United States into a "socialist hellscape," while House Speaker Mike Johnson framed the midterms as a choice between "common sense and crazy." Many speakers emphasized their own Christian faith while stressing the godlessness of communism and conflating it with threats from outside America's borders, at times invoking the 25th anniversary of the September 11, 2001, attacks.

Images of the influencer Hasan Piker and other left-wing figures loomed on giant video screens as a narrator's voice boomed: "Today's extreme left hasn't simply moved away from Republicans. It has moved away from Democrats in its own party. Far beyond the common sense of the American people. This is no longer simply Republican versus Democrat or conservative versus liberal. Today, the choice is unmistakable. Extreme versus mainstream."

Texas Senator Ted Cruz warned of what he called "a new and dangerous red-green alliance – communists and Islamists coming together and sweeping over the Democrat party." Cruz told the arena: "They are united because they share the same enemies. They hate Christians, they hate Jews, they hate capitalism, they hate the constitution, and they hate America!" As Cruz singled out leading progressives by name — telling the crowd, "When people tell you who they are, believe them. When Hasan Piker says, America deserved 9/11, believe him. When Mamdani says he wants to seize the means of production, he means it. When Abdul El-Sayed says he has an 'obligation' to comply with Sharia law, he means it. And when James Talarico says he wants to dismantle capitalism, he means this" — members of the crowd shouted, "He should be shot!"

Voters' economic and foreign-policy concerns received limited attention from the convention's main-stage speakers. Gregorio Heise, a 59-year-old disabled Army veteran from Texas, described Trump's second term as "friggin' awesome," explaining: "I prefer to pay $5 a gallon [of gas] and put my head on a pillow and wake up knowing this country is still here than watching it go boom with a nuke. No, thank you. I'm a vet and I didn't serve this country to watch it go to crap." Joseph Ditto, a 50-year-old statistician from suburban Minneapolis, told the Guardian he was frustrated with the administration's foreign policy: "I'm not a fan of the war in Iran. I hope it's over quickly. I voted for the anti-war Trump, so that's frustrating."

Pavel Sokolov, the 33-year-old vice-chair of the New Jersey Republican Party, drew on his Soviet background in his remarks: "I was born in the former Soviet Union; my parents and I fled that exact rhetoric. It always starts nice and friendly, saying everyone must pay their fair share. But it inevitably ends up the same. In the Soviet Union, ostensibly everyone was equal, yet somehow the party bosses ate caviar every day."

Trump dismissed voter concerns about inflation as exaggerated, telling the audience that the word "affordable" was "fake." Most speakers, the Guardian reported, swerved past voters' everyday concerns about the cost of living and Trump's war of choice in Iran.

California Congresswoman Sydney Kamlager-Dove, who traveled to Dallas to counter-message, told the Guardian: "Last night, Donald Trump was talking about how he wanted to build a big ballroom like they have in China. The average American family can't afford groceries or gas, and he's fantasising about a ballroom in the people's house. This entire event is built around one man who likes to hear himself talk." Democrats, who held three midterm conventions in 1974, 1978 and 1982 with mixed results, have no plans for a rival event this time, though some Democrats traveled to Dallas to counter the Republican messaging.

A recurring theme through both nights was the question of who inherits the MAGA movement when Trump, the 45th and 47th president, leaves office. Vice President JD Vance drew chants of "JD 48!" as he delivered Thursday night's keynote address, during which he described Democrats as "the party that despises America and the people who built it." When a protester waving a Mexican flag briefly interrupted his speech, Vance responded: "Well, my friend, if you love Mexico so much, get your ass over there. I'll buy you the plane ticket."

In the concourses, delegates were sizing up the potential 2028 field. While Vance commands a substantial lead in early polling among Republican primary voters, several other names — most notably Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Florida Governor Ron DeSantis — were frequently cited. Heise told the Guardian: "Marco Rubio is doing everything. He's building on the foundations this country was created on in 1776." Chris Daniel, a 44-year-old delegate from Houston who has attended every Republican convention since 2004, said: "I prefer Marco Rubio over Vance if I had to pick one today. He has proven his ability to lead in any capacity." Rusat Ramgopal, the 26-year-old executive director of the Queens County Republican Party in New York, predicted a more orderly process: "Instead of a divisive primary between Vance and Rubio, I think the two of them and the president will sit down in a room and decide the ticket. It just depends on who is top of the ticket and who is vice president."

Boston College political science professor David Hopkins assessed the strategic logic of tying the midterms so tightly to Trump. "There's been a lot of journalistic dunking on Trump saying that making the election more about him is an ego trip winning out over smart strategy," Hopkins said. "That's not an unreasonable take but there's another way to look at it. The worst-case scenario for Republicans is that Republican voters stay home." Hopkins noted that Trump commands a unique, low-propensity "Make America great again" (Maga) electorate that turns out passionately for him but frequently ignores down-ballot congressional races when his name is absent. "If they can't get those people out, it looks like a real national wipeout," Hopkins said.

Republicans declared the convention a triumph. Outside the arena, the verdict was less enthusiastic. Veteran pollster John Zogby described the two-day gathering as an "act of desperation" that highlighted internal vulnerabilities rather than strength. "The president promised that the war in Iran would end by August, inflation would be down by August, fuel prices would be low again by August," Zogby said. "None of those have been achieved. We have moved from the politics of anger into the politics of disappointment."
