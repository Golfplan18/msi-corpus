Iranian state media has broadcast at least 97 confessions from detained protesters since demonstrations began on Dec. 28, a pace that a U.S.-based rights group describes as unprecedented and that activists say often follows psychological or physical torture. The Human Rights Activists News Agency, which is tracking the videos, says the confessions frequently involve charges that carry the death penalty.

The videos represent a documented escalation of a tactic rights organizations say Iran has deployed for decades to suppress dissent, arriving as the reported death toll from the current protests has reached more than 2,500 and the number of arrests has exceeded 18,100, according to the Human Rights Activists News Agency.

The confession videos, as described by the Associated Press, show detainees handcuffed with their faces blurred. Iran's state television — the only broadcaster in the country — airs the recordings against dramatic background music interspersed with footage appearing to show protesters attacking security forces. Some videos feature what authorities describe as homemade weapons; others show suspects in security camera footage apparently setting fires or destroying property. Many confessions include references to Israel or the United States.

Iranian officials have said the protests are foreign-orchestrated operations. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the violence in the demonstrations must be foreign-influenced because Iranians would never set mosques on fire. Iran's mission to the United Nations did not respond to a request for comment from the Associated Press.

"These rights violations compound on top of each other and lead to horrible outcomes. This is a pattern that's been implemented by the regime time and time again," said Skylar Thompson, deputy director of the Human Rights Activists News Agency.

## An unprecedented pace

The volume of confessions aired since Dec. 28 far exceeds what Iranian state television broadcast in any comparable prior period, Thompson told the AP. From 2010 to 2020, around 350 forced confessions were broadcast on state media over the entire decade, according to activist groups Justice for Iran and the International Federation for Human Rights, which compiled the last major study on the topic. The rights group Together Against the Death Penalty reported 40 to 60 confessions were aired in all of 2025.

After the 2022 protests over the death of Mahsa Amini — who died following her arrest by Iran's morality police for allegedly not wearing her hijab to the authorities' liking — Iran Human Rights and Together Against the Death Penalty reported at least 37 televised confessions of people facing the death penalty in the weeks that followed. More than 500 people were killed and over 22,000 were detained during the monthslong protests.

Thompson said she is "gravely concerned" over a potential surge in executions connected to the current protests, noting that many of the video confessions involve serious security-related offenses that carry the death penalty.

## Executions and espionage

Iran executed 975 people in 2024, the highest number since 2015, according to a United Nations report. Four of the executions were carried out publicly; Iran carries out executions by hanging. According to the U.N. report, most people in Iran are executed for drug-related offenses or murder. Security-related offenses, such as espionage, accounted for just 3% of executions in 2024.

Tehran has executed 12 people for espionage since a 12-day war with Israel in June. The most recent took place the week before the AP article's publication, when Iranian authorities said a man accused of spying for Israel's Mossad intelligence agency in exchange for cryptocurrency had been executed. The state-run IRNA news agency reported that the man confessed to the spying charges.

## A long history

The use of televised confessions in Iran dates to the period following the 1979 Islamic Revolution, when state television aired confessions from suspected members of communist groups and others. Even Mehdi Bazargan, Iran's first prime minister after the revolution, warned at one point that he could be detained and placed on television "repeating things like a parrot."

A 2014 U.N. Special Rapporteur human rights report on Iran found that among interviews with previously detained individuals, 70% said coerced information or confessions had been used in their hearings. In nearly half those cases, the trial lasted just a few minutes.

In January 2023, the European Parliament adopted a resolution strongly condemning "the Islamic Republic's policy of forcing confessions using torture, intimidation, threats against family members or other forms of duress, and the use of these forced confessions to convict and sentence protesters."

## Toll of the current protests

Since demonstrations began on Dec. 28, 18,100 people have been arrested and more than 2,500 have been killed, the vast majority of them protesters, according to the Human Rights Activists News Agency. The organization relies on a network of activists inside Iran that confirms all reported fatalities. The Iranian government has not released overall casualty figures. The Associated Press said it was unable to independently assess the toll.

Human rights organizations and Western governments had already condemned Iran's increasing use of capital punishment for political and espionage-related offenses before the current demonstrations began. Activists say many of the resulting convictions rely on coerced confessions and that trials often take place behind closed doors without access to independent legal representation.
