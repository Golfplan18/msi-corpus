Three U.S. Catholic cardinals issued a joint statement Monday urging the Trump administration to ground its foreign policy in ethical principles, warning that U.S. military action in Venezuela, threats to acquire Greenland, and cuts to foreign aid risk causing "vast suffering" rather than promoting peace.

Cardinals Blase Cupich of Chicago, Robert McElroy of Washington, and Joseph Tobin of Newark, N.J., warned in the statement that without a moral vision, the current debate over Washington's foreign policy was mired in "polarization, partisanship, and narrow economic and social interests," according to the Associated Press.

The declaration marks the second time in two months that members of the U.S. Catholic hierarchy have publicly challenged the Trump administration on human-dignity grounds. In November, the entire U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops condemned the administration's mass deportation of migrants and their "vilification" in public discourse.

"Most of the United States and the world are adrift morally in terms of foreign policy," McElroy told the AP. "I still believe the United States has a tremendous impact upon the world."

## Rooted in Pope Leo's January address

The three cardinals — described by the AP as prominent figures in the more progressive wing of the U.S. church — took as their starting point a major foreign policy address Pope Leo XIV delivered January 9 to ambassadors accredited to the Holy See.

Leo, described as history's first U.S.-born pope, delivered the nearly 45-minute speech almost entirely in English, denouncing how nations were using force to assert their dominion worldwide and warning the practice was "completely undermining" peace and the post-World War II international legal order. He did not name individual countries, but the AP reported the remarks came against the backdrop of the recent U.S. military operation in Venezuela, U.S. threats regarding Greenland, and Russia's ongoing war in Ukraine.

Cupich said the hearing from fellow cardinals at a January 7–8 Vatican meeting — where participants expressed alarm about U.S. actions in Venezuela, foreign aid cuts, and Greenland threats — provided the initial impetus. Leo's address the following day gave the American cardinals the language they needed, allowing them to "piggyback on" the pope's words, Cupich said.

## Questioning the use of force

The joint statement cited Venezuela, Greenland, and Ukraine as having "raised basic questions about the use of military force and the meaning of peace," alongside the administration's foreign aid reductions.

"We renounce war as an instrument for narrow national interests and proclaim that military action must be seen only as a last resort in extreme situations, not a normal instrument of national policy," the cardinals wrote. "We seek a foreign policy that respects and advances the right to human life, religious liberty, and the enhancement of human dignity throughout the world, especially through economic assistance."

Cupich acknowledged that the prosecution of Nicolás Maduro could be seen positively, but said the manner of the Venezuela operation was problematic.

"When we go ahead and do it in such a way that is portrayed as saying, 'Because we can do it, we're going to do it, that might makes right' — that's a troublesome development," he said. "There's the rule of law that should be followed."

Trump has insisted the capture of Maduro was legal. On Greenland, Trump has argued the United States needs control of the resource-rich island — a semiautonomous region of NATO ally Denmark — for national security.

## Foreign aid cuts draw concern

Tobin, who ministered in more than 70 countries as a Redemptorist priest and the order's superior general, expressed concern about the administration's reductions to the U.S. Agency for International Development. The Trump administration significantly reduced USAID, saying its projects advanced a liberal agenda and were a waste of money.

"It can't be that my prosperity is predicated on inhuman treatment of others," Tobin said. "The real argument isn't just my right or individual rights, but what is the common good."

## Conference endorses the emphasis

The U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops was consulted on the statement. Its president, Archbishop Paul Coakley, "supports the emphasis placed by the cardinals on Pope Leo's teaching in these times," spokesperson Chieko Noguchi said.

The three cardinals said their goal was not to criticize the administration but to encourage the United States to regain its moral standing in the world by pursuing a foreign policy ethically guided and oriented toward the common good.

"We're not endorsing a political party or a political movement," Tobin said. "They can make an argument of basic human decency."

The White House did not respond to the AP's request for comment.
