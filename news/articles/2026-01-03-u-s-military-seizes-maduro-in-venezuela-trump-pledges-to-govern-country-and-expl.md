CARACAS — U.S. military forces removed Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro from power in a pre-dawn raid Saturday, seizing him at his home on a military base in Caracas and transferring him to federal custody in New York to face narco-terrorism charges, the Associated Press reported. Hours after the operation, President Donald Trump said at a press conference at his Mar-a-Lago estate in Florida that the United States would administer Venezuela at least temporarily and sell large quantities of the country's oil to other nations.

The operation, which legal experts and members of Congress from both parties immediately questioned for lacking congressional authorization, represented the most assertive U.S. military intervention to force a change of government since the 2003 invasion of Iraq, according to the AP.

The raid concludes a months-long escalating U.S. pressure campaign against Maduro's government and opens immediate questions about the legal authority for the operation, the scope of Trump administration plans to control a neighboring sovereign nation, and regional stability — with the United Nations Security Council scheduled to convene Monday at Colombia's request.

## Capture and transfer to U.S. custody

Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, were seized before dawn at their home on a military base, then taken aboard the USS Iwo Jima before being flown to the United States, the AP reported. A plane believed to be carrying Maduro landed at approximately 4:30 p.m. Saturday at an airport in the northern suburbs of New York City.

Maduro was escorted from the aircraft and brought by helicopter to Manhattan, where a convoy of law enforcement vehicles transported him to a nearby DEA office. He is expected to be held at a federal prison in Brooklyn while awaiting trial.

The U.S. Department of Justice issued a new indictment Saturday against Maduro and Flores, describing the Venezuelan government as a corrupt and illegitimate regime sustained by a drug trafficking operation flooding the United States with cocaine. Maduro and other Venezuelan officials had originally been charged with conspiracy to commit narco-terrorism in 2020. The United States has not recognized Maduro as Venezuela's legitimate president.

## Trump announces plans to govern and exploit oil

Speaking at Mar-a-Lago, Trump said U.S. forces had cut off nearly all electricity to Caracas during the early morning raid and described the operation as extremely successful. He said it should serve as a warning to anyone who threatens U.S. sovereignty or endangers American lives.

Trump said the United States would administer Venezuela until a safe, proper, and judicious transition of power could be arranged, and that the country's oil infrastructure would be repaired and its oil sold to other nations in large quantities. He did not identify immediate partners or timelines for those plans.

Venezuelan state television continued broadcasting pro-Maduro content hours after the raid, the AP reported, and there were no immediate signs that U.S. administration of the country had begun.

General Dan Caine, Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, said U.S. forces had spent months rehearsing the operation, learning detailed information about Maduro's movements, routines, and personal habits.

## Legal questions and congressional concern

The operation was conducted without specific congressional authorization, according to the AP. Legal experts raised immediate questions about its lawfulness under both U.S. and international law.

Rep. Jim Himes of Connecticut, the ranking Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, said he had seen no evidence justifying an attack on Venezuela without congressional approval. He demanded the administration immediately provide a legal justification for the operation and a plan for ensuring regional stability.

Members of Congress from both parties had previously expressed concern about earlier U.S. military operations near Venezuela's coast — including strikes on vessels the administration described as drug-trafficking boats — which also lacked specific congressional authorization.

The United Nations Security Council was scheduled to hold a meeting Monday to discuss the U.S. operations in Venezuela, at Colombia's request, according to a diplomat speaking on condition of anonymity because the meeting had not been publicly announced.

## Divided reaction inside Venezuela

Venezuelan Vice President Delcy Rodríguez demanded the United States release Maduro, calling him the country's only legitimate president. Before the country's Supreme Court later ordered her to assume the interim presidency, Rodríguez said in a televised appearance that she had no plans to take power.

Reaction in Caracas was sharply divided. Armed individuals and uniformed members of a civilian militia occupied a neighborhood long considered a stronghold of the ruling party. Caracas Mayor Carmen Meléndez joined a crowd calling for Maduro's return.

In other parts of the capital, streets remained largely empty hours after the raid as residents absorbed the events. Some areas remained without electricity. One resident, Noris Prada, told the AP he felt frightened and that many families had been unable to sleep.

In Doral, Florida — home to the largest Venezuelan community in the United States — crowds celebrated, wrapping themselves in Venezuelan flags, listening to music, and chanting for freedom.

Videos verified by the AP showed tracer fire, smoke, and repeated explosions illuminating the night sky over Caracas and an unidentified coastal city during the operation. The raid lasted less than 30 minutes and involved at least seven explosions, the AP reported.

Rodríguez said some Venezuelan civilians and military members died in the operation but did not provide a number. Trump said some U.S. forces were injured but that none died.

## Background and context

The operation came after months of escalating U.S. pressure against Maduro's government, including a significant U.S. military buildup in waters off South America and strikes on boats the administration accused of drug trafficking in the Pacific and Caribbean. The CIA had reportedly conducted a drone strike the previous week against a docking area allegedly used by Venezuelan drug traffickers.

Maduro had publicly accused the United States of pursuing a thinly veiled plan to remove him from power. The operation drew comparisons to the 1989 U.S. invasion of Panama, which led to the capture and removal of strongman Manuel Antonio Noriega.

Venezuela's governing party has held power since 1999, when Hugo Chávez took office. Maduro assumed the presidency following Chávez's death in 2013. His 2018 reelection was widely condemned as fraudulent after the main opposition parties were barred from participating. In the 2024 elections, electoral authorities loyal to the ruling party declared Maduro the winner hours after polls closed, but the opposition said it had gathered evidence showing he lost by more than a 2-to-1 margin.
