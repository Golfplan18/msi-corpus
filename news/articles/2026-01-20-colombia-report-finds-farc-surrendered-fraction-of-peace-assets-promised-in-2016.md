The asset shortfall leaves victim-reparation programs severely underfunded nearly a decade after the historic deal ended more than 50 years of FARC insurgency, and a special peace tribunal established under the agreement separately lacks the resources to implement its rulings.

BOGOTÁ, Colombia — Colombia's Inspector General's Office said Tuesday that former FARC rebels have surrendered only a small fraction of the gold, land, and cash they promised under the 2016 peace agreement, leaving reparations for victims of the country's decades-long armed conflict severely underfunded.

The agency's report found that the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia has handed over 252 kilograms (556 pounds) of the 444 kilograms (979 pounds) of gold it pledged to surrender — roughly 57 percent of the total. The group has transferred one of the 722 rural properties it promised, and 17 percent of the promised cash.

## What the deal required

The 2016 agreement ended more than five decades of armed conflict between the FARC and the Colombian government. Under its terms, FARC fighters were spared prison sentences, allowed to form a political party, and given 10 reserved seats in Colombia's congress.

In exchange, the group's leaders agreed to hand over FARC assets to fund reparation projects, including infrastructure in rural areas, memorials to conflict victims, and truth-and-reconciliation events. Those assets were to be transferred to a government body known as the Society of Special Assets, which would sell them and direct the proceeds to an agency responsible for helping victims.

## FARC cites security and title problems

José Lisandro Lascarro, a former FARC commander known as "Pastor Alape," told The Associated Press that the group had done its best to comply with the asset-handover requirements, in a process supervised by the United Nations.

"We handed over all of the assets that we could turn in, before other groups occupied our territory," Lascarro said.

He said cash buried in chests in remote areas is now inaccessible because those territories are controlled by other armed groups. Lascarro also said the FARC held no formal title documents for its rural properties, making it impossible to transfer land or buildings to the government.

The FARC's 2016 disarmament was followed by a surge in violence in parts of rural Colombia, where several smaller armed groups have been fighting for control of territory the FARC once held.

## Tribunal also underfunded

The Inspector General's report also found that the Special Jurisdiction for Peace — a tribunal established under the 2016 deal — currently lacks funds to implement its rulings, which involve truth-finding and reparation activities for conflict victims.

Last year the tribunal issued calls for reparations to victims of FARC kidnappings and to victims of extrajudicial executions carried out by the Colombian military. The budget currently reserved for those reparations is only 17 percent of what is required, the Inspector General's Office said.
