A Hong Kong court heard arguments Monday on the sentencing of Jimmy Lai, the 78-year-old founder of the now-defunct Apple Daily newspaper, who was convicted in December under the city's national security law. Lai faces a maximum penalty of life imprisonment on charges of conspiring to collude with foreign forces and conspiring to publish seditious articles. No sentencing date was announced; the four-day mitigation hearings were expected to continue Tuesday with arguments on Lai's co-defendants.

The case has drawn criticism from the United States and Britain and is widely regarded as a test of press freedom in the former British colony since Beijing imposed its national security law in 2020 following mass anti-government protests. Lai, a British citizen, has been held in detention since his arrest that year.

## Defense argues age and health weigh against lengthy term

Lai's lawyer Robert Pang told the three-judge panel that his client's age, health conditions, and solitary confinement would make any prison term "more burdensome" than it would be for a younger, healthier inmate.

"Every day he spends in prison," Pang said, "brings him closer to the end of his life."

Pang said Lai suffers from hypertension, diabetes, and a blocked vein in one eye. He also noted that Lai's weight had dropped 11 kilograms (about 24 pounds) within a single year at one point during detention, though the court heard he had since regained some of that weight.

Prosecutor Anthony Chau contested the portrait of deteriorating health, telling the court that Lai's condition was stable. Chau said Lai weighed 79.2 kilograms (about 175 pounds) as of the most recent measurement and remained medically classified as obese by Asian adult standards — a comment that drew brief laughter from those in the public gallery, including Lai himself.

Pang also argued that Lai's solitary confinement — which Chau said was at Lai's own request — rendered his prison conditions harsher than those of other inmates. Judge Esther Toh, one of three government-vetted judges on the panel, questioned relying on physical appearance to assess health, noting that cameras can distort how a person looks.

## Judges found Lai directed the conspiracies

When Lai entered the courtroom, he pressed his palms together in an apparent gesture of gratitude and greeted a convicted Apple Daily editor also in the dock. Dozens of supporters had lined up outside the court building before sunrise to secure seats in the public gallery.

In their December verdict, the judges wrote that Lai had spearheaded the conspiracies and had made a "constant invitation" to the United States to help bring down the Chinese government, with Hong Kong's interests as the stated rationale. Lai's lawyers had acknowledged during the 156-day trial that he called for foreign sanctions before the security law took effect, but argued he stopped doing so to comply with the law and that his actions were protected by freedom of expression. The judges rejected those arguments, finding that Lai had never wavered in his intention to destabilize the ruling Communist Party even after the law's enactment, though he pursued that goal less explicitly.

Lai was convicted of two counts of conspiracy to commit collusion with foreign forces to endanger national security and one count of conspiracy to distribute seditious publications. He pleaded not guilty to all charges.

## Co-defendants may receive reduced terms

Six former Apple Daily senior executives — including publisher Cheung Kim-hung, associate publisher Chan Pui-man, editor-in-chief Ryan Law, executive editor-in-chief Lam Man-chung, and editorial writers Fung Wai-kong and Yeung Ching-kee — entered guilty pleas, along with two activists, Andy Li and Chan Tsz-wah. Several served as prosecution witnesses during the trial.

Lawyers for the two activists argued their clients' final sentences should be cut to under 10 years in part because of their cooperation. Cheung's attorney Lucas Lau also cited his client's assistance to prosecutors as a factor warranting a lighter penalty. Under the security law, a reduced penalty may be granted to those who provide information about offenses committed by others.

## U.S. and Britain press for release

The verdict drew condemnation from foreign governments. U.S. President Donald Trump, who had previously raised Lai's case directly with Chinese President Xi Jinping, said after the verdict that he had asked Xi "to consider his release." British Prime Minister Keir Starmer said his government has made securing Lai's release a priority. Beijing has rejected foreign criticism, saying its judicial authorities perform their duties according to law.

Retiree Simon Ng, a former Apple Daily reader who said he had been waiting in line since Friday morning to attend the hearing, told the Associated Press he came to send Lai a message.

"I want to let him know that he's not alone," Ng said. "Many people support him."
