The credits, originally designed to bridge an average price gap of roughly $9,000 between new electric and gas-powered vehicles, helped lower- and middle-income Americans enter the EV market — a population advocates warn will be priced out once the incentives disappear.

Congress passed a sweeping tax and spending cut bill Thursday that ends federal tax incentives for electric vehicles, eliminating a $7,500 credit for new EV buyers and up to $4,000 for those purchasing used ones. Consumers who want to claim the credits must qualify by Sept. 30.

The credits had been designed to help offset the higher up-front cost of electric vehicles. According to Kelley Blue Book, the average new EV costs roughly $9,000 more than the average new gas-powered car in the United States. Used EVs run about $2,000 more than comparable gas vehicles on average.

"That's really disappointing because ... they're just a really great way to reduce transportation energy cost burden," said Ingrid Malmgren, Senior Policy Director at the nonprofit advocacy group Plug In America.

Malmgren said that without the federal incentives — combined with similar credits available in many states — electric vehicles will become unaffordable to many lower- and middle-income Americans.

## Long-term costs still favor EVs

Despite the higher purchase price, experts say electric vehicles typically cost less over the life of the vehicle.

"Quickly you'll end up paying less than a gas car because it costs much less to fuel, and it needs almost nothing for maintenance," Malmgren said. She said EV owners pay less in upkeep because the cars have fewer moving parts and require less frequent servicing.

A 2020 study published in the academic journal Joule found that the average EV in the United States saves drivers $7,700 in fuel costs over a 15-year life span compared to a gas-powered car. The study found that charging at home during off-peak hours in Washington state — where electricity is relatively affordable — could yield fuel savings exceeding $14,000 over 15 years. The study did not account for purchase price, maintenance costs, or tax credits.

The point at which lifetime savings outweigh the higher initial price varies by vehicle type, driving frequency, and local fuel costs, Malmgren said. Online calculators can help prospective buyers estimate their own break-even point based on those criteria.

## Environmental case holds, even in coal states

Manufacturing an electric vehicle typically generates more emissions than producing a gas-powered car. But experts say the balance shifts quickly once both vehicles are on the road.

After traveling roughly 15,000 miles — slightly more than the average American drives in a year — the combined manufacturing and driving emissions of an EV and a gas car are comparable, said Peter Slowik, U.S. Passenger Vehicles Lead for the International Council on Clean Transportation. Every mile after that widens the gap in the EV's favor.

"They are a no-brainer," Slowik said. "Electric vehicles are already inherently so much more efficient."

By the end of a vehicle's life, emissions from the average EV are roughly half those of the average gas car, according to the U.S. Department of Energy.

A 2023 analysis by Yale Climate Connections found that EVs produce less carbon dioxide than equivalent gas-powered cars even in states where electricity generation relies heavily on coal. In West Virginia, among the most coal-dependent states, an EV still produces 31% less pollution than a comparable gas vehicle, according to that analysis.

Slowik attributed the efficiency advantage to electric motors' ability to convert energy into forward motion. The Tesla Model Y and Tesla Model 3 — the two most popular EVs in the United States — can travel more than 100 miles on energy equivalent to that contained in a gallon of gasoline.

"If you compare that to a 25-mpg gasoline vehicle, that's already four to five times more efficient," Slowik said.
