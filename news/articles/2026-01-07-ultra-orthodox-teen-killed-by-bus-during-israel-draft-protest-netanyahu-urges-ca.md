JERUSALEM — A 14-year-old yeshiva student was killed Tuesday evening in Jerusalem when a bus driver ran over him during a large gathering of ultra-Orthodox Jewish demonstrators protesting legislation that would require members of their community to serve in Israel's military, police said.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu on Wednesday called for restraint in the aftermath of the death. "I call for restraint to prevent the mood from becoming further inflamed so that, heaven forbid, we do not have additional tragedies," Netanyahu said in a statement, adding that the incident would be thoroughly investigated.

The killing deepens a protracted confrontation between Israeli authorities and the ultra-Orthodox community, known as Haredim, as the government weighs mandatory military conscription for a population that has been broadly exempt from compulsory service since Israel's founding in 1948.

Police identified the victim as Yosef Eisenthal, 14. Video from the protest obtained by the Associated Press showed Eisenthal trapped under the vehicle while the driver continued forward for several meters as onlookers jostled and screamed.

Officers arrested and questioned the bus driver, who told investigators he was attacked by protesters before his vehicle struck Eisenthal. Police said Wednesday that the driver's arrest had been extended until January 15. He has not been charged.

A police spokesperson said protesters had been blocking the road and throwing eggs and other objects at officers.

## Background

When Israel was founded in 1948, a small number of gifted ultra-Orthodox scholars were granted exemptions from compulsory military service. With backing from politically powerful religious parties, the scope of those exemptions expanded substantially over the following decades.

Support for curtailing the exemption has grown among many secular Israelis, particularly those who have served multiple rounds of duty in the ongoing war between Israel and the Palestinian militant group Hamas in Gaza. Measures to draft the ultra-Orthodox have drawn staunch opposition and occasional violence from religious protesters, who argue that military service would destroy their way of life.

The standoff presents a political challenge for Netanyahu, who depends on the support of ultra-Orthodox religious parties to maintain his governing coalition in the Israeli Parliament.
