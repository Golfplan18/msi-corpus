## Summary

**Subtype:** fact

- A week of back-to-back chemical emergencies on the West Coast — a fatal tank rupture at a Washington paper mill and an overheating tank in Southern California that prompted 50,000 evacuations — has exposed the fragmented system of agencies responsible for overseeing the millions of chemical tanks across the United States.
- The Associated Press reviewed regulatory records and found that local, state, and federal agencies all share responsibility for tank safety, but no single authority oversees all aspects of storage and maintenance.

A fatal chemical tank rupture at a Longview, Washington, paper mill and a near-catastrophic overheating tank in Southern California, occurring within days of each other, have thrust the nation’s chemical tank safety oversight into the spotlight. An Associated Press review found that responsibility for keeping the country’s millions of chemical tanks safe is spread across local, state, and federal agencies, with no single entity holding full authority over inspecting and maintaining these massive containers.

The Washington state incident, which occurred Tuesday, involved a large tank that held a corrosive chemical and ruptured, killing two workers and possibly leaving nine others unaccounted for, according to local authorities cited by the AP. The second alert, late last week, unfolded in Southern California when a chemical tank overheated, threatening a catastrophic explosion; authorities ordered the evacuation of about 50,000 people while they worked to mitigate the danger, and the risk was later lowered enough for residents to return home.

The overlapping crises have drawn attention to the patchwork nature of the regulatory system that governs chemical tank storage. The AP review of agency records found that the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, and various state and local bodies each hold a role in setting and enforcing safety standards, but no single agency is tasked with overseeing every facet of tank maintenance and emergency preparedness. Experts cited by the AP said that tank failures are extremely uncommon when facilities adhere to rigorous inspection and maintenance schedules, suggesting that lapses in enforcement or coordination can create windows for disaster.

The Washington paper mill is one of thousands of industrial facilities nationwide that handle hazardous materials daily. The Southern California tank, which authorities said held a chemical used in industrial processes, overheated to a critical level before authorities intervened. While the specific causes of both incidents remain under investigation, the cluster of emergencies in a single week — coming after a series of chemical-related scares in recent months — has prompted renewed calls from safety advocates for clearer regulatory accountability.

The Associated Press’s analysis underscores that, while the vast majority of chemical tanks operate safely for decades, the sheer volume of storage infrastructure — millions of tanks across the country — makes comprehensive oversight a significant challenge. The question of whether current regulatory frameworks are adequate to prevent more disasters like the one in Washington, or to contain near-misses like the Southern California overheating, is likely to face fresh scrutiny from Congress and state legislatures in the coming weeks.
