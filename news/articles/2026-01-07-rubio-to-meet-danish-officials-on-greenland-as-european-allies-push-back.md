The planned talks come as the Trump administration has declined to rule out military force to acquire the self-governing Danish territory, drawing pushback from European allies and members of Congress in both parties.

WASHINGTON — Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Wednesday he plans to meet with Danish and Greenlandic officials next week to discuss the Trump administration's bid to acquire Greenland, as leaders from six European nations joined Denmark in warning that the mineral-rich Arctic island "belongs to its people."

The planned talks mark the first confirmed diplomatic engagement after Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen and Greenland's Foreign Minister Vivian Motzfeldt requested the session, according to a statement posted Tuesday to Greenland's government website. The statement noted that previous requests for a meeting had not been successful.

## Administration's stated intentions

"That's always been the president's intent from the very beginning," Rubio told reporters in Washington. "He's not the first U.S. president that has examined or looked at how we could acquire Greenland."

In a classified briefing Monday evening on Capitol Hill, Rubio told a select group of U.S. lawmakers that the administration's intention was to eventually purchase the island rather than use military force, according to a person with knowledge of his comments who was granted anonymity because it was a private discussion. The Wall Street Journal first reported those remarks.

The Trump administration has revived its push for Greenland following the capture of former Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro, framing control of the world's largest island as necessary for U.S. security in the face of rising threats from China and Russia in the Arctic.

Asked Wednesday whether the administration was willing to risk the NATO alliance by potentially pursuing a military option, Rubio declined to answer directly. "I'm not here to talk about Denmark or military intervention, I'll be meeting with them next week, we'll have those conversations with them then, but I don't have anything further to add to that," he said. He added that every president retains the option to address national security threats through military means.

White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt said Tuesday that using the military to acquire Greenland was an option. She told reporters Wednesday that "the president's first option always has been diplomacy."

## European governments respond

The leaders of France, Germany, Italy, Poland, Spain, and the United Kingdom joined Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen in issuing a statement reaffirming that Greenland, which guards the Arctic and North Atlantic approaches to North America, "belongs to its people." Frederiksen has warned that a U.S. takeover would amount to the end of NATO.

Maria Martisiute, a defense analyst at the European Policy Centre think tank, said the joint statement signaled that European governments viewed the administration's language as a direct threat to alliance norms. "The Nordics do not lightly make statements like this," she told the Associated Press. "But it is Trump whose very bombastic language bordering on direct threats and intimidation is threatening the fact to another ally by saying, 'I will control or annex the territory.'"

## Mixed reaction in Congress

Alaska Sen. Lisa Murkowski said she hated "the rhetoric around either acquiring Greenland by purchase or by force," adding, "I think that it is very, very unsettling."

Democratic Sen. Jeanne Shaheen of New Hampshire and Republican Sen. Thom Tillis of North Carolina, co-chairs of the bipartisan Senate NATO Observer Group, issued a joint statement saying the U.S. must honor its treaty obligations to Denmark. "Any suggestion that our nation would subject a fellow NATO ally to coercion or external pressure undermines the very principles of self-determination that our Alliance exists to defend," they said.

Some Republican senators stopped short of opposing the administration's broader ambitions. Kansas Sen. Roger Marshall said he hoped "we can work out a deal." North Dakota Sen. John Hoeven said some of the discussion about taking Greenland by force had been "misconstrued."

"One of the things about President Trump, you may have noticed, is he keeps our adversaries off balance by making sure they don't know what we're going to do," Hoeven said.

## Strategic calculus disputed

Thomas Crosbie, an associate professor of military operations at the Royal Danish Defense College, said the U.S. would gain no security advantage from annexing Greenland that it does not already have.

"The United States will gain no advantage if its flag is flying in Nuuk versus the Greenlandic flag," Crosbie told the AP. "There's no benefits to them because they already enjoy all of the advantages they want. If there's any specific security access that they want to improve American security, they'll be given it as a matter of course, as a trusted ally. So this has nothing to do with improving national security for the United States."

The U.S. Department of Defense operates Pituffik Space Base in northwestern Greenland. Denmark's parliament approved a bill in June to allow U.S. military bases on Danish soil, widening a 2023 agreement made with the Biden administration that gave U.S. troops broad access to Danish air bases. Denmark's foreign minister has said the agreement could be terminated if the U.S. attempts to annex Greenland.

Crosbie said U.S. military personnel already stationed at Pituffik could assert symbolic control without a conventional military operation. "They don't need to bring any firepower. They don't need to bring anybody," he said. "They could just direct the military personnel currently there to drive to the center of Nuuk and just say, 'This is America now,' right? And that would lead to the same response as if they flew in 500 or 1,000 people."

He said the lasting consequences would be systemic rather than physical. The danger of annexation, he said, lay in "the erosion of the rule of law globally and to the perception that there are any norms protecting anybody on the planet."

"The impact is changing the map," Crosbie said. "The impact I don't think would be storming the parliament."
