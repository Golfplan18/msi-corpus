On the fifth anniversary of the Jan. 6, 2021, attack on the U.S. Capitol, political leaders across party lines marked the day while also continuing to dispute what should be remembered about it.

Democrats and Republicans each held their own events on Tuesday, reflecting deep divisions over history, accountability and Capitol security, even as the country focused on other developments. There was no official event to memorialize what happened that day, according to the report, and a plaque honoring the police who defended the Capitol has never been hung.

The differences were visible in how the day unfolded in Washington. President Donald Trump delivered a lengthy morning speech to House Republicans away from the Capitol, at a rebranded Kennedy Center carrying his own name, shifting blame for Jan. 6 onto the rioters themselves. Trump said he had intended only for his supporters to go “peacefully and patriotically” to confront Congress as it certified Democrat Joe Biden’s election victory, and he blamed the media for focusing on other parts of his speech that day.

Democrats, meanwhile, held their own morning meeting at the Capitol. Members reconvened the House committee that investigated the Jan. 6 attack for a panel discussion, with Rep. Jamie Raskin warning about the “Orwellian project of forgetting” as lawmakers sought to preserve what they said should be a shared historical record.

A third set of events also drew attention to how the anniversary is being interpreted and politicized. Enrique Tarrio, the former leader of the Proud Boys, summoned supporters for a midday march retracing the rioters’ steps from the White House to the Capitol, this time to honor Trump supporter Ashli Babbitt and those who died in the siege and its aftermath. More than 100 people gathered, including Babbitt’s mother. Tarrio told the crowd, “They should be fired and prosecuted,” before marchers arrived at the Capitol, where they were confronted along the way by counter-protesters and then sang the National Anthem.

Tarrio and other organizers said they were seeking pressure on the Trump administration to punish officials who investigated and prosecuted Jan. 6 rioters. Tarrio was sentenced to 22 years in prison for seditious conspiracy for orchestrating the attack, and the report said that more than 1,500 defendants saw their charges dropped after Trump issued a sweeping pardon on his return to the White House last year. The White House, in a glossy new report, highlighted what it said the president has already done to free those charged and turned blame on Democrats for certifying Biden’s election victory.

On the eve of the anniversary, House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said the administration wanted to lecture the world about democracy while undermining the rule of law at home, warning that Americans would be reminded of those contradictions. Jeffries and Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer held a candlelight vigil outside the Capitol with lawmakers and family members of police officers to mark the anniversary.

Among Republicans, fewer joined the remembrances. Speaker Mike Johnson, when asked about the delay in hanging the plaque honoring the police as required by law, said in a statement that the statute “is not implementable,” adding that proposed alternatives “also do not comply with the statute.”

At the morning hearing at the Capitol, lawmakers heard from witnesses and others, including former U.S. Capitol Police officer Winston Pingeon, who testified that as a kid he had always dreamed of being a cop but that on Jan. 6 he thought he was going to die on the Capitol steps. Pingeon urged the country to find common ground, saying, “I implore America to not forget what happened,” and he said he believed the vast majority of Americans have more in common than what divides them.

Pamela Hemphill, a rioter who refused Trump’s pardon, testified while apologizing to an officer sitting beside her at the witness table. Hemphill said, “I can’t allow them not be recognized, to be lied about,” about police who she said also saved her life as she fell and was trampled by the mob. She added, “Until I can see that plaque get up there, I’m not done.”

The hearing included former Rep. Adam Kinzinger, one of two Republicans on the panel that investigated Trump’s efforts to overturn Biden’s win, while the other Republican, Liz Cheney, did not appear. Speaker Emerita Nancy Pelosi urged the country to turn away from persistent lies and violence that she said sends the wrong message about democracy.

Republican Rep. Barry Loudermilk of Georgia, who Johnson tapped to lead a new committee to probe other theories about what happened on Jan. 6, rejected Tuesday’s session as a “partisan exercise” designed to hurt Trump and his allies. Loudermilk also said in a social media post that “The Capitol Complex is no more secure today than it was on January 6,” and he argued that the security failures that occurred on Jan. 6 and the partisan investigation that followed should not be repeated. The report also said Republicans have focused on alleged security lapses, including delays in National Guard arrival and failures by police canine units to discover pipe bombs found outside party headquarters.

In the Senate, Republican Sen. Thom Tillis displayed a replica plaque and said he would try later this week to push a vote ensuring it complies with the law so it can be displayed as intended. Another Republican, Sen. Tommy Tuberville, objected to a separate resolution condemning the Capitol attack.

The anniversary also brought renewed attention to the aftermath of the siege. The report said at least five people died in the Capitol siege and its aftermath, including Babbitt, who was shot and killed by police while trying to climb through a window of a door near the House chamber, and Capitol Police Officer Brian Sicknick, who died later after battling the mob. The report said several law enforcement personnel died later, some by suicide.

The criminal cases and legal developments have also remained part of the political backdrop. The Justice Department indicted Trump on four counts alleging a conspiracy to defraud voters with claims of a rigged election in the run-up to the attack. Former Justice Department special counsel Jack Smith told lawmakers last month that the riot at the Capitol “does not happen” without Trump, the report said, though Smith ended up abandoning the case once Trump was reelected president, citing department guidelines against prosecuting a sitting president.

Trump was impeached by the House on the sole charge of having incited the insurrection and acquitted by the Senate after top GOP senators said they believed the matter was best left to the courts. Ahead of the 2024 election, the Supreme Court ruled ex-presidents have broad immunity from prosecution, setting another legal context for how Jan. 6-related cases have proceeded.

Associated Press writers Will Weissert, Joey Cappelletti and Gary Fields contributed to this report.
