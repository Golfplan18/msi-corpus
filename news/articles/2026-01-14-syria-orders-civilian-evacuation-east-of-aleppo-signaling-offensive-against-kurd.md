The military buildup east of Aleppo marks a sharp escalation between Damascus and the SDF, the Kurdish-led militia that controls large swaths of northeastern Syria and has served as the primary U.S. partner in fighting the Islamic State group. Negotiations between the two sides over a power-sharing agreement have stalled, and Syria's interim president publicly accused the SDF of refusing international mediation attempts during the recent clashes.

Syria's military announced a humanitarian corridor Wednesday for civilians to evacuate towns east of Aleppo, in a move that appeared to signal plans for an offensive against Kurdish-led forces in the region.

The corridor, announced late Wednesday, would allow civilians to pass through from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Thursday, the army said. The announcement covered the towns of Deir Hafer and Maskana and surrounding areas, approximately 60 kilometers east of Aleppo city.

The military called on the Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces and other armed groups to withdraw east of the Euphrates River.

## Clashes follow last week's fighting

The announcement followed days of clashes in Aleppo city last week that killed at least 23 people, wounded dozens more, and displaced tens of thousands, according to the Associated Press. Those clashes ended with the evacuation of Kurdish fighters and government forces taking control of three contested neighborhoods.

Government troops have already sent reinforcements to the area east of Aleppo after accusing the SDF of building up its own forces there. The SDF denied the accusation and said Turkish drones carried out strikes in the area.

The government also accused the SDF of launching drone strikes in Aleppo city, including one that struck the Aleppo governorate building on Saturday, shortly after two Cabinet ministers and a local official held a news conference there.

The SDF warned in a statement of "dangerous repercussions on civilians, infrastructure, and vital facilities" in the event of further escalation and said Damascus bears "full responsibility for this escalation and all ensuing humanitarian and security repercussions in the region."

## Al-Sharaa accuses SDF of refusing mediation

Syria's interim president, Ahmad al-Sharaa, praised the "courage of the Kurds" in a televised interview aired Wednesday and said he would guarantee their rights and wants them integrated into the Syrian army. But he lashed out at the SDF, accusing the group of not abiding by an agreement under which its forces were supposed to withdraw from neighborhoods they controlled in Aleppo city.

Al-Sharaa accused the SDF of forcibly preventing civilians from leaving when the army opened a corridor during the recent clashes. He also claimed the group refused French and U.S. attempts to mediate a ceasefire because of an order from the Kurdistan Workers' Party, known as the PKK.

The interview was initially scheduled to air Tuesday on Shams TV, a broadcaster based in Irbil — the seat of northern Iraq's semi-autonomous Kurdish region. The station's manager later said the interview was canceled out of fear of further inflaming tensions because of the hard line al-Sharaa took against the SDF. Syria's state TV instead aired clips from the interview on Wednesday. There was no immediate response from the SDF to al-Sharaa's comments, the AP reported.

## U.S. calls for restraint

Adm. Brad Cooper, commander of U.S. Central Command, said in a statement Tuesday that the U.S. is "closely monitoring" the situation. He called for "all parties to exercise maximum restraint, avoid actions that could further escalate tensions, and prioritize the protection of civilians and critical infrastructure" and urged the parties to "return to the negotiating table in good faith."

The Trump administration has developed close ties with Syria's interim government under al-Sharaa and has pushed the Kurds to implement an integration deal. Washington has not publicly taken sides in the Aleppo clashes, the AP reported.

## Stalled negotiations

The fighting broke out as negotiations between Damascus and the SDF have stalled over an agreement to integrate their forces and for the central government to take control of institutions including border crossings and oil fields in northeastern Syria.

Some factions within Syria's new national army — formed after the fall of former President Bashar Assad in a rebel offensive in December 2024 — were previously Turkey-backed insurgent groups with a long history of clashing with Kurdish forces.

The SDF has for years been the main U.S. partner in Syria in fighting against the Islamic State group. Turkey, however, considers the SDF a terrorist organization because of its links to the PKK, which has waged a long-running insurgency in Turkey. A peace process between the PKK and the Turkish government is now underway.
