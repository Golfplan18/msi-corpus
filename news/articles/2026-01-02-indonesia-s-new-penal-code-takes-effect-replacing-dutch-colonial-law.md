The new code affects more than 270 million people in the world's most populous Muslim-majority nation and ends a legal framework rooted in Dutch rule that Indonesia inherited at independence in 1945. International human rights organizations condemned key provisions as threats to civil liberties, privacy, and freedom of expression.

Indonesia on Friday began enforcing its newly ratified penal code, replacing Dutch colonial criminal law that had governed the country since before independence and marking the most significant overhaul of the nation's legal system in modern history.

The 345-page Indonesian Penal Code, known by its Indonesian acronym KUHP, took effect following a three-year transition period after parliament unanimously approved it in December 2022. It criminalizes sex outside marriage, restores penalties for insulting the president and state institutions, and expands an existing blasphemy law — provisions that drew sharp condemnation from international human rights organizations.

Coordinating Minister for Law, Human Rights, Immigration, and Corrections Yusril Ihza Mahendra called the enforcement "the end of the colonial criminal law era and the beginning of a more humane, modern, and just legal system rooted in Indonesian culture."

"This is a historic moment for the Indonesian nation," Mahendra said in a statement Friday.

## Provisions under scrutiny

Under the new code, sex outside marriage is punishable by up to one year in prison, while cohabitation carries a six-month sentence. Adultery cases can proceed only following a complaint by a spouse, parents, or children — a safeguard the government says prevents arbitrary enforcement, including against tourists.

Human Rights Watch warned the morality-based provisions could lead to invasions of privacy and selective enforcement.

The code also restores a ban on insulting the president or vice president, state institutions, and the national ideology, carrying penalties of up to three years in prison for "attacking the honor or dignity" of state leaders. Cases must be reported by the president. Deputy Minister of Law Edward Hiariej said the government has issued strict guidelines distinguishing criticism from criminal insult, but rights advocates argue the provisions threaten freedom of expression.

The code expands an existing blasphemy law, maintaining prison sentences of up to five years for deviations from core teachings of Indonesia's six officially recognized religions. It preserves penalties of up to 10 years for associating with Marxist-Leninist organizations and four years for spreading communist ideology.

## Rights groups condemn the code

Amnesty International Indonesia Executive Director Usman Hamid described the KUHP as "a significant blow" to civil liberties.

"This overreaching criminal code will entrench obstacles to freedom of speech while criminalizing legitimate and peaceful dissent," Hamid said, warning it could open the door to abuse of power.

## Limited gains for LGBTQ community

Legislators dropped a proposed article that would have criminalized gay sex following opposition from civil society groups — an outcome advocates called a rare positive development for Indonesia's LGBTQ community. The move was among the few concessions to critics of the code.

## Death penalty reform and sentencing changes

The code retains the death penalty despite calls from rights groups to abolish capital punishment, but introduces a 10-year probationary period after which sentences may be commuted to life imprisonment or 20 years if a convict demonstrates good behavior.

The Institute for Criminal Justice Reform said the code expands non-custodial sentences, including community service and supervision, and gives judges greater discretion to tailor penalties. ICJR Executive Director Erasmus Napitupulu said these measures could help ease prison overcrowding and better serve victims.

Napitupulu called the probationary mechanism for death row inmates "an initial step toward eventually abolishing the death penalty in Indonesia."

The code also maintains a ban on abortion while formalizing existing exceptions for life-threatening medical conditions and pregnancies resulting from rape where the fetus is less than 12 weeks old.

## Decades of failed revision

Indonesia proclaimed independence in 1945 but continued operating under the Dutch colonial criminal framework. Efforts to revise the code stalled for decades as lawmakers debated how to balance human rights, religious norms, and local traditions in the world's most populous Muslim-majority nation.

A previously revised code was poised for passage in 2019, but then-President Joko Widodo urged lawmakers to delay a vote amid nationwide protests involving tens of thousands of people. Opponents said the legislation discriminated against minorities and that the legislative process lacked transparency.

A parliamentary task force finalized the current bill in November 2022 and lawmakers unanimously approved it the following month, in what the government called a historic step.
