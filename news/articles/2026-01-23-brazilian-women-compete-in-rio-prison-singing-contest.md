Fifteen Brazilian women held in Rio de Janeiro prisons competed in a singing contest called 'Voice of Liberty' in late January, an event designed to showcase their talents and boost confidence ahead of their release. The competition, held in a multipurpose prison hall, featured performances of gospel and Brazilian popular music, with a panel of judges including actor David Brazil and singer Maurício Mattar.

The competition was the third organized by Rio de Janeiro's state prison administration, which views cultural programs like singing contests as part of the rehabilitation process alongside traditional educational and vocational opportunities.

Fernanda Fernandes Domingues, 36, tried to compose herself as she stepped from the purple-themed stage. She had just won the first prize in "Voice of Liberty," a singing contest held inside a Rio de Janeiro women's prison in late January.

"I don't even know what to say, I'm really happy!" she said. "Music is a way of empowering myself in this sad time, because being in prison is sad."

## A Showcase of Talent

The competition brought together women from four Rio detention facilities, each having auditioned and rehearsed for months in preparation. On the day of the event, they applied makeup, donned gowns, and took the stage in a multipurpose prison hall before an audience of prison officials, volunteers, and fellow detainees who clapped and sang along as each woman performed.

The women selected songs from multiple genres. Many chose gospel pieces. Others offered interpretations of MPB — the acronym for Brazilian popular music in Portuguese — including "Black Sheep," a composition by the late singer Rita Lee. A panel of judges including actor David Brazil and singer Maurício Mattar evaluated the performances on voice, presentation, performance, and charm.

The months of preparation included more than rehearsals. Candidates underwent makeup, costume, and hair tests, adding variety and stimulation to the repetitive structure of prison life.

## Reclaiming Their Lives

Rilary Cristina Leite, 31, had performed in bars, at parties, and in churches before being arrested six years ago. She said the competition offered her a chance to pursue rehabilitation by showcasing her skills as she prepared to leave prison within a year.

"It's magical because we're doing what we want," she said. "It means liberation for us. We're in prison and art liberates us."

Leite added that her dream is to perform at the Rock in Rio festival after her release.

Cassiane Victoria Moura Martins won the competition the previous year. She has been singing since she was three years old and once performed on the radio and in public spaces. Her singing stopped after she was incarcerated for involvement in drug-trafficking, but she resumed it thanks to the contest. She plans to make videos for TikTok once released.

"When I sing, I feel at peace," she said. "It's kind of like a calming medicine. I need it. So I sing, and everything is OK."

## Culture's Role in Healing

Rio's state secretary for penitentiary administration, Maria Rosa Lo Duca Nebel, said the singing competition served the broader mission of prison administration.

"The objective of prison is also to rehabilitate through work, studies, reading and — like on Friday — through culture," she said.

She noted that cultural programs help ease tensions within detention facilities, which she described as naturally high-stress environments. "This helps calm things down, in the sense that it eases the tension, because the prison system — even though this is a women's unit — is an environment that's tense by nature. What we've provided here, with everyone's presence, creates that sense of relief," she said.
