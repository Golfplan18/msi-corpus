## Japan subsidizes Micron expansion into high-bandwidth memory production

Micron Technology broke ground last Saturday on a ¥1.5 trillion ($9.2 billion) expansion of its plant in Higashihiroshima, Hiroshima Prefecture, western Japan, according to Japanese media. The project adds roughly 28,000 square meters of cleanroom space at Micron Memory Japan's existing facility and will produce high-bandwidth memory, or HBM, used in AI servers. The Japanese government, through the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, will provide up to ¥536 billion ($3.3 billion) in subsidies for capital investment and research and development. The expansion is expected to create more than 1,000 jobs, and Micron plans to begin equipment installation in the second half of 2028, with first shipments targeted around that summer.

Former Japanese Prime Minister Fumio Kishida and Economy, Trade and Industry Minister Ryosei Akazawa attended the groundbreaking ceremony. "As AI is increasingly implemented in society, demand for high-performance semiconductors will continue to grow," Kishida said. "The efforts in Hiroshima will become even more important."

The investment is part of Tokyo's broader strategy to rebuild its domestic semiconductor base by attracting foreign leaders in segments where Japanese companies alone cannot quickly restore global competitiveness. Japan has already supported Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co.'s plants in Kumamoto and state-backed chip venture Rapidus. With Micron, the government extends that approach into the memory sector for the first time.

High-bandwidth memory is a core component of AI semiconductors, used alongside graphics processing units in large AI servers to process data quickly. South Korea's SK hynix has led the HBM market through its supply relationship with Nvidia, while Samsung Electronics has been working to close the gap with HBM3E and next-generation products. Micron is also accelerating production of HBM3E and future HBM products, combining its technology and customer network with Japan's subsidies, manufacturing base and semiconductor materials ecosystem.

The expansion reflects Japan's push to secure advanced semiconductor supply chains at home amid U.S.-China technology competition and risks surrounding the Taiwan Strait, according to Japanese media. The Tokyo government increasingly views advanced semiconductors not only as industrial products but as core infrastructure for economic security. If next-generation AI memory demand grows sharply after 2028, Hiroshima could emerge as a key base in a U.S.-linked HBM supply chain.

For South Korea, Micron's move does not immediately threaten the competitiveness of SK hynix or Samsung, but Japan's willingness to spend heavily to attract U.S. memory chip production is a development Seoul cannot ignore, the reports said. South Korean media reported that the competition for AI semiconductor production is expanding beyond graphics processors and foundries into the location of memory production.
