CARACAS, Venezuela — Nicolás Maduro, who spent four decades rising from unionized bus driver to Venezuelan president, was captured Saturday in a U.S. military operation targeting his capital, President Donald Trump announced in an early-morning social media post.

Venezuela's vice president, Delcy Rodríguez, said the whereabouts of Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, remained unknown following the operation. U.S. Attorney General Pam Bondi said both would face charges stemming from a New York indictment.

The reported capture ends the presidency of one of Latin America's most enduring authoritarian leaders, whose 13 years in power coincided with Venezuela's economic collapse, the displacement of more than 7.7 million citizens, and an ongoing International Criminal Court investigation into crimes against humanity.

## A bus driver's path to power

Maduro's political career began formally in 1986, when he traveled to Cuba for a year of ideological instruction — his only formal education after high school. He returned to work as a bus driver for the Caracas subway system, where he quickly became a union leader. Venezuelan intelligence agencies in the 1990s identified him as a leftist radical with close ties to the Cuban government, according to the Associated Press.

He joined the political movement organized by Hugo Chávez after the military officer received a presidential pardon in 1994 for leading a failed and bloody coup years earlier. Maduro rose steadily through the ranks: six years as a lawmaker, a term as president of the National Assembly, six years as foreign minister, and a brief stint as vice president.

When Chávez used his final public address before his death in 2013 to anoint Maduro as his political successor, the choice surprised both supporters and critics. Chávez's electoral capital delivered Maduro a narrow victory that year — his first six-year term — though Maduro never commanded the devotion voters had expressed for his mentor.

Maduro married Flores, his partner of nearly two decades, in July 2013, shortly after taking office. He referred to her as the "first combatant," rather than first lady, and considered her a crucial adviser.

## A presidency defined by repression

Maduro's tenure was almost immediately contested. Political opposition, including María Corina Machado — later a Nobel Peace Prize winner — organized street protests in Caracas and other cities in 2014. Security forces pushed back the demonstrations; 43 people died and dozens were arrested.

The ruling United Socialist Party of Venezuela lost control of the National Assembly for the first time in 16 years in the 2015 election. Maduro moved to neutralize the opposition-controlled legislature by establishing a pro-government Constituent Assembly in 2017. Months of violently suppressed protests followed. More than 100 people were killed and thousands were injured. Hundreds were arrested, prompting the International Criminal Court to open a crimes-against-humanity investigation against Maduro and members of his government — a proceeding that remained ongoing as of 2025.

Thousands of real or perceived government opponents were imprisoned during his presidency, and many were tortured, some at his direction, the AP reported.

In 2018, drones rigged with explosives detonated near Maduro while he delivered a speech during a nationally televised military parade. He survived.

## Economic collapse

Venezuela's economy shrank 71% between 2012 and 2020, according to the AP. Inflation topped 130,000%. Oil production — the country's primary revenue source — dropped to less than 400,000 barrels per day. Severe food and medicine shortages drove mass migration on a scale that reshaped the region; more than 7.7 million Venezuelans left the country, many traveling on foot to neighboring nations.

The first Trump administration imposed economic sanctions, freezing Venezuelan government assets in the United States and prohibiting American citizens and international partners from doing business with Venezuelan government entities, including the state oil company.

Maduro implemented a series of economic measures beginning in 2021 that eventually ended Venezuela's hyperinflation cycle. He paired those changes with negotiations with the U.S.-backed political opposition, which produced the prison release of a close ally and a sanctions license allowing oil giant Chevron to resume Venezuelan oil operations — a financial lifeline for his government.

## The 2024 election and final isolation

Negotiations led by Norwegian diplomats did not resolve the underlying political differences. In 2023, the government barred Machado from running for office. In early 2024, it intensified repressive efforts, detaining opposition leaders and forcing key members of Machado's campaign to seek asylum at a diplomatic compound in Caracas, where they remained confined for more than a year to avoid arrest.

When polls closed in the 2024 presidential election, the National Electoral Council declared Maduro the winner without providing detailed vote counts. The opposition published tally sheets it said it had collected from more than 80% of electronic voting machines; those records showed Edmundo González defeated Maduro by more than a 2-to-1 margin. World leaders rejected the official results. The National Assembly nonetheless swore Maduro in for a third term in January 2025.

Trump's return to the White House that same month proved consequential. Trump quickly pressured Maduro's government to accept regular deportation flights for the first time in years. By summer 2025, the United States had built a military presence in the Caribbean that put Venezuela's government on high alert. Saturday's operation was the culmination of that buildup.
