The back-to-back trade actions signal that China is willing to deploy economic pressure against Japan as their disagreement over Taiwan deepens — even as Beijing simultaneously courts Seoul with cooperation agreements and joint investment pledges signed during South Korean President Lee Jae Myung's four-day state visit.

BEIJING — China's Commerce Ministry launched a dumping investigation Wednesday into dichlorosilane — a chemical gas used in manufacturing semiconductors — imported from Japan, citing a 31 percent price decline between 2022 and 2024 that the ministry said had damaged Chinese producers.

The probe arrived one day after Beijing banned exports of dual-use goods with potential military applications to Japan, a rapid sequence of measures that marked a sharp deterioration in relations between Asia's two largest economies.

"The dumping of imported products from Japan has damaged the production and operation of our domestic industry," the ministry said in a statement.

## Taiwan dispute drives the measures

Beijing has signaled mounting displeasure with Tokyo since Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi said late last year that her country's military could intervene if China takes action against Taiwan, an island democracy that Beijing claims as its own territory.

Tensions rose further Tuesday when Japanese lawmaker Hei Seki — also known as Yo Kitano and previously sanctioned by China for what Beijing described as "spreading fallacies" about Taiwan — visited the island and called it an independent country.

"I came to Taiwan … to prove this point, and to tell the world that Taiwan is an independent country," Hei Seki said, according to Taiwan's Central News Agency.

Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Mao Ning dismissed the remarks. "The nasty words of a petty villain like him are not worth commenting on," she said.

Masaaki Kanai, head of Asia Oceanian Affairs at Japan's Foreign Ministry, urged China to abandon its trade restrictions, arguing that a measure targeting only Japan and departing from international practice is unacceptable. Japan had not announced retaliatory measures as of Wednesday.

## Rare earth concern hangs over talks

As the two governments feuded, speculation mounted that China might restrict exports of rare earth minerals to Japan — a tool Beijing has deployed against the United States in their broader trade dispute.

China controls most of the world's production of heavy rare earths, which are used in powerful heat-resistant magnets critical to defense equipment and electric vehicles.

China Daily, an official state newspaper seen as a government mouthpiece, quoted unidentified sources saying Beijing was considering tightening exports of certain rare earths to Japan. The Associated Press reported that account could not be independently confirmed, and the Commerce Ministry made no mention of rare earth restrictions in its Wednesday statement.

## Seoul visit offers a deliberate contrast

On the same day China escalated against Japan, South Korean President Lee Jae Myung concluded a four-day visit to Beijing — his first since taking office in June — that produced a markedly different tone.

Lee and Chinese President Xi Jinping oversaw the signing of cooperation agreements covering technology, trade, transportation, and environmental protection. South Korean and Chinese companies signed 24 export contracts worth a combined $44 million, according to South Korea's Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources.

During Lee's visit, Chinese media reported that South Korea had overtaken Japan as the leading destination for outbound flights from China's mainland over the New Year's holiday. China has been discouraging its citizens from traveling to Japan, saying that Japanese leaders' comments on Taiwan have created, in Beijing's words, "significant risks to the personal safety and lives of Chinese citizens in Japan."
