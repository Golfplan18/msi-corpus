HELSINKI — For decades, Finland has required children as young as 3 to study media literacy as a civic defense against propaganda and false information. Educators are now expanding the program to cover artificial intelligence, as Finnish officials say disinformation from neighboring Russia has grown more sophisticated since Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

Finland, which shares a 1,340-kilometer (830-mile) border with Russia and joined NATO in 2023, has woven media literacy into its national curriculum since the 1990s and regularly ranks at the top of the European Media Literacy Index. Officials say the rapid advancement of AI tools — capable of generating convincing fake images and video — has made the effort more urgent than ever.

"We think that having good media literacy skills is a very big civic skill," Kiia Hakkala, a pedagogical specialist for the City of Helsinki, told the Associated Press. "It's very important to the nation's safety and to the safety of our democracy."

## Teaching children to spot fakes

At Tapanila Primary School, in a quiet neighborhood north of Helsinki, teacher Ville Vanhanen recently asked fourth graders to compile a list of criteria for assessing whether online news sources are trustworthy. His students have been evaluating headlines and short texts for years. Now they are moving into new territory.

"We've been studying how to recognize if a picture or a video is made by AI," said Vanhanen, who also serves as vice principal at the school.

Ten-year-old student Ilo Lindgren, evaluating a "Fact or Fiction?" prompt on a classroom TV screen, acknowledged the difficulty: "It is a little bit hard."

Finnish media have reinforced the effort. The country holds an annual "Newspaper Week," during which papers and other news are sent to young people. In 2024, the Helsinki-based daily Helsingin Sanomat collaborated on a new "ABC Book of Media Literacy," distributed to every 15-year-old in Finland as they began upper secondary school.

"It's really important for us to be seen as a place where you can get information that's been verified, that you can trust, and that's done by people you know in a transparent way," Jussi Pullinen, the paper's managing editor, said.

Additional media literacy courses are also available for older adults, who educators say may be especially vulnerable to misinformation.

## A program shaped by proximity to Russia

Finland's media literacy skills are so ingrained into national culture that the Nordic nation of 5.6 million regularly ranks at the top of the European Media Literacy Index, compiled by the Open Society Institute in Sofia, Bulgaria, between 2017 and 2023.

According to the Associated Press, Russia stepped up its disinformation operations across Europe after launching its full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Russia has repeatedly denied that it interferes in the internal affairs of other countries. Finland's 2023 accession to NATO has intensified the country's concern about cross-border information threats.

Finnish Education Minister Anders Adlercreutz said the scale of today's disinformation environment exceeded what educators originally anticipated. "I don't think we envisioned that the world would look like this," he said. "That we would be bombarded with disinformation, that our institutions are challenged — our democracy really challenged — through disinformation."

## A narrowing window for detection

Martha Turnbull, director of hybrid influence at the Helsinki-based European Centre of Excellence for Countering Hybrid Threats, said the information environment is already more difficult to navigate than it once was.

"It already is much harder in the information space to spot what's real and what's not real," she said.

Turnbull said the current window of relative detectability may be closing. "It just so happens that right now, it's reasonably easy to spot the AI-generated fakes because the quality of them isn't as good as it could be," she said. "But as that technology develops, and particularly as we move toward things like agentic AI, I think that's when it could become much more difficult for us to spot."
