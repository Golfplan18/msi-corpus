One week after the nation marked the fifth anniversary of the Jan. 6, 2021, Capitol attack, the House Republican majority used a new Select Committee on the Jan. 6 assault to revisit what happened that day. The committee held its first hearing Wednesday, framed by Republicans as an examination of the FBI’s investigation into pipe bombs found outside Democratic and Republican party headquarters during the attack.

The hearing, which lasted about two hours, also turned quickly into a fight over competing explanations for the violence. Republicans pursued alternative theories about why Trump’s supporters engaged in mob violence at the Capitol, where an attack was watched around the world. Militant Oath Keepers founder Stewart Rhodes sat in the front row.

Rep. Barry Loudermilk, R-Ga., acknowledged rising tension during the hearing and said his “objective is to get to the truth.” The top Democrat on the panel, Rep. Jamie Raskin of Maryland, responded that the committee would not accept what he described as an effort to rewrite history. “The truth is a resilient thing,” Raskin said, adding, “We’re not going to put up with a pack of lies in this subcommittee and a bunch of conspiracy theories.”

The new committee is the second time House Republicans, since they took control, have created a panel to probe Jan. 6 while disputing the findings of an earlier investigation. That original panel, established by then-Speaker Nancy Pelosi after the riot, released its findings in 2022. The findings put the blame on Trump for having incited the violence at the Capitol.

Trump was impeached on the charge of inciting the insurrection but was acquitted by the Senate, and the article says the Justice Department’s four-count indictment against Trump brought by special counsel Jack Smith was abandoned after Trump won reelection in 2024, citing department protocol against prosecuting a sitting president. Five years after the assault, the hearing reflected how the national trauma still engulfs Congress as Americans continue to grapple with the riot, which the sources describe as occurring after defeated President Trump told supporters to “fight like hell” as Congress certified the 2020 election results.

Republicans also challenged how the attack has been described in testimony and evidence. Rep. Troy Nehls of Texas, who was among those seen at barricaded doors to the House chamber, called the original Jan. 6 committee a “total sham.” He disputed eyewitness testimony from police officers who had described being bloodied and injured, saying the accounts were “highly scripted” and from “Trump haters.” Nehls added that the prior panel “put on a pretty good show.”

The hearing examined the pipe bomb case alongside the broader dispute over narratives. The FBI arrested Brian Cole Jr. of Virginia last month on suspicion of placing pipe bombs. Republicans had long believed the pipe bombs were part of a potential inside job—described as a distraction that diverted law enforcement that day while Trump’s supporters marched to the Capitol to confront Congress.

Rep. Morgan Griffith, R-Va., said he had been “concerned with this whole pipe bomb situation for some time.” Rep. Harriet Hageman, R-Wyo., also probed why investigators overlooked various leads, and lawmakers questioned why bomb-sniffing dogs failed to discover the device outside one of the party headquarters.

Testifying to the panel was John Nantz, described as a former FBI special agent. He said the Biden-era FBI under then-Director Christopher Wray was focused elsewhere, and testified that “Every resource should have been made available.” Hageman responded with a quip that the criticism was the “understatement of the century.”

The hearing also touched on Trump’s pardons of people convicted in the siege. Raskin discussed how many of those convicted had later committed other crimes, and Mike Romano, described as a former Justice Department prosecutor, said he viewed Trump’s pardons of rioters as “ill-advised.” Romano said many of them remained proud of their conduct, adding, “They have celebrated the pardons and tried to lie about what happened.” Pressed by Raskin on whether the pardon would apply to Cole if convicted, Romano testified, “I don’t know that there’s a clear yes or no,” and that “And I think that’s a problem.”

As tensions remained visible, Rep. Jasmine Crockett, D-Texas, said she was “so frustrated with this country” and that she was not alone, adding that “The conspiracy theories” were driving her “insane.”
