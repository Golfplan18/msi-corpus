The survey, based on early 2024 data from Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico and Peru—nations representing about 75% of Latin America's population—documents the continuation of a decades-long shift away from the institutional Catholic Church in a region where the faith had dominated for centuries and where two recent popes, Pope Leo XIV and Pope Francis, built significant portions of their ecclesiastical careers before their papal elections.

Catholicism in six of Latin America's largest countries has significantly declined over the past decade, while growing shares of adults identify as religiously unaffiliated, according to a Pew Research Center survey released Wednesday. The shifts mark a major transformation in a region long considered a global Catholic stronghold, even as most Latin Americans continue to express religious belief and engage in religious practice.

The Pew survey found that Catholics make up 46% to 67% of the adult population in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico and Peru. In each nation, the Catholic share declined by at least 9 percentage points over the past decade. Meanwhile, the share of adults who identify as religiously unaffiliated grew by 7 percentage points or more across the region.

"Our analysis found that the Catholic share of the population in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico and Peru — which collectively make up about 75% of Latin America's population — has significantly declined since 2013-14, while a growing share of adults in the region are religiously unaffiliated," said Kirsten Lesage, a Pew research associate and lead author of the report.

## The Scale of the Shift

A decade ago, all six countries had Catholic majorities, with about 6 in 10 or more adults in each identifying as Catholic. Today, about half of Brazilians and Chileans identify as Catholic. About two-thirds of Mexicans and Peruvians are Catholic, as are about 6 in 10 adults in Argentina and Colombia — but all of those majorities are smaller than 10 years ago.

The religiously unaffiliated now outnumber Protestants in four of the six countries. In Argentina, Chile, Colombia and Mexico, more adults identify as unaffiliated than with any branch of Protestantism. In Mexico, about 2 in 10 adults identify as atheist, agnostic or "nothing in particular," compared with roughly 1 in 10 who identify with any Protestant denomination.

## Reasons for the Shift

The Catholic Church's influence in the region has eroded following high-profile clergy sex abuse scandals and the church's opposition to abortion and LGBTQ+ rights. Some Latin Americans now seek spiritual answers outside organized religion—in yoga, tarot, astrology and other non-religious belief systems. The share of religiously unaffiliated adults increased significantly across the region, rising as high as about one-quarter of adults in Argentina and Colombia.

Protestantism, by contrast, has remained relatively stable across the region over the same period. In Brazil, which has the largest percentage of Protestants among the six countries, 29% of adults now identify as Protestant, compared with 26% a decade ago.

## Religious Belief Persists Despite Affiliation Decline

The data reveals an apparent paradox: while formal religious affiliations have shifted dramatically, Latin Americans remain substantially more religious than populations in many other regions. About 9 in 10 or more adults surveyed in each country say they believe in God. About half or more of adults in Brazil, Colombia, Mexico and Peru say religion is "very important" in their lives. Majorities in Brazil, Colombia and Peru report praying at least once a day.

"What's striking is that, despite these shifts in religious identity, Latin Americans remain quite religious, on average," Lesage said.

The persistence of religious belief extends even to those without formal religious affiliation. Most religiously unaffiliated adults across the region say they believe in God. Latin Americans are about as likely to believe in God now as they were a decade ago.

Latin Americans remain more religious on average than adults in many other countries Pew has surveyed recently, especially in Europe, where many have left Christianity since childhood.

## Methodology

The survey included 6,234 Latin American adults from January 22 through April 27, 2024. The margin of error ranges from plus or minus 4.0 percentage points to 4.5 percentage points, depending on the country. The six countries surveyed collectively represent approximately 75% of Latin America's population.
