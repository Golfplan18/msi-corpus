Bulgaria became the 21st member of the eurozone on New Year's Day, retiring its national currency, the lev, and adopting the euro at a fixed conversion rate of approximately 1.96 levs per euro. The accession, which the Bulgarian government had pursued since the country joined the European Union in 2007, gives the country's 6.4 million people access to the continent-wide currency used across the EU's single-currency bloc. Bank accounts were converted automatically, and while Bulgarians may continue paying in levs for about a month, they will receive their change in euros.

Old lev notes and coins can be exchanged for no fee at banks, post offices, and the Bulgarian Central Bank through June 30. The central bank will accept old currency indefinitely.

Bulgaria's euro membership is expected to reduce cross-border trade costs and deepen economic integration with Western Europe, though it arrives against a backdrop of persistent public skepticism rooted in inflation fears, long-standing distrust of institutions, and what analysts say is an active Russian disinformation effort targeting EU member states.

## What changes for businesses and travelers

Companies trading with the rest of the eurozone will no longer bear currency exchange costs, estimated at 1 billion levs per year in savings, according to the Bulgarian National Bank. Travelers will be able to use euros in neighboring Greece and other eurozone countries without exchanging currency.

Bulgaria also gains a seat on the European Central Bank's governing council, giving the country a voice in decisions on interest rates and monetary policy across the eurozone. Interest rates are set by the ECB in Frankfurt. Bulgaria had already ceded that aspect of economic sovereignty years ago, when it fixed the lev's exchange rate to the euro.

Countries that join the eurozone give up the ability to devalue their currency to gain trade advantage. For Bulgaria, analysts said, that loss is largely theoretical given the long-standing peg.

## Widespread public skepticism

The changeover is taking place against a background of public opposition that polls have documented for months. A Eurobarometer survey conducted in March, which sampled 1,017 people with a margin of error of about plus or minus 3.1 percentage points, showed 53% of respondents opposed joining the eurozone while 45% were in favor. A separate Eurobarometer poll taken between October 9 and November 3 similarly showed about half of Bulgarians opposed the single currency, with 42% in favor.

Dimitar Keranov, program coordinator for engaging Central Europe at the German Marshall Fund in Berlin, said the resistance reflects structural concerns rather than opposition to European integration. Public anxiety is "more about economic anxiety and low institutional trust overall, not ideological concerns against the euro or the European integration of Bulgaria," Keranov said.

Bulgaria ranks as the second most corrupt country in the EU after Hungary, according to Transparency International. Average wages stand at 1,300 euros ($1,530) per month, near the bottom of EU income levels. Disinformation spread on social media and attributed to Russian efforts to sow dissension among EU countries has also played a role in shaping public opinion, Keranov said.

## What officials say to expect

Fears that merchants will round prices upward during the changeover are not without basis, though European officials say the effect in previous adoptions has been modest and brief. European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde said that in earlier euro changeovers, the inflation impact was 0.2 to 0.4 percentage points and quickly faded.

"Before adoption, uncertainty is natural," Lagarde said. "But once households and firms begin using the new currency in their daily lives — and see that a credible central bank is safeguarding price stability — confidence grows."

Some price increases visible in the changeover period may reflect increases already in the works, economists said, as restaurants and other businesses that held off revising menus and price lists ahead of the switch begin doing so with the transition.

ECB economists Ferdinand Dreher and Nils Hernborg found that after adoption, public opinion shifts in favor of the euro by an average of 11 percentage points.
