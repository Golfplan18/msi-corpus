The dispute underscores how Trump's tariff threats are reshaping trade relationships between long-standing allies, even as Carney has emerged as a vocal critic of the American president's approach to international relations.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney said Sunday his country has no intention of pursuing a free trade agreement with China, directly contradicting U.S. President Donald Trump's threats of 100% tariffs on Canadian goods. Carney's statement came after he had recently reduced Canada's tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles—a move Trump claimed was part of China's attempt to "take over" Canada.

"We have no intention of doing that with China or any other nonmarket economy," Carney said. "What we have done with China is to rectify some issues that developed in the last couple of years."

Under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, signed after the Trump administration's first term, Canada committed not to pursue free trade agreements with nonmarket economies without prior notification. Carney's statement affirms Canada's compliance with that obligation, even as Trump disputed the characterization of Carney's recent deal-making with Beijing.

## Carney's Tariff Reversal

In January, Carney cut Canada's blanket 100% tariff on Chinese electric vehicles to 6.1%, with an initial cap of 49,000 vehicles that is expected to grow to about 70,000 over five years. The move followed the 2024 trade war escalation, when Canada mirrored U.S. tariffs by imposing a 100% duty on Chinese EVs and 25% tariffs on Chinese steel and aluminum. China had responded by imposing its own retaliatory tariffs on Canadian products, including 100% on canola oil and 25% on pork and seafood.

## Trump's Online Response

Trump posted on social media that "China is successfully and completely taking over the once Great Country of Canada. So sad to see it happen. I only hope they leave Ice Hockey alone!"

In subsequent posts, Trump escalated his criticism: "A MUST WATCH. Canada is systematically destroying itself. The China deal is a disaster for them. Will go down as one of the worst deals, of any kind, in history. All their businesses are moving to the USA. I want to see Canada SURVIVE AND THRIVE!"

Trump also warned that if Carney "thinks he is going to make Canada a 'Drop Off Port' for China to send goods and products into the United States, he is sorely mistaken."

## U.S. Administration Follows With Concerns

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent echoed the president's concern. "We can't let Canada become an opening that the Chinese pour their cheap goods into the U.S.," Bessent said on ABC's "This Week." He suggested Carney's motives were political, saying the prime minister was "trying to virtue-signal to his globalist friends at Davos."

The tariff dispute reflects broader tensions between Trump and Carney over foreign policy. At the World Economic Forum in Davos this month, Carney positioned himself as a leader of resistance to Trump's approach, telling assembled international leaders that "middle powers must act together because if you are not at the table, you are on the menu." The comment drew widespread praise and appeared to overshadow Trump's own appearance at the forum.

Those tensions extend to Trump's threats over Greenland and his repeated suggestions that Canada be absorbed into the United States. Carney has emerged as a leading voice among allies seeking to counter Trump's positions, and the tariff dispute represents the most direct economic test of that alignment.
