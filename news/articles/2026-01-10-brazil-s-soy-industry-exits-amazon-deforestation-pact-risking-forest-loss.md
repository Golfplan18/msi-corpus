Brazil's largest soy trading association announced Monday it was withdrawing from the Amazon Soy Moratorium, a nearly 20-year voluntary agreement barring purchases of soy grown on newly deforested Amazonian land. The Brazilian Association of Vegetable Oil Industries, known as ABIOVE — whose members include Cargill, Bunge, Cofco International, Amaggi, and JBS — said it had begun the withdrawal process. Brazil's Environment Ministry said the departure effectively ended the agreement.

The collapse of the moratorium, launched in 2006 under pressure from environmental groups and international buyers, threatens President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's pledge to halt deforestation by 2030. A preliminary estimate from the nonprofit Amazon Environmental Research Institute warned that ending the pact could raise deforestation in the Amazon by up to 30% by 2045.

## Government declares the agreement over

André Lima, the Brazilian government's secretary for deforestation control and land-use planning, told the Associated Press that ABIOVE's exit left the moratorium without a viable foundation.

"If they withdrew from the pact, then a marriage with only one husband or only one wife is no longer a marriage, right?" Lima said. "I understand that, as of now, the moratorium has come to an end."

Environmentalists agreed. Greenpeace, which had supported the moratorium, said its collapse could fuel unchecked soy expansion in the Amazon, driving up deforestation and Brazil's greenhouse gas emissions.

## State tax law triggered the withdrawal

The immediate cause of ABIOVE's exit was a tax change in Mato Grosso, Brazil's top soy-producing state. Legislation enacted there ended tax incentives for companies participating in the moratorium as of January 1. Lucas Beber, president of the Mato Grosso Soy Producers Association, told the AP that participating companies received approximately 4 billion reais ($743.5 million) per year in such incentives, according to state estimates.

Soy producers have long argued the moratorium was more restrictive than Brazilian environmental law. Under national rules, Amazon farmers must preserve 80% of their land and may legally clear the remaining 20%. The moratorium banned any clearing, even when Brazilian law permitted it. Beber said the pact was designed to meet European market demands, which now account for less than 14% of Brazil's soy exports.

## A two-decade conservation record

The moratorium was launched in 2006 after environmental groups and international buyers pressed major soy traders to stop purchasing crops grown on cleared Amazonian forest. Brazil's government later endorsed the commitment, which barred purchases from farms that had cleared Amazon land after July 2008. The agreement was renewed in 2016 without an end date.

The system relied on satellite monitoring and government farm registry data, with independent auditors reviewing trader supplier lists annually. Traders were required to cut off purchases from farms found in violation.

The results, over nearly two decades, were substantial. Deforestation in municipalities tracked by the moratorium fell 69% between 2009 and 2022, according to Imaflora, a Brazilian agriculture and conservation group. During the same period, soybean crops in the Amazon expanded 344%, with Imaflora attributing the increase largely to converting land previously used for cattle ranching.

Brazil is the world's largest soybean producer, accounting for roughly 40% of global output. The country produced 171.5 million metric tons in the 2024–25 season, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture.

## Environmental risks ahead

Ana Paula Valdiones, from the Mato Grosso-based policy group Observa-MT, said the state law represented a significant environmental setback.

"The weakening or end of the soy moratorium could lead to increased deforestation, whether through the direct conversion of remaining native vegetation, the expansion of the agricultural frontier or land speculation," she said.

Lima disputed the Amazon Environmental Research Institute's projection that deforestation could rise 30% by 2045, saying he was unfamiliar with the study's underlying assumptions, including whether it accounts for individual company commitments or foreign laws restricting market access for products linked to deforestation.

He said soy market growth would likely increase legal deforestation — which now accounts for about 10% to 15% of total Amazon forest loss — but that overall deforestation could continue to decline if the government maintained its enforcement efforts. He added that companies remain legally barred from buying soy grown on illegally deforested land and face penalties if they do.

In its statement, ABIOVE said individual member companies would meet the demands of global markets on their own and rely on Brazilian authorities to implement a regulatory framework ensuring Brazil's international environmental commitments are upheld.
