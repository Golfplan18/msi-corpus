The Texas American Federation of Teachers filed a federal lawsuit Tuesday against the Texas Education Agency and its commissioner, alleging the state launched an unconstitutional campaign of retaliation against public school employees who posted social media comments about conservative activist Charlie Kirk following his killing in September.

The suit, filed in U.S. District Court in Austin, accuses Commissioner Mike Morath of directing school districts to document educators' social media posts that the agency described as "vile content" — actions the union argues violated teachers' First Amendment rights.

The Texas Education Agency said Tuesday that it could not comment "on outstanding legal matters" but confirmed that 95 investigations remain open. The agency had received more than 350 complaints about individual educators.

The union, Texas AFT, which represents about 66,000 teachers and other school employees, cited four unnamed teachers in the suit — one in the Houston area and three in the San Antonio area — investigated over posts critical of Kirk or of the reaction to his death. According to the lawsuit, the Houston-area teacher was fired. The three San Antonio-area teachers remain under investigation.

The teachers were kept anonymous because of concerns about their safety, said Zeph Capo, president of Texas AFT, at a news conference in Austin.

"It was in fact a witch hunt," Capo said.

The union's national president, Randi Weingarten, argued the case involved clear private speech.

"We're talking about schoolteachers when they were not in classrooms — in private, on their own social media, commenting on a matter that everyone in the country and the world saw," she said.

The lawsuit challenges a September 12 letter Morath sent to school superintendents stating that social media posts could violate the state educator code of ethics and that "each instance will be thoroughly investigated." The union argues the directive was too broad and too vague to be enforced without suppressing protected speech.

The U.S. Supreme Court has ruled that agencies may restrict public employees' speech when it involves official duties or could disrupt the workplace. Weingarten said neither condition applied to the teachers in the Texas cases, adding that the posts were made outside the classroom and did not celebrate or promote violence.

Capo alleged the state applied its standards selectively, noting the agency had issued no similar directives following mass shootings or the deaths of other public figures, including the killing of actor-director Rob Reiner.

Kirk's killing prompted a wider backlash: universities, sports teams and media companies fired employees who had posted criticism of Kirk after his death, and Florida's education commissioner promised to investigate teachers who made objectionable comments.

The lawsuit was filed less than a month after Abbott and Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick, both Republicans, announced a partnership with Turning Point USA to create chapters on every high school campus in the state. Kirk founded the organization in 2012 and built it into one of the country's largest conservative political groups before he was shot during an appearance at a university in Utah.
