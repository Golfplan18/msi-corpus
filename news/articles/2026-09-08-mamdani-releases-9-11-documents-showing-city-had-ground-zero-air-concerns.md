## Related illnesses have claimed twice as many lives as the 9/11 attacks

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani on Tuesday released previously unseen documents showing city officials had concerns about toxic air at Ground Zero in the weeks after the September 11, 2001 attacks, while publicly assuring residents the environment was safe.

"People got sick because the leaders they trusted lied and told them they were safe to breathe in toxic air," Mamdani said at a news conference, surrounded by survivors.

"Administration after administration has ignored those calling for answers and clarity," he added, standing next to television host and 9/11 survivor advocate Jon Stewart. "We will do no such thing."

The release resolves a lawsuit brought by advocacy group 9/11 Health Watch, which sued the city for the documents. City officials found 68 boxes of the documents last year. Officials had previously said they did not have them.

Mamdani said he planned to release hundreds of thousands of pages of air-quality reports through an online portal, timed with the 25th anniversary of the attacks. He said City Hall will spend more than $34m (£25m) on the effort.

The mayor said the documents show what the city "knew about the toxic air around Ground Zero, when they knew it, and how they acted to limit liability." He said the documents show asbestos was found "nearly everywhere" they tested. More documents will be released over the course of the next year, according to Mamdani.

Speaking at the news conference on Tuesday, Stewart said the documents show the city didn't recognize that the air that everyone was breathing in downtown Manhattan was toxic until October of 2001. "So to be fair to them, they only buried that information for 25 years," he said.

The documents include an October 2001 file known as the "Harding Memo," in which an aide warned former Deputy Mayor Robert Harding — serving under Mayor Rudy Giuliani — that the city could face as many as 35,000 legal claims for its response to the attacks.

The memo states the city could be sued because health advisories caused individuals "to return to the area too soon (causing toxic exposure or emotional harm)."

Days after the attacks, city and federal officials — including the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) — declared the air near the Twin Towers was safe, though research suggests people were breathing in toxic chemicals. Christine Todd Whitman, the head of the EPA at the time, later apologised for that declaration, saying the agency "did the very best we could at the time with the knowledge we had."

Illnesses related to the dust and smoke from the collapse of the Twin Towers have claimed twice as many lives than the attacks themselves.

Elizabeth Cascio, a former New York Fire Department (FDNY) emergency medical technician, previously told the BBC that she suffered sinus issues and headaches after responding to the attacks. "We all knew the air quality was not safe – it was very toxic in terms of how it felt," she said. "Initially, when I came off the bus and arrived at the Trade Center, you felt like you had to hold your breath. But there's only so long you can hold your breath. I could feel the particles coming into my nose and mouth and thought: 'This can't be good.'"
