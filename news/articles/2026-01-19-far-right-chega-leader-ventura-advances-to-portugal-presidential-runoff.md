The result marks another milestone in the European far right's rise toward political power, extending Chega's trajectory from a party founded less than seven years ago to a contender for Portugal's presidency — in a race shaped by immigration backlash, a housing crisis, and the country's worst period of political instability in decades.

LISBON — André Ventura, leader of Portugal's far-right populist Chega party, finished second in the country's presidential election Sunday with about 24 percent of the vote, setting up a February 8 runoff against center-left Socialist António José Seguro, who led the record 11-candidate field with nearly 31 percent, according to results with almost 98 percent of ballots counted.

No candidate reached the more than 50 percent threshold required for a first-round victory.

Ventura's second-place finish marked another step in a broader European shift toward far-right populism. Chega — whose name translates as "Enough" — became the second-largest party in Portugal's Parliament last year, just six years after Ventura founded it. Nationalist parties with similar platforms have gained power or moved close to it in France, Germany, Italy, and neighboring Spain in recent years, the Associated Press reported.

## Immigration, housing, and the campaign's fault line

Immigration has been the centerpiece of Ventura's campaign. He placed billboards across Portugal reading "this is not Bangladesh" and arguing that immigrants should not be allowed to receive welfare benefits, according to the AP. "Portugal is ours," Ventura has said.

Voters appeared more focused on the housing crisis and cost of living than on immigration, the AP reported.

## The presidency: limited but not without leverage

Portugal's head of state is largely a ceremonial role. The president customarily stays above the political fray, mediating disputes and defusing tensions, and holds no executive power.

The office does carry real tools. The president can veto legislation passed by Parliament, though Parliament can override that veto. The president also holds the power to dissolve Parliament and call early elections — what Portuguese political jargon calls the "atomic bomb," according to the AP.

The winner will replace Marcelo Rebelo de Sousa, who has served the constitutional limit of two five-year terms. Whoever wins the February 8 runoff will serve a five-year term at the Pink Palace, the presidential residence alongside the river in Lisbon.

## A country seeking stability

Portugal held its third general election in three years in May 2025, a stretch described as the country's worst period of political instability in decades, according to the AP. Stabilizing that environment ranks among the central challenges facing the next president.

Among the items likely to reach the new president's desk: a law permitting euthanasia and physician-assisted suicide that Parliament passed in 2022 but that has been stalled by constitutional objections, the AP reported.

The election drew a record 11 candidates. Only one woman was among them. Portugal has never had a woman or a non-white person serve as head of state, the AP reported.
