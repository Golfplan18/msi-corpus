The cut targets a longstanding disparity between Colombian legislators — who earned roughly 32 times the nation's minimum wage before the decree — and most Colombians who earn about $500 a month or less, and comes as Petro's government seeks $4 billion in additional revenue with a contested election cycle opening in March.

Colombian President Gustavo Petro issued a decree Tuesday cutting congressional wages by approximately 30%, eliminating a salary supplement lawmakers had received for more than a decade as the country faces a budget shortfall ahead of elections this year.

The decree removes what the government calls the "bonus for special services," a payment introduced over a decade ago to help cover relocation costs for legislators. Without it, monthly pay for members of Congress will fall from roughly $13,000 to about $9,400 once a new Congress is sworn in this July. Most Colombian workers earn about $500 a month or less.

Colombia's government said in the decree that wages currently received by legislators are "disproportionate in relation to the average income of the (nation's) population and the country's economic reality."

"Austerity measures are necessary to the extent that they don't affect the fundamental rights of citizens," the decree stated.

The cut comes as Colombia prepares for legislative elections in March and presidential elections in May. Public spending under Petro, Colombia's first left-wing president, has grown to levels exceeding spending during the COVID-19 pandemic. Colombia's national government carried a budget of approximately $134 billion in 2025, according to the Associated Press, and the government said Tuesday it is seeking to add $4 billion to this year's budget to cover payments to health insurance companies, fund fuel subsidies, and invest about $700 million in infrastructure to help the military counter drone attacks from rebel groups.

Colombia also recently issued an economic emergency decree that gives Petro authority to raise taxes without congressional approval.

The wage gap between legislators and ordinary Colombians had drawn public criticism for years. Before Tuesday's decree, legislators earned roughly 32 times the nation's minimum wage. Some members of Congress had proposed reducing their own pay in recent years, but those initiatives failed repeatedly after being blocked by legislators who argued high wages were needed, in part to fund future political campaigns.

Senator Angélica Lozano praised the decree on X as "a minimal measure of equity." Senate President Lidio García criticized the action, saying Petro was trying to "punish" legislators who had not approved his social and economic reforms, including a tax bill Congress rejected in December.

García also noted that Petro himself drew the bonus for nearly two decades in Congress. "While he was a congressman, for almost 20 years, Gustavo Petro received the special services bonus, without complaining about it," García wrote on X.

The reduced wages take effect in July, once a newly elected Congress is seated.
