The delivery illustrated Argentina's break from decades of protectionist economic policy under President Javier Milei, underscored China's accelerating push into Latin American auto markets, and highlighted the divergence between Milei's free-trade agenda and the tariff-heavy approach of his ideological ally, U.S. President Donald Trump.

A Chinese cargo ship carrying more than 5,800 electric and hybrid vehicles arrived Monday at Puerto de Zárate, a river port in Buenos Aires province, in what observers and industry officials described as the first large-scale delivery of Chinese-made electric vehicles to Argentina.

The BYD Changzhou completed a 23-day voyage from Singapore to unload the vehicles — manufactured by Chinese automaker BYD — under a new Argentine government policy that allows up to 50,000 electric and hybrid vehicles priced under $16,000 to enter the country duty-free this year. The shipment arrived under terms that eliminated what had been a 35% import tariff on automobiles.

"This milestone reflects a long-term vision in Argentina: investing, constantly expanding our dealership network throughout the national territory," said Stephen Deng, BYD's manager for Argentina.

## Milei's trade opening reverses decades of protectionism

The delivery was a direct consequence of the sweeping trade liberalization President Javier Milei has pursued since taking office in late 2023. Under the Peronist governments that preceded him, Argentina maintained some of the region's highest import barriers, which restricted consumer choices and limited foreign competition. Milei has dismantled much of that framework — reducing customs bureaucracy, lowering import barriers, and strengthening the peso to make foreign goods more affordable.

Argentina recorded a 30% increase in imports last year compared to the prior year, according to the Associated Press. Chinese goods have been among the principal beneficiaries: Chinese imports to Argentina rose more than 57% in 2025 compared to the year before, far outpacing the 9.6% increase in U.S. shipments over the same period. Chinese investment has also flowed into Argentina's energy and mining sectors.

"During many years they had the vision that everything must be manufactured in Argentina," said Claudio Damiano, a professor at the Institute of Transport of the National University of San Martín. "It's the symbol, it's what this gesture means — it's a first move by BYD, and everyone wants to know how far it can go."

## Milei invokes Trump slogan at Davos as trade paths diverge

On Wednesday, as BYD completed its unloading, Milei told business and political leaders at the World Economic Forum in Davos that his deregulation measures allow Argentina to have a more dynamically efficient economy. "This is MAGA, 'Make Argentina Great Again,'" Milei said, invoking President Donald Trump's campaign slogan.

The declaration underscored the ideological affinity between the two leaders, who share opposition to multilateral institutions, skepticism toward progressive policies, and a commitment to rolling back state intervention. That alliance yielded concrete support last year when the United States offered Argentina a $20 billion credit exchange line, according to the AP, which bolstered Milei's position ahead of crucial midterm elections.

But the two leaders' approaches to trade diverge sharply. While Trump has demanded tariff concessions from trading partners and threatened allies with economic consequences for non-compliance, Milei has moved in the opposite direction — eliminating barriers and welcoming imports, including from China, a country Washington has sought to constrain commercially.

"When you do the math on the economics, there's no possibility of bringing vehicles from Europe that can compete with the Chinese," Damiano said.

## EU-Mercosur deal faces new delay

The arrival of the Chinese vehicles at Puerto de Zárate provided a pointed backdrop to events in Brussels on the same day, where European Union legislators voted to delay ratification of a landmark free trade agreement with Mercosur, the South American trade bloc that includes Argentina. That agreement had promised to reduce barriers on European electric vehicle imports — a market entry path that now faces a more complicated competitive environment given China's early position in Argentina.

Andrés Civetta, an economist specializing in the auto sector at the Argentine consultancy Abeceb, said China was best positioned to benefit from Milei's zero-tariff quota because the policy's $16,000 price ceiling aligns precisely with the segment where Chinese automakers concentrate their production.

"The Chinese are those who have developed more of this type of technology and have more supply of vehicles at a lower price that can take advantage of this limit the government has put in place," Civetta said. "And I believe China won the race for electromobility."

## Infrastructure and service gaps limit near-term disruption

Some Western automakers operating in Argentina have expressed concern about the new competitive dynamics, and opposition legislators have warned of unfair competition. But Pablo Naya, founder of Sero Electric, Argentina's only domestic electric vehicle manufacturer, said the immediate threat to local producers was limited by structural factors.

Argentina's aging electrical grid is not equipped to handle a mass influx of electric vehicles, Naya said. Dealership service centers capable of performing internal repairs on Chinese-brand EVs do not yet exist in Argentina. Naya spoke from Sero Electric's factory in the Buenos Aires suburb of Castelar.

"It really doesn't worry us," Naya said.

He added a longer-term caveat: if Argentine infrastructure and consumer demand eventually catch up to China's capacity, the calculation would change.

"They could complicate things for us a bit," Naya said. "Then we would really have a problem."
