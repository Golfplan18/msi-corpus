BRUSSELS — European Union officials are weighing retaliatory tariffs and the bloc's Anti-Coercion Instrument after U.S. President Donald Trump threatened to impose 10% import duties starting in February on goods from eight European nations, citing their opposition to American control of Greenland.

Trump said the rate would climb to 25% on June 1 unless a deal was reached for what he called "the Complete and Total purchase of Greenland" by the United States, according to a social media post he made Saturday from his golf club in West Palm Beach, Florida.

European leaders from Dublin to Helsinki condemned the announcement as economic coercion and convened an emergency meeting in Brussels on Sunday. EU leaders are scheduled to gather again Thursday in Brussels for a summit on transatlantic relations, with no meetings yet arranged between European leaders and Trump at the World Economic Forum in Davos, where both are in attendance.

The confrontation places at risk a U.S.-EU trade relationship valued at 1.7 trillion euros ($2 trillion) in 2024 — roughly 4.6 billion euros per day, according to the EU statistics agency Eurostat — and has thrown into doubt ratification of a trade framework the two sides agreed in June.

## Tools at the EU's Disposal

The EU has three major economic levers it could use to pressure Washington: imposing new tariffs, suspending the U.S.-EU trade deal, and activating the Anti-Coercion Instrument — a mechanism informally called the bloc's "trade bazooka" that can sanction individuals or institutions found to be applying undue pressure on the EU.

The EU could also reinstate tariffs on U.S. goods worth 93 billion euros ($108 billion) that it suspended following the July trade framework. European Commission spokesperson Olof Gill said those levies would take effect Feb. 7 if the U.S. follows through on its tariff threat and the suspension is not extended.

Gill struck a restrained tone Monday. "Our priority is to engage, not escalate," he said. "The EU has tools at its disposal and is prepared to respond should the threatened tariffs be imposed."

## A Fraught Strategic Calculus

Europe faces a difficult balance, said Penny Naas, senior vice president of the German Marshall Fund, a Washington-based think tank.

"It's tough for the Europeans to find that space where they can both demonstrate strength without incurring significant retaliation. And as long as they're unwilling to accept retaliation, they're going to have trouble projecting strength," Naas said.

France and Germany — the EU's two largest economies — have signaled openness to deploying the Anti-Coercion Instrument, but there is widespread reluctance elsewhere in European capitals out of concern that doing so would escalate the confrontation.

"Tariff threats are unacceptable and have no place in this context," French President Emmanuel Macron said in a social media post. "Europeans will respond in a united and coordinated manner should they be confirmed. We will ensure that European sovereignty is upheld."

## Trade Deal Ratification in Doubt

The eight nations targeted by Trump's threat — Denmark, Norway, Sweden, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Finland — were identified in his post for their opposition to American control of Greenland.

Manfred Weber, the center-right German lawmaker who leads the largest group in the European Parliament, said Saturday that ratification of the June trade framework was "not possible at this stage."

European Council President António Costa said EU leaders agreed "that tariffs would undermine transatlantic relations and are incompatible with the EU-U.S. trade agreement" and expressed "readiness to defend ourselves against any form of coercion."

Ireland's Finance Minister Simon Harris urged restraint before a meeting of EU finance ministers in Brussels. "This is a time for cool heads," he said, adding that any deviation from the existing trade framework could have "potentially very, very significant consequences."

## U.S. Warns Against Retaliation

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, speaking Monday to reporters in Davos, said EU officials had been reaching out to the administration in the wake of Trump's threat, noting "evidently there are a lot of inbounds." Asked about the prospect of EU retaliatory measures, Bessent said: "I think it would be very unwise."

## EU Pursues Parallel Trade Diversification

As the standoff with Washington deepened, EU officials pointed to a broader strategy of trade diversification. Brussels signed a major trade deal last week with the five Mercosur nations of South America, as well as separate agreements with Indonesia and Japan. Officials are working on deals with the United Arab Emirates and India, where European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen is expected in late January to finalize negotiations.

The Anti-Coercion Instrument was established in 2021 after China restricted trade with Lithuania, which had built closer ties with Taiwan, claimed by China as its territory. According to the European Commission, the instrument's "primary objective" is deterrence and it "will, therefore, be most successful if there is no need to use it."
