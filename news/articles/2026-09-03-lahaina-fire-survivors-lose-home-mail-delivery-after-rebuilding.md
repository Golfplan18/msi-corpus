## Lahaina fire survivors lose home mail delivery after rebuilding

**Subtype:** fact

- The United States Postal Service has classified rebuilt Lahaina neighborhoods as "new development" and cut off door-to-door mail delivery to fire survivors returning to rebuilt homes.
- Three years after the August 2023 wildfire killed more than 100 people and destroyed roughly 2,000 residential buildings, only about 25% of homes have been rebuilt.

## USPS labels rebuilt Lahaina 'new development,' ending door-to-door service

The United States Postal Service has classified rebuilt neighborhoods in fire-stricken Lahaina as "new development" and cut off door-to-door mail delivery to returning fire survivors, three years after the August 2023 wildfire killed more than 100 people and destroyed roughly 2,000 residential buildings. Calvin Cox, whose home burned in the fires, learned of the policy only after filing a federal complaint in July.

Cox, who moved back to a rebuilt Lahaina residence in February, said a postmaster initially told him USPS had not yet re-established a neighborhood route because not enough new homes had been completed. Cox said he returned to the post office several times over several months without receiving clarity, and only learned of the new policy after pressing for an answer. In July, after filing a complaint with USPS federal headquarters and a back-and-forth with a customer relations specialist for USPS's Hawaii district, Cox received an email reading: "All dwellings that were affected by the fires in 2023 that have been rebuilt will no longer receive street delivery."

Cox said he was told the reconstruction was being treated as "new development" and that under current USPS policy, that means no door-to-door mail service. He called the designation shocking given Lahaina's history. "If the fire wouldn't have happened, then we wouldn't be a new development," Cox said. "But, because our houses burned down, we're a new development." Cox noted Lahaina is a centuries-old town and the first capital of the Hawaiian Kingdom, and that in 1962 the National Park Service designated downtown Lahaina as a national historic landmark.

For Kesa Ferguson, who was born and raised in Lahaina and is now raising her children in a rebuilt home after the family lost theirs, the change surfaced when the county called to ask if she had moved — her trash bill had been returned. She went to the postmaster to ask what was going on. "His response, literally word for word, was: 'Your address has been eliminated,'" she said. "That's where I pay my property tax. That's where we live. That's the address I give out to people. Like, you can't just delete me out of your system."

Ferguson said she had nearly missed medical prescriptions, tax forms, her Social Security card, and DMV paperwork for her son's driver permit. Cox said two home insurance policies had lapsed because he did not receive renewal notices, in a market where he said insurance is difficult to obtain. Cox also said he did not receive his voting ballot for the primaries.

Pam Reader, who returned to Lahaina two months after the fire to find her home destroyed, said her mailbox was one of the few things left standing. "Everybody's house was ash, but their mailboxes were standing," Reader said. She and her daughters had painted the metal box when the girls were young with drawings of fish, butterflies, and surfboards. She has since built a sturdier stand for it and coated it with shellac to preserve the artwork. "We lost all of our kids' little mementos and old drawings and all of these things, and then there's the mailbox with all of our art on it," she said. "Every little thing matters when you've lost everything."

Sara Clark, who has lived in Lahaina for nearly 40 years and moved back with her husband Gerald in June, said their mail was initially delivered for six weeks and then simply stopped. They watched mail carriers drive past their home, she said, and did not realize anything had changed. Gerald Clark said he was late on a car registration because he did not get a DMV notice. "Until we found out they were holding [our mail], we just didn't know," Gerald Clark said. "They just took it away without notifying anybody." Sara Clark said the situation is especially hard for the elderly. "You have these kupuna who are used to getting their mail at home and are also used to walking to the store, and now they're having to catch rides to go get their mail," she said.

The downtown Lahaina post office burned in the fire, and USPS told residents in a letter last month that it planned to permanently close that branch due to "irreparable damage." The main post office is more than two miles from the affected neighborhoods; residents say there is often only one clerk working, lines can extend out the door, and opening hours are limited. The cheapest PO boxes, which residents say cannot fit packages, rent for $250 a year.

The Lahaina mail cuts arrive against a broader retrenchment at USPS. President Donald Trump has called the postal service "a tremendous loser for this country." Weeks after Trump's January 2025 inauguration, then-Postmaster General Louis DeJoy, a top Trump donor, signed an agreement with Elon Musk's "department of government efficiency" (Doge) to pursue "efficiency gains" and "cost savings." Since then, the agency has cut thousands of jobs and closed distribution facilities, the Guardian reported. In March 2026, USPS declared a cash crisis and warned it would run out of money without congressional intervention; the Guardian reported that the Lahaina mail cutoff coincided with that announcement. Victims of major fires in past years, including in Colorado and California, did not see their mail service terminated, the Guardian reported.

Hawaii Rep. Jill Tokuda called the USPS decision "absolutely unacceptable." "My staff and I have been pressing USPS on this and are working directly with USPS Hawaii leadership to get it fixed," she said. "That's not how you welcome people home. Lahaina has already lost enough." Maui Mayor Richard Bissen wrote in a letter to the postmaster general: "Families should not lose an essential service simply because their homes were destroyed."

A USPS spokesperson said the agency had received "many mail delivery questions" from Lahaina fire victims and that "local and national postal service representatives are diligently working to evaluate this input and determine next steps." Cox has started a petition asking USPS to immediately reinstate pre-disaster mail delivery; it had collected about 130 signatures.

For Ferguson's 80-year-old mother, who is living in a trailer on the family's property while they rebuild, the loss of daily mail delivery compounds the loss of home. "I swear to you, my mom's favorite part of the day is probably going out to walk to her mailbox and check her mail," Ferguson said. "You lose your house, and then to have that as a cherry on top — it's kind of a slap in the face."
