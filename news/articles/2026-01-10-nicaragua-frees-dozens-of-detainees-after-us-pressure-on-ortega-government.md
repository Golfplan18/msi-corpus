Nicaragua's Interior Ministry announced Saturday that dozens of prisoners would be returned to their homes and families, as the United States pressed President Daniel Ortega's government over the detention of pastors, religious workers, the sick, and the elderly. It was not immediately clear who was freed or under what conditions, and the Nicaraguan government did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

The announcement followed a week of intensifying U.S. pressure on Nicaragua's government, coinciding with Washington's seizure of former Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro and public criticism of Ortega's record of imprisoning and exiling dissidents, journalists, and religious figures since 2018.

## Announcement Without Details

On Saturday, the Interior Ministry said in a statement that "dozens of people who were in the National Penitentiary System are returning to their homes and families." The ministry provided no list of names or further details about who was released or under what terms.

A day earlier, the U.S. Embassy in Nicaragua had noted that Venezuela had taken what it described as "an important step toward peace" by releasing political prisoners, while lamenting that in Nicaragua, "more than 60 people remain unjustly detained or disappeared, including pastors, religious workers, the sick, and the elderly."

## Years of Imprisonment and Exile

Nicaragua's government has imprisoned opponents, religious leaders, and journalists since mass social protests in 2018, which it violently repressed, according to the Associated Press. Since then, it has stripped hundreds of Nicaraguans of their citizenship and possessions and sent them to other countries, including the United States and Guatemala. It has shuttered more than 5,000 organizations, largely religious, and forced thousands to flee the country, the AP reported.

In recent years, the government has released hundreds of imprisoned political opponents, critics, and activists, stripping them of citizenship before sending them abroad. Observers have called those releases an effort to rid the country of organized opposition while deflecting international human rights criticism — leaving many of those expelled stateless.

## 'Great Deal of Fear'

Danny Ramírez-Ayérdiz, executive-secretary of the Nicaraguan human rights organization CADILH, said Saturday he had mixed feelings about the announced releases.

"On the one hand, I'm glad. All political prisoners suffer some form of torture. But on the other hand, I know these people will continue to be harassed, surveilled and monitored by the police, and so will their families," Ramírez-Ayérdiz said.

He attributed the move to pressure from Washington. "There is surely a great deal of fear within the regime that the U.S. might completely dismantle it," he said.

On X, the U.S. State Department's Bureau of Western Hemisphere Affairs posted a rebuke Saturday. "Nicaraguans voted for a president in 2006, not for an illegitimate lifelong dynasty," it said. "Rewriting the Constitution and crushing dissent will not erase the Nicaraguans' aspirations to live free from tyranny."
