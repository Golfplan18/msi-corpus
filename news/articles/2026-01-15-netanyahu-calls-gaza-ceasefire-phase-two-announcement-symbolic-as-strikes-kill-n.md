The ceasefire's second phase faces unresolved questions about Hamas disarmament, the composition of a proposed Palestinian governing committee, and reconstruction costs the United Nations has estimated at more than $50 billion — even as more than 450 people have been killed in Gaza since the October ceasefire halted major fighting.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu dismissed the announcement that Gaza's ceasefire had entered its second phase as a "declarative move" on Wednesday, casting doubt on how the accord's more demanding elements will be carried out, as Israeli strikes killed nine people in the territory on Thursday.

Netanyahu made the remark while speaking with the parents of Ran Gvili, an Israeli police officer whose remains are still held in Gaza. The prime minister told Gvili's parents that his son's return remained a top priority. The parents had earlier pressed Netanyahu not to advance the ceasefire until their son's remains were returned, Israel's Hostage and Missing Families Forum said Wednesday.

The announcement of the ceasefire's second phase, described by U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff as a significant step, left many questions unanswered — including the composition of a proposed apolitical governing committee of Palestinian experts, the timing of international force deployments, the reopening of Gaza's southern Rafah border crossing, and concrete plans for disarming Hamas and rebuilding the strip.

## Nine killed Thursday

Nine people died in several Israeli strikes on Thursday, according to local hospitals. The first strike killed two men. Three women and a man died in a second strike, according to Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital. Two more people were killed and five injured when a strike hit a house, according to Al-Awda Hospital in Nuseirat. One additional person was killed in Gaza City, Shifa Hospital said. Israeli military officials did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

The military separately said it had killed someone who had approached troops near the Yellow Line — which divides the Israeli-held portion of Gaza from the rest — and posed an imminent threat.

## Palestinians on the ground

Palestinians who spoke to the Associated Press questioned what advancing to the second phase would change in practice.

"We see on the ground that the war has not stopped, the bloodshed has not stopped, and our suffering in the tents has not ended," said Samed Abu Rawagh, a man displaced to southern Gaza from Jabaliya.

Hamza Abu Shahab, from eastern Khan Younis, said he was waiting for tangible improvements in access to food, fuel, and medical care rather than announcements.

"We were happy with this news, but we ask God that it is not just empty words," Abu Shahab told the AP in Khan Younis. "We need this news to be real, because in the second phase we will be able to return to our homes and our areas … God willing, it won't just be empty promises."

Gaza's population of more than 2 million has struggled to keep cold weather and storms at bay while facing shortages of humanitarian aid and a lack of more substantial temporary housing — a hardship that has persisted through the third winter since the war began on Oct. 7, 2023, when Hamas militants stormed into southern Israel, killing around 1,200 people and abducting 251 others.

## Death toll

More than 450 people have been killed since Israel and Hamas agreed to halt fighting in October, Gaza's Health Ministry said Thursday. UNICEF said more than 100 children were among those killed in that period. The ministry puts the total Palestinian death toll since the start of Israel's offensive at 71,441. The ministry, which is part of the Hamas-run government and staffed by medical professionals, is considered by the United Nations and independent experts to be the most reliable source on war casualties. Israel disputes the figures but has not provided its own count.

## Governing committee and reconstruction

The second phase of the ceasefire confronts more complex problems than the first, including disarming Hamas and transitioning to a new governance structure after nearly two decades of the group's rule in the strip.

Ali Shaath, an engineer and former Palestinian Authority official slated to head the proposed governing committee, said in a Wednesday interview with West Bank-based Radio Basma that he anticipated reconstruction and recovery to take roughly three years, beginning with immediate needs such as shelter.

"If I bring bulldozers, and push the rubble into the sea, and make new islands (in the sea), new land, it is a win for Gaza and (we) get rid of the rubble," Shaath said.

An Israeli official speaking on condition of anonymity said the committee's composition had been coordinated with Israel.

The United Nations has estimated reconstruction will cost over $50 billion. Little money has been pledged so far.

## Hamas's position

Hamas said it would dissolve its existing government to make way for the new committee, but has not made clear what will happen to its military arm, Hamas-affiliated civil servants, or civilian police.

Bassem Naim, a member of Hamas's political bureau, said Thursday that the group welcomed the committee's announcement as a step toward establishing an independent Palestinian state, but did not elaborate on the unresolved questions. Writing on X, Naim said "the ball is now in the court" of the United States and international mediators to allow the committee to operate.

Israel has insisted Hamas must lay down its weapons, while Hamas leaders have rejected calls to surrender, saying Palestinians have "the right to resist."
