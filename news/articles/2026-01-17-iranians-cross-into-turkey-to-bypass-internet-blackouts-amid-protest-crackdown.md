The blackout has imposed particular hardship on Iranians who depend on connectivity for their livelihoods, driving some to travel hundreds of kilometers to the Turkish border — and fund the trips by selling goods cheaper in Iran than in Turkey — while revealing a divided picture of public sentiment near the frontier.

KAPIKÖY BORDER CROSSING, Turkey — Iranians who rely on internet access for work and education have been making short trips across the Turkish border at the Kapiköy crossing in Van province, using Turkey as a workaround for a communications blackout that has largely cut off the country since Jan. 8, when Iranian authorities blocked service during mass protests and a government crackdown, the Associated Press reported Friday.

The Kapiköy crossing has not seen a major influx of Iranians seeking asylum. The protests that began Dec. 28 over Iran's ailing economy have largely subsided, and an uneasy calm has settled over much of the country. But the ongoing blackout has created hardship for those who depend on connectivity for their livelihoods.

On Saturday, witnesses said text messaging and very limited internet services briefly resumed in parts of Iran, though the situation remained volatile.

Iranians enter Turkey without visas, making brief crossings accessible for those living in the north of the country or with the means to travel from areas farther afield. Some funded their trips by selling cigarettes and tea — goods that are cheap in Iran and fetch higher prices in Turkey, according to the AP.

## Workers and students cross to stay connected

Sami Ranjbar, an e-commerce worker from Tehran, said he had stayed in Van for four days to conduct business that required internet access.

"My work depends on the internet, so I am forced to come here to access it and do my work, and then return to Iran to see how conditions develop," Ranjbar said. "If the internet is restored, we will stay, if not, we will be forced to come out again to use the internet, and go to neighboring countries or elsewhere."

Ali, 37, who spoke on condition of not being identified by his full name out of security concerns, said he traveled with his brother, who needed internet access for university applications. He said he planned to return to Tehran despite the dangers.

"A lot of our friends are arrested in Iran, and some of them have been killed," Ali said. "But we have to go back to Iran because we have our family there and we have jobs there."

The crackdown on protests has left at least 3,095 people dead, according to the U.S.-based Human Rights Activists News Agency. Ali said he held complicated views about the situation, supporting neither the current authorities nor the alternatives on offer.

"We are under a dictatorship, but we are also in danger of war from Israel and other countries," Ali said. "I'm not OK with being attacked by other countries, but I'm not OK at all with my government."

## Divided views near the frontier

The area of northeastern Iran near the border crossing is conservative, with a large population of ethnic Turks and Azeris, according to the AP. Residents said life in the region returned largely to business as usual following little unrest there.

Many travelers from the north said the protests in Tehran and other areas had little to do with them, and some echoed Iranian government allegations that the unrest was seeded by the United States and Israel.

Milad Soleimani, 28, who came from the Iranian town of Qatur — about 8 kilometers from the border gate — for a day's shopping, attributed the turmoil to external pressure rather than domestic discontent.

"It's true that people are struggling financially. But that is because of the things America and Israel are doing," Soleimani said in Turkish. "The majority supports their state. It doesn't matter if you have 2 million Pahlavi supporters in a nation of 94 million."

Ethnic Turks and Azeris in Iran have been broadly averse to exiled Crown Prince Reza Pahlavi, who has urged protesters into the streets, according to the AP. Ethnic minorities were repressed under Pahlavi's father, Shah Mohammed Reza Pahlavi, who was overthrown in the 1979 Islamic Revolution that brought the current Islamic Republic to power. Many Iranians from the north said they preferred the stability of the current government over the prospect of unrest and uncertainty.

Afshin, 24, a Kurdish traveler from the northeastern Iranian city of Khoy who gave only his first name out of security concerns, said stability was paramount.

"Iran is stable, that's what is good about it," Afshin said. "If there is a civil war, the country will split into 80 pieces."
