Gavin Newsom is entering his final year as California governor, with the Legislature set to hear from him and with his budget proposal scheduled for this week. The push for housing affordability, progress on homelessness and decisions about health coverage are expected to be central to how allies and critics judge his seven-year record—and to how voters look at him as he seeks the national stage.

The story frames the year as a “last chance” for Newsom to use his office to advance priorities that will follow him into his expected presidential primary run. It also says voters are increasingly focused on affordability, an issue that Democratic political consultant Kelly Calkin said Newsom is likely to keep in view.

Calkin told the outlet that the year is “a pivotal” one for Newsom, saying voters elsewhere are “feeling the pinch of affordability.” She added that Newsom will likely look through that lens when shaping the agenda for the next year, as competing interest groups press for policy wins and warn against unwanted cuts or regulatory rollbacks.

Homelessness, the article says, has worsened during Newsom’s tenure, even as his administration has poured more than $24 billion into efforts. It also points to other earlier goals that have fallen short or shifted, including the governor’s paid leave proposal for new parents: Newsom’s initial plan to give new parents six months was later pared down to a two-week increase, for a total of eight weeks, with gradual increases in how much the program pays.

In child care, the story says Newsom had pledged in 2021 that California would add 200,000 subsidized child care slots by this year, but that the plan was delayed by two years and remains tens of thousands of slots short. The article adds that Newsom has promised to resume the expansion this year.

The reporting also describes major changes in the governor’s approach to health coverage. It says Newsom campaigned on a single-payer public health care system, later pivoting to “universal coverage,” with the state slowly expanding coverage for low-income Californians, including immigrants who entered the country illegally. The story says the expansion was abruptly halted amid a budget deficit last year.

For housing, the article describes Newsom’s promise and the gap between ambition and results. It says about 40% of California households are “burdened” by their rent or mortgage—a policymaker benchmark used to indicate housing costs consuming more than a third of income. Newsom, the story says, ran on building an ambitious 3.5 million new homes by 2025, describing it as “achievable,” but it reports that in 2024 California added just under 120,000 new units—about a fifth of the annual pace needed to meet the goal.

The story says that in recent media appearances, the governor has downplayed the original number as a “stretch goal,” while advocates argue he has still accomplished more than previous governors. Ray Pearl, executive director of the California Housing Consortium, said, “Leadership sets the tone,” adding that the shift has changed “the focus and the conversation” toward serious state planning and production of affordable housing.

Pearl also pointed to actions Newsom has taken, including an early budget move to quintuple the state’s tax credit for low-income housing construction, backing laws that relax rules on where housing can be built, and legal battles with cities that he says refuse to plan adequate housing units. The article adds that Pearl hopes Newsom will support a proposed $10 billion bond that lawmakers want to place on this year’s ballot to boost a state affordable housing fund.

On modular housing, the article says Newsom has expressed interest in the final year, with the governor hinting at a coming legislative debate over how California can promote modular construction. It reports that an Assembly committee chaired by Democratic Oakland Assemblymember Buffy Wicks is set to discuss the method this year, and it quotes Newsom saying, “This holds a lot of promise. It holds a lot of political peril, in the context of the politics within labor. And that has to be accommodated and dealt with,” adding that it would be the closest he has come recently to stating a new policy goal.

The article says Newsom spokesperson Izzy Gardon did not provide details on housing or any other agenda for the governor’s final year. In comments to CalMatters, Gardon told readers to “stay tuned,” and refused interview requests about Newsom’s policy goals for his final year. The governor is expected to deliver his State of the State address on Thursday, the story adds.

While housing and homelessness sit at the center of the political test, the article also highlights pressure on health and social services. It reports that advocates for safety-net programs that Newsom has championed are urging him to maintain them as the state faces another budget deficit estimated at $18 billion. It says the agency overseeing those services accounts for nearly 40% of California’s general fund spending and that many programs are projected to lose significant federal funds through President Donald Trump’s tax and spending bill.

The article says Newsom’s health record includes incremental coverage expansions that, after the Affordable Care Act, grew from more than 90% of Californians insured at the time he took office to nearly everyone insured in 2023. It says the expansions first covered immigrant young adults and later older groups, but it adds that the governor undid coverage for the last group of immigrants to become eligible when he froze new enrollment for adults effective Jan. 1.

It reports that later this year, adult immigrants who entered the country illegally will lose Medi-Cal dental coverage, and next year most will face monthly premiums expected to force some off coverage. Amanda McAllister-Wallner, executive director of the advocacy group Health Access California, said she is worried the administration may consider further cuts, adding that she had hoped the “Health for All” expansion would be treated as a baseline long-term budget commitment—and that a backing off of those commitments would be the biggest disappointment for her.
