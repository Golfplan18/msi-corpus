Vietnam’s ruling Communist Party opened its National Congress on Monday in Hanoi, a five-yearly meeting expected to decide the country’s leadership and set the broad policy course for the next five years. The congress, Vietnam’s highest decision-making body, gathers delegates to elect party leadership and approve priorities that will shape Vietnam’s political and economic direction.

The congress drew 1,586 delegates to Hanoi. Delegates are set to elect about 200 members to the Central Committee, which in turn will appoint 17 to 19 members to the Politburo, the party’s powerful top leadership body.

Beyond selecting who leads Vietnam through 2031, the National Congress is also expected to shape how the single-party system responds to external turbulence as Vietnam pursues its ambition to become a high-income economy by 2045. The party is holding the meeting as U.S.–China rivalry intensifies and global economic conditions splinter.

One of the central questions for diplomats and analysts is whether General Secretary To Lam will be confirmed for a full five-year term and whether he will also combine the roles of party chief and state president. The article notes that Vietnam has traditionally been governed through a “four pillars” system that balances the party chief, president, prime minister and the National Assembly chair, and that collapsing those roles could concentrate power more than in recent decades.

To Lam, the article says, has overseen an ambitious round of bureaucratic and economic reforms since the late 1980s-era liberalization that began under predecessors. It describes reforms that cut tens of thousands of public-sector jobs, redrew administrative boundaries to speed decision-making, and initiated dozens of major infrastructure projects.

The article also describes To Lam’s background, saying he spent decades in the Ministry of Public Security before becoming its minister in 2016. It adds that he led the anti-corruption campaign championed by his predecessor, Nguyen Phu Trong, and that during an anti-corruption campaign the Politburo lost six of its 18 members, including two former presidents and the parliamentary head.

A visiting fellow in Vietnam studies said the internal alignment around To Lam includes a faction aligned with the ministry, while another main grouping is closer to a more conservative military. Nguyen Khac Giang, of the Singapore ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute, said conservatives were uneasy about Lam’s reform agenda, fearing the “Vietnamese socialist system could go astray,” and want conservative voices kept within the leadership as a check on power concentration and to keep Vietnam on a socialist path.

The congress also comes with a defining national ambition: whether Vietnam can transform into a high-income country by 2045. Delegates will finalize a resolution of a draft released in October, targeting average annual GDP growth of 10% or more from 2026 to 2030.

The article says that target comes after Vietnam fell short of a 6.5% to 7.0% growth target set for the first half of the decade, and it reports that Vietnam’s 2025 GDP grew at 8%. To reach the new goals, the draft calls for changes in how the economy grows, including upgrading industry, modernizing production, and relying more on science, technology and digital tools.

The draft also highlights specific industry steps, including that military-run Viettel broke ground in January on its first semiconductor chipmaking plant. It says the project aims to begin trial production by 2027 and is part of Hanoi’s strategy to build high-tech capacity and reduce reliance on foreign suppliers.

The draft places the private sector among the most important driving forces of the economy, underscoring an emphasis on business-led growth and a shift away from state dominance. Nguyen Khac Giang said To Lam may seek to deepen that approach by giving large private conglomerates a greater role in major projects across Vietnam.

The resolution further places “foreign affairs and international integration” on the same footing as national defense and security, reflecting the role of global markets and geopolitics for Vietnam’s export-driven economy. It also elevates environmental protection to a “central” task alongside economic and social development, a notable shift described in the article as Vietnam has grappled with pollution pressures tied to rapid growth.
