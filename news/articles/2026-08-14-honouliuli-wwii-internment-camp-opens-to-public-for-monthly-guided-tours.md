The National Park Service opened Honouliuli, the largest and longest-operating of Hawaiʻi's 17 known wartime detention sites, to public guided tours for the first time on July 18, launching a monthly pilot program that runs through the end of 2026. Free tours, led by a rotating group of about a dozen docent volunteers, take up to 22 visitors at a time, and more than 1,800 people are already on the waiting list.

Honouliuli opened in March 1943 in a remote, sweltering gulch in central Oʻahu and closed three years later. Prisoners who lived there gave the camp a Japanese nickname — Jigoku Dani, or "Hell Valley" — for the heat that pooled, windless, at the bottom of the ravine.

Hours after Japanese warplanes bombed Pearl Harbor and other military installations across Oʻahu on December 7, 1941, martial law fell over the Hawaiian Islands. The military took control of the territory almost overnight. The FBI, working from a list of suspects, began arresting people — including American citizens who had committed no crime.

Christine Ogura, the National Park Service superintendent in charge of Honouliuli since 2021, said: "I don't want local kids growing up not knowing that this happened in Hawaiʻi. It didn't just happen on the mainland."

This year marks 80 years since Honouliuli closed in 1946. The pilot program is the first time the National Park Service has opened the site to the general public.
