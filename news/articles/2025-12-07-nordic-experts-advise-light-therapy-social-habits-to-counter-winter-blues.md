Health researchers in Norway, Sweden and Finland say morning light therapy, dawn simulators and deliberate outdoor routines can ease the biological and psychological effects of winter darkness, offering evidence-backed options for the millions of people worldwide estimated to suffer from seasonal affective disorder and related conditions, experts told The Associated Press.

As the Northern Hemisphere approaches the winter solstice on Dec. 21 — the shortest day and longest night of the year — scientists say limited sunlight disrupts circadian rhythms and suppresses serotonin, but interventions exist that do not require a prescription.

## Sleep, mood and the circadian clock

Dark winters prevent the body's internal clock from resetting properly, said Dr. Timo Partonen, a research professor at the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare. Even when people sleep longer during winter months, quality suffers — leaving them fatigued through the day.

Partonen recommended using a dawn simulator, also called a sunrise alarm clock, that gradually brightens a bedroom to ease waking. He said social withdrawal also increases in winter, making people more irritable and prone to conflicts with friends.

Maintaining relationships and exercise routines through the colder months is important because symptoms rarely improve in isolation, Partonen said. He added that wintertime weight gain typically runs 2 to 5 kilograms (4 to 11 pounds) per year, driven in part by carbohydrate cravings that intensify in the evenings.

## Light therapy and seasonal depression

Seasonal affective disorder, known as SAD, typically involves depressive episodes that begin in fall and ease in spring or summer. A milder variant, subsyndromal SAD, is also recognized by medical experts.

Scientists have found that specialized cells in the eye convert blue-wavelength light into neural signals that affect mood and alertness — a pathway sunlight activates. Kathryn Roecklein, a researcher at the University of Pittsburgh, tested people with and without SAD and found that those with the condition are less sensitive to blue light than others, particularly during winter months, suggesting a biological basis for wintertime depression.

Christian Benedict, a pharmacology professor at Uppsala University in Sweden, said light therapy — using devices that emit light about 20 times brighter than regular indoor lighting — can benefit both SAD patients and those experiencing milder winter-blues symptoms.

"It's not like it's a fate, an annual or a seasonal fate, and you cannot do anything about it," Benedict said. "There are possibilities to affect it."

Research supports using a device rated at approximately 10,000 lux for 30 minutes each morning. Special lights cost from $70 to $400, though some products marketed for SAD do not reach clinically useful brightness. Insurance may cover at least part of the cost for those with a formal SAD diagnosis.

Partonen recommended using both a dawn simulator and a separate light therapy device before noon each day.

## Outdoor habits and the Nordic mindset

Ida Solhaug, an associate professor in psychology at the University of Tromsø — described as the world's northernmost university, located 350 kilometers (217 miles) north of the Arctic Circle — said embracing rather than dreading winter is a key Norwegian coping strategy.

"It's part of the culture," Solhaug said, adding that prioritizing a positive outlook functions as a survival strategy when daylight is scarce for months.

She recommended a mix of indoor and outdoor activities: channel hygge — the Danish focus on coziness — with blankets and films at home, but also venture outside with a thermos for fika, the Swedish tradition of a social coffee break. Even overcast days offer benefit from fresh air and movement, she said.

Solhaug said she does cold plunges in the frigid waters off Tromsø at least once a week and finds them revitalizing. "Challenge yourself to look for light in the darkness," she said.

Finland's President Alexander Stubb offered a more bracing prescription when the AP asked him last month how to survive a Nordic winter. "Take an ice bath and then followed up by a sauna and do one more ice bath, one more sauna, then a shower and go out there. You'll manage," Stubb said.
