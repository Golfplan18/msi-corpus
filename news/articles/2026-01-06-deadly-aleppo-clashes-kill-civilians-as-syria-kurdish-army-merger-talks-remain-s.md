Syrian government forces and Kurdish fighters exchanged the deadliest fire yet in Aleppo's contested northern neighborhoods on Tuesday, killing and wounding civilians across multiple districts as negotiations to integrate the U.S.-backed Syrian Democratic Forces into Syria's national army remained deadlocked. Syria's state-run SANA news agency reported that a soldier was killed and three others wounded, and state television said three civilians — including two women — died in residential shelling it attributed to the SDF. The SDF denied firing on civilian areas and blamed government-affiliated armed factions for the deaths.

The violence exposes the fragility of a March agreement under which the SDF was to merge with Syria's national army by the end of 2025 — a deadline that has passed with both sides accusing the other of seeking to wreck the deal.

ALEPPO, Syria — The deadliest clashes to date between Syrian government forces and the U.S.-backed Syrian Democratic Forces erupted Tuesday in Aleppo's predominantly Kurdish northern neighborhoods, killing and wounding civilians across multiple districts as a formal merger agreement between the two sides showed no sign of progress.

Syria's state-run SANA news agency said a soldier was killed and three others wounded in what it described as an SDF attack. Syrian state television later reported that three civilians, including two women, were killed and others wounded, including two children, in shelling of a residential area it attributed to the SDF. SANA also said nine employees of the Aleppo Directorate of Agriculture were wounded when shelling struck their office.

The SDF denied firing on civilian areas. In a statement, the group said a shell launched by "factions affiliated with the Damascus government" landed in the al-Midan neighborhood, targeting the adjacent Kurdish Sheikh Maqsoud neighborhood. "This indiscriminate shelling constitutes a direct attack on residential areas and exposes the lives of civilians to grave danger," the SDF said.

## Children among those wounded

At Aleppo's Al-Razi Hospital, Ahmad Abu Sheikh waited outside the operating room where his 4-year-old daughter, Fatima, had been in surgery for hours after shrapnel from a shell struck her. He said she lost her eye.

"I just want to know what can I tell my daughter when I see her? Where did her eye go?" he said.

The SDF separately reported that a government drone strike killed one resident of the Sheikh Maqsoud neighborhood and wounded two children, and that shelling in the nearby Bani Zaid neighborhood killed a woman and wounded dozens. Syrian state media did not report those incidents.

Afrin Jawan, a civil society activist in Sheikh Maqsoud, said in a message that thousands of civilians were besieged in Kurdish neighborhoods "and subjected to indiscriminate shelling with all types of heavy and medium weapons ... by factions affiliated with the Ministry of Defense in Damascus."

A tense calm returned to the area by Tuesday evening, but clashes flared again within hours.

## Merger agreement a casualty of mutual distrust

The predominantly Kurdish neighborhoods of Sheikh Maqsoud and Achrafieh in Aleppo have seen intermittent fighting in recent months. Previous rounds of clashes ended with truce agreements.

The SDF, which has tens of thousands of fighters and controls much of northeastern Syria, was the primary U.S. partner in fighting against the Islamic State group. It is also the main force that Syria's new government must absorb if it is to unify the country's fractured military.

Syria's interim government under Ahmad al-Sharaa signed a deal in March for the SDF to merge with the Syrian army by the end of 2025 — a deadline that has since passed. In April, scores of SDF fighters withdrew from Sheikh Maqsoud and Achrafieh as part of that accord.

Government and SDF officials met again on Sunday in Damascus, but government officials said no tangible progress had been made.

## Competing accusations

Syria's Defense Ministry accused the SDF on Tuesday of seeking to unravel the agreement. "The SDF organization once again proves that it does not recognize the March 10 Agreement and is trying to derail it and drag the army into an open battle of its choosing," the ministry said.

The SDF countered by accusing government forces of "a blatant violation of international humanitarian law" and said the attacks were "planned and deliberate, systematically targeting infrastructure and essential services, including water and electricity."

The integration effort is complicated by the makeup of Syria's new national army. Some of its constituent factions were previously Turkish-backed groups that have a long history of clashing with Kurdish forces.

Turkey considers the SDF a terrorist organization because of its ties to the Kurdistan Workers' Party, or PKK, which has waged a long-running insurgency in Turkey. A peace process between Turkey and the PKK is now underway.

Both the SDF and Syria's government have accused the other of seeking to derail the March agreement.
