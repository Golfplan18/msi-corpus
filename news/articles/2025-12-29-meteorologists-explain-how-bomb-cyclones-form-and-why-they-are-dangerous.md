A winter storm that "bombs out" is more than dramatic weather jargon. According to the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, bombogenesis occurs when a storm's central pressure drops at least 24 millibars in 24 hours — a threshold that signals a rapidly intensifying storm capable of producing blizzard conditions, downed trees, and power outages.

Storm intensity is measured by central pressure: the lower the pressure, the stronger the storm. A storm undergoing bombogenesis can rapidly generate heavy rain, blizzard conditions, and intense winds, the National Weather Service said.

"If you're watching TV at night and the weather report comes on and you're hearing 'bomb cyclone' being used, that usually means there's quite a bit of active weather going on," said Andrew Orrison, a meteorologist with the National Weather Service in College Park, Maryland.

Bomb cyclones occur mainly in fall and winter, when cold Arctic air meets warmer air masses, and are most common in Alaska, the Pacific Northwest, and the Great Lakes region — knowledge that can help residents prepare when forecasters issue warnings.

## What drives a bomb cyclone

The term bombogenesis comes from the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, which defines it as the process by which a storm's central pressure drops at least 24 millibars in 24 hours. Because storm intensity is measured by central pressure, a storm undergoing bombogenesis is strengthening rapidly — and the lower the pressure falls, the more dangerous the storm becomes.

Rapidly intensifying storms of this kind can produce heavy rain, blizzard conditions, and intense winds strong enough to down trees and knock out power.

## When and where they form

Bomb cyclones can form in any season, but they occur mainly during fall and winter, when frigid air from the Arctic pushes south and collides with warmer air masses.

"It's really the clash of those air masses that really kind of helps to generate the areas of low pressure in the first place," Orrison said.

In North America, the regions most likely to see bomb cyclones are Alaska, the Pacific Northwest, and the Great Lakes region.

The phenomenon is less common farther south. "It's not common to get bomb cyclones at lower latitudes. So generally speaking, you wouldn't see a bomb cyclone across, let's say, the southern United States," Orrison said.
