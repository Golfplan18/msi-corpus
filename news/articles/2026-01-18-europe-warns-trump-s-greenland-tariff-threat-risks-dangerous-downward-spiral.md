BERLIN — Eight European nations issued a joint statement Sunday warning that U.S. President Donald Trump's threatened 10% tariffs on countries opposing American control of Greenland "undermine transatlantic relations and risk a dangerous downward spiral," marking the most forceful collective rebuke those allies had delivered since Trump returned to the White House.

The statement from Denmark, Norway, Sweden, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Finland came roughly 24 hours after Trump's announcement Saturday and appeared to signal a departure from the diplomatic restraint European governments had largely maintained in their dealings with Trump in recent months — even as they sought his continued support for Ukraine.

For months, European leaders had opted for flattery and quiet diplomacy around Trump. Sunday's joint statement, combined with the arrival of small numbers of European troops in Greenland for a Danish military training exercise, appeared to mark a step away from that approach.

## EU convenes emergency talks

Following emergency consultations among the EU's national envoys Sunday, EU Council President Antonio Costa said the bloc's leaders had agreed that "tariffs would undermine transatlantic relations and are incompatible with the EU-U.S. trade agreement." Costa said EU leaders expressed "readiness to defend ourselves against any form of coercion" and was expected to convene a summit of bloc leaders later in the week.

EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said China and Russia stand to gain from any rift between the United States and Europe. "If Greenland's security is at risk, we can address this inside NATO," Kallas said on social media.

Six of the eight countries targeted by the threatened tariffs are members of the 27-nation European Union, which functions as a single economic zone in trade matters. There were immediate questions about how the White House could implement tariffs against the EU, and it remained unclear how Trump could act under U.S. law, though he could potentially cite emergency economic powers that were at the time subject to a U.S. Supreme Court challenge.

## Allied governments respond

The joint statement said the eight nations "stand in full solidarity with the Kingdom of Denmark and the people of Greenland" and called for dialogue "based on the principles of sovereignty and territorial integrity." It specified that troops deployed to Greenland for the "Arctic Endurance" exercise "pose no threat to anyone."

Danish Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen, speaking to reporters in Oslo, said his government was committed to dialogue. "We will not give up on that. ... So we will stay on track — unless U.S. decides differently," he said.

Norwegian Foreign Minister Espen Barth Eide, appearing at the same news conference, said his country would not yield to pressure. "We will not allow ourselves to be put under pressure, and those types of threats (of U.S. tariffs) are unacceptable between close allies," he said.

U.K. Prime Minister Keir Starmer spoke directly with Trump and told him that "applying tariffs on allies for pursuing the collective security of NATO allies is wrong," a Downing Street spokeswoman said. That call followed conversations Starmer had with NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen, and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen.

Rutte wrote on social media that he had spoken with Trump and looked forward to seeing him at the World Economic Forum in Davos later that week.

## Domestic criticism in the United States

The threatened tariffs drew criticism from members of both parties in the United States.

Sen. Mark Kelly, an Arizona Democrat, said the policy would make Americans "pay more to try to get territory we don't need." He added on social media: "Troops from European countries are arriving in Greenland to defend the territory from us. Let that sink in."

Former Vice President Mike Pence said he supports the United States ultimately acquiring Greenland but raised concerns about Trump's methods. Pence said he had concerns about whether Trump had the constitutional authority to impose unilateral tariffs on NATO allies and warned the current approach threatens "to fracture that strong relationship, not just with Denmark, but with all of our NATO allies," in remarks on CNN.

## Criticism from Trump's own allies

The tariff announcement also drew pushback from some of Trump's closest European supporters.

Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni, considered among Trump's strongest allies on the continent, said she had spoken to him about the tariffs, which she described as "a mistake." She said the deployment of European troops to Greenland had been misunderstood by Washington and was not directed against the United States but at providing security against "other actors" she did not name.

Jordan Bardella, president of Marine Le Pen's far-right National Rally party in France and a member of the European Parliament, called the threats "commercial blackmail" and called for the EU to suspend its tariff deal with the United States.

In Britain, all major political parties — including the hard-right Reform UK party — criticized the tariff threat.

## Expert: tariff use in territorial dispute 'unprecedented'

Rasmus Søndergaard, a senior researcher at the Danish Institute for International Studies, said Trump's announcement was "unprecedented" because tariff threats normally arise from trade disagreements, not territorial disputes between allies.

"That's of course why we're seeing the response from European countries saying 'enough is enough,'" Søndergaard told the Associated Press. He added that a strategic calculation appeared to be driving the European pushback: "If you give in to Trump on this, what will be the next thing? And at some point you have to sort of push back."

Søndergaard also noted that the tariff threat had in effect leveled the playing field: European nations cannot compete militarily with the United States, but the EU can wield economic leverage through reciprocal tariffs.

Late Sunday, Trump posted on his social media platform that NATO had warned Denmark for two decades about the Russian threat to Greenland and that Denmark had not acted. "Now it is time, and it will be done!!!" he wrote.
