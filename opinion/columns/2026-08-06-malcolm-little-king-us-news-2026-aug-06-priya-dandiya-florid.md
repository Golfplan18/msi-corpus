Ron DeSantis redrew Florida’s congressional map to pre-empt voters and protect Trump’s House majority.

Pia Dandiya is a teacher, a mother, a first-generation American, and a first-time candidate for Congress in Florida. She had declared her candidacy in the 21st District, preparing a long-shot challenge to Brian Mast. Then the governor tore apart the district lines in the middle of the decade, after the decennial map was already complete, and pushed Dandiya into the newly configured 22nd District, an open seat that national Democrats now regard as one of their strongest opportunities.

So she picked up the maps and went to work.

Dandiya has said something true: most families are not staying awake at night worrying about congressional lines. They are worrying about rent, groceries, their children, their parents, making ends meet, and whether the country is being left in good hands.

That is true. It is also the sentence the man who drew the lines is counting on.

She is right about the families. She is wrong only if she thinks the map is separate from those problems.

The map is the problem translated into machinery.

Florida’s mid-decade redistricting was encouraged by Trump as he sought to shore up a narrow House majority. DeSantis’s intervention was expected to put as many as four additional seats within his coalition’s reach, leaving that coalition with an advantage in 24 of the state’s 28 congressional districts. The governor pushed the map through and signed it into law. There is [no mystery about who benefits](/articles/2026-04-30-new-florida-congressional-map-could-cost-democrats-up-to-four-seats/).

When the Florida Supreme Court [allowed the new map to stand](/articles/2026-06-10-florida-supreme-court-allows-republican-drawn-house-districts-for-midterm-electi/) for the midterm elections, it did not rule that the map was fair. It did not even rule, on the merits, that the map was legal. The underlying [partisan-gerrymandering challenge continues](/articles/2026-05-16-florida-s-new-us-house-map-faces-court-test-over-partisan-gerrymandering-ban/). What the court ruled was that the election was too close to stop.

That is not the same as ruling that the map is right. The distance between those two holdings is where a republic goes to be buried.

District 22 makes the transfer visible. It was once concentrated almost entirely in Palm Beach County, a solid-blue urban stronghold. DeSantis’s map stretched it across the state’s southern region, joining populated communities on both coasts to rural and agricultural counties in between. A “Frankenstein district”: a political body stitched together for electoral purposes, then presented to its residents as if it were a natural community.

It is not a natural community. It is a designed advantage.

Nonpartisan forecasters at Sabato’s Crystal Ball now rate the district as leaning Republican. Its communities have different histories, economies, political priorities, and local institutions. They have nothing in common except the need to be put in the same box.

The public justification for such maps is always procedural: population changes, geographic continuity, compactness, representation. Those principles matter. A district cannot remain frozen forever. Political coalitions do compete for power. Democracy does not eliminate conflict.

But the cui-bono question comes first. Who benefits when a district is redrawn in the middle of an election cycle? Who gains when communities with different interests are forced into one contest? Who loses when voters and candidates must learn boundaries designed by the officeholders who benefit from them?

The beneficiaries are not mysterious. They are the governor, the officials who ordered the intervention, and the national coalition whose House majority the map is meant to protect. The cost is dispersed across voters, candidates, local organizations, and communities that must spend their political energy learning a district built for someone else’s advantage.

Dandiya had been running against Mast. The map made that race beside the point, so she switched to the open seat. She has done everything the system asks of a good citizen since: raised more than $2 million, won endorsements from Florida officials and labor organizations, including the state chair, sitting members of Congress, the AFL-CIO, Emily’s List, and the national committee’s Red to Blue program. She is traveling through heavily populated coastal areas, rural agricultural counties, and the strange political geography DeSantis assembled between them.

She has miles on the car and hope in the speech.

None of it should be required of her.

The distance, the money, the steep learning curve, the rebuilding of an entire campaign: that is the tax the line-drawer imposes on ordinary people. The line-drawer pays none of it.

This is how concentrated power operates when it wants to avoid looking concentrated. No one has to announce that voters are being denied meaningful representation. Officials redraw the lines, call the result reform, and wait for the public to argue over the technical details. The transfer happens beneath the vocabulary.

The polite word is redistricting. The accurate word is pre-emption.

DeSantis moved the people’s decision off the table before they could make it. Too many Florida voters were going to vote the wrong way. The problem was democracy. The solution was to draw democracy out of the room.

The tired speech treats district lines as inside baseball and the kitchen table as the real world. That is the inversion. Lines are not separate from the kitchen table. The lines decide who writes the insurance law, who funds the schools, who sets the rules around rent, wages, health care, and the price of heat.

Fix the lines, and the rest of the agenda follows. That is why the governor fixed them.

A well-made gerrymander hides itself. It looks like geography. It feels like fate. It persuades people that the outcome was waiting inside the landscape all along.

But the landscape did not redraw itself.

The state’s map already faced a court challenge over Florida’s ban on partisan gerrymandering. A court’s involvement does not make a political operation politically innocent. Legality is not the same thing as democratic legitimacy. A map can survive a procedural test and still reveal a governing class more interested in choosing its voters than in answering to them.

The wickedness of this problem is that every technical defense contains a piece of truth. Districts must be redrawn. Communities do need representation. A map cannot eliminate conflict from democracy.

But that true half is then used to conceal the larger fact: the people drawing the lines possess the power to decide which conflicts will matter and which voters will be made electorally irrelevant.

The question is not whether redistricting exists. The question is who controls it, who gains from it, and whether those who bear its consequences have any practical way to correct it.

The map’s defenders do not need to prove that every voter will be silenced. They need only keep the public from asking why the same intervention happened when it could benefit the people ordering it. That is the motivated stop. The argument moves from “districts must change” to “this particular power arrangement must change in this particular direction,” and then pretends the second conclusion came from geography.

That is not geography. That is power wearing a measuring tape.

Dandiya’s campaign is an answer to that machinery, though not yet a sufficient one. She entered the August 18 primary against Kaysia Earley with national attention behind her. She is crossing communities the mapmakers joined without consent, listening for the common material pressures beneath the manufactured boundaries.

That work matters because the architects of the map want voters to experience politics as fragmentation. The district is supposed to feel too broad, too strange, too divided to organize. The stitched-together territory is supposed to exhaust the candidate and confuse the voter until the imposed arrangement begins to feel permanent.

But political community does not have to be inherited from a line on paper. It can be built through shared demands: wages that cover life, housing that does not consume the household, care for children and elders, schools treated as public obligations rather than local accidents, and government that answers to residents instead of using residents as raw material for a national seat count.

The map is designed to separate those demands. Organizing can connect them.

Martin Luther King Jr.’s late political work, in the run-up to the Poor People’s Campaign, insisted that legal change without economic and democratic reconstruction was only a foothold. The point was not to admire the foothold. The point was to ask what structure still stood beyond it. King’s late work refused the comfortable story in which formal rights completed the work of freedom. The machinery beneath the right had to be changed as well.

Florida’s map is one of those structures.

The formal right to vote is not the whole of political freedom when officials can prearrange the field in which the vote operates. A ballot cast inside a rigged boundary is still a ballot. But the people who drew the boundary have already made a decision about how much that ballot will be allowed to matter.

The answer is not despair. It is not faith in the benevolence of the people who drew the lines. It is organized counterpressure: candidates who cross the artificial boundaries, voters who compare the public rationale with the distribution of power, courts that examine the machinery rather than merely its paperwork, and movements that refuse to let a district’s design dictate the limits of its people’s imagination.

The August primary will not undo the gerrymander. It will not make the courts brave. It will not restore the old map. But it can answer the map with something the map cannot fully engineer: people learning what was done to them and acting together anyway.

The line can be drawn. It can also be challenged.

The map can be imposed. It can also be made politically useless by people who organize across its seams.

That is how the arc bends—not as a weather report, not by benevolence, not because power suddenly develops a conscience. It bends because specific people, in a specific moment, push it.

In Florida this August, the pushing begins with a ballot.
