Hotter weather is driving Spokane families from their homes.

At my bench in Adams County, the distance between Washington and Wisconsin is a map distance, not a weather distance. The same heat that dries a western hillside changes the work of every crew trying to hold a fire line. A cooler two days can give firefighters room to work. Upper-90s temperatures, lower humidity, and wind can take that room back.

Three wildfires near Spokane have destroyed about 850 buildings and forced roughly 67,000 people to evacuate since last weekend, according to the Associated Press. No deaths have been reported. Damage crews have not finished determining how many homes were lost or damaged. Firefighters established containment lines around all three fires after two cooler days helped crews make progress.

That is progress. It is not safety.

A containment line is a human answer to a moving problem. It holds when the fuel, weather, terrain, and fire behavior allow it to hold. The forecast is a reminder that the line is not a wall and the fire is not obligated to respect the work put into it.

The [earlier report on the Spokane-area destruction and evacuations](/articles/2026-08-04-spokane-area-wildfires-destroy-hundreds-of-homes-force-60-000-to-evacuate/) described the first scale of the damage. The count—about 850 buildings and 67,000 evacuees—shows how quickly a fire becomes more than a fire. It becomes a housing emergency, a school problem, a work problem, a livestock problem, a medical problem, and a question about whether people have somewhere to return.

That is the part of a wildfire people far from it can miss. Evacuation is not the end of the danger. It is the beginning of waiting to learn whether the place you left will still be there.

One of the three fires was deliberately set, authorities said, and a man was arrested and charged with arson. That charge matters. Whoever set that fire must answer for lighting it. But the arrest does not explain the conditions that let a fire move through a community at this scale.

Arson can supply the spark.

Heat, dryness, wind, fuel, and the condition of the landscape determine how much power that spark acquires. Those are different facts, and both have to stay in the frame. The forecast is not an accusation against the person charged with arson. It is evidence about the conditions firefighters and evacuees face after the flames begin.

Rural communities are repeatedly handed the smallest part of this story. A spark becomes the whole explanation. The weather becomes background. The homes become a count. The evacuees become a number. The people who benefit from treating climate as scenery get to avoid the harder question: what conditions are we building around ourselves, and who is left to defend them?

Wisconsin's climate record is not Spokane's fire record. The Wisconsin Initiative on Climate Change Impacts reports that statewide temperatures have risen about 3 degrees Fahrenheit since 1950 and annual precipitation has increased about 17 percent. The 2010–2019 decade was Wisconsin's wettest on record. Those figures do not turn every fire into a climate story, and they do not prove what caused these Spokane fires.

They do show a climate moving toward less stable conditions: wetter periods that grow vegetation, followed by heat and dryness that turn vegetation into fuel. A place can be too wet in one season and too dry in another. That is not a contradiction. It is what a changing climate looks like on the ground.

Fire does not need a single cause when several conditions line up.

Aldo Leopold's *A Sand County Almanac* begins with attention to the season as it is, not as a slogan says it ought to be. His land ethic treats land as a community to which people belong, not scenery behind the human story. That kind of attention is not sentimental. It is practical. Fire crews need to know what the air, soil, fuels, and wind are doing now. County planners need to know which risks are becoming ordinary. Families need more than a warning after the flames are already on the road.

The crews cutting containment lines, the families sleeping away from home, and the people waiting for word about a house are all inside the same land system. The fire makes that membership impossible to ignore.

The Community Collapse Contradiction is plain enough. People are told that preparation is a private responsibility until the disaster becomes too large for private people to survive. Then the public crews arrive, public roads carry the evacuees, public agencies assess the damage, and public money begins the long work of rebuilding.

No one should resent that work. It is what a society is for.

But the country keeps treating public protection as an emergency expense instead of permanent infrastructure. We spend heavily after the fire and argue about preparation before it. We measure response in heroic hours while leaving communities to bargain with insurance companies, housing shortages, drying forests, and hotter summers on their own.

Containment lines are temporary public infrastructure. They are built by people working under dangerous conditions, and they hold only while the conditions permit them to hold. A hotter, drier, breezier week turns a line into a test of labor, equipment, evacuation routes, housing capacity, and public coordination.

Wendell Berry's writing insists that membership in a place carries obligations. A place is not merely something to develop, insure, abandon, or rebuild after the next disaster. It is a set of relationships: homes, roads, schools, watersheds, working forests, public agencies, and the people who depend on all of them. When those relationships are weakened, the fire has more than timber to consume. It consumes the margin that lets a community recover.

The person charged with arson may face a criminal case. The families who lost homes face years of paperwork, temporary housing, repair bills, disrupted work, and the question of whether rebuilding in the same place is possible. Those burdens are not distributed equally. The people with the fewest savings have the fewest choices.

The response cannot stop at an arrest. Authorities must protect evacuation corridors, maintain firefighting capacity, harden water systems and escape routes, preserve defensible space where it is genuinely useful, plan for temporary housing, and stop treating repeated climate emergencies as isolated surprises. People who lose homes need shelter, money, records, schools, and a path back—not praise for their resilience after the institutions around them have failed.

Real fire policy begins before the smoke. Climate projections are infrastructure information. Rural and semi-rural communities need the staff and money to prepare before the upper-90s forecast arrives, not a ceremony after the last house burns.

The line around a fire is built by hand, but it is supported by institutions. If those institutions hold, people have a chance to go home. If they do not, the fire keeps traveling long after the flames are gone—through mortgages, rents, insurance notices, empty lots, and the children who learn that home can disappear in one afternoon.

The fire line outside Spokane is a boundary between what crews can still protect and what the weather is trying to take. That boundary is being rebuilt every day. So is the boundary between a county that can care for its people and one that merely counts the losses.
