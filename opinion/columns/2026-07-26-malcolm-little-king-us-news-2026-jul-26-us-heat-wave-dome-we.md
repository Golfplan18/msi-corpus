Pete Miller stands at an intersection in Omaha on Sunday morning and watches people collapse.

"We're seeing a lot of people who are exhausted, drained by the heat in a way that is really dangerous," said Miller, an outreach manager for Threshold CoC, a homeless services network. He reported that standing outside for thirty seconds produces drenching, and that the situation would persist through the night. The National Weather Service warned that children, older adults, and people with certain medical conditions face the highest risk of heat stroke or death — populations whose overlap with the homeless communities Miller serves is not coincidental but structural. Forty million Americans across the central and southern United States remained under extreme heat warnings as the heat dome that had already [pushed 70 million under advisories earlier this week](/articles/2026-07-25-70-million-under-heat-advisories-as-dome-expands-across-central-us/) settled back into its second consecutive day of trapping dangerously high temperatures over the country's midsection. Rapid City, South Dakota, recorded an all-time high of 112°F on Saturday. The continental United States endured its second-hottest first half of any year on record in 2026.

These are the receipts. Now follow the money.

---

A heat dome is an atmospheric event in which high pressure traps hot air over a region, amplifying and prolonging already-dangerous temperatures. It is not a mystery. It is not unforeseeable. It is a phenomenon whose increasing frequency and intensity were predicted by climate scientists decades ago, predicted with specificity, and whose underlying driver — the combustion of fossil fuels — is the single most-studied causal chain in the modern atmospheric sciences. The Guardian reported what every relevant scientific body has confirmed: extreme heat events are intensified by the climate crisis, which results from burning gasoline, oil, and coal. The National Centers for Environmental Information placed 2026's first-half temperatures at second-hottest on record.

This is not a surprise. It was never going to be a surprise.

The question a structural-political analysis must answer is not *what is happening* — the thermometer answers that — but *why the response to what was known did not arrive in time to prevent 40 million Americans from spending a weekend in the conditions Pete Miller described*. The answer to that question runs through the offices of the fossil fuel industry, through the apparatus of manufactured doubt, and through decades of regulatory capture that made the heat dome a policy choice rather than an inevitability.

The fossil fuel industry's own internal research confirmed the connection between carbon emissions and atmospheric warming as early as the 1970s. ExxonMobil's internal scientists produced models projecting warming trajectories that proved remarkably close to what actually occurred. The industry's public-facing apparatus did something different: it funded a multi-decade campaign of manufactured doubt about the very science its own researchers had validated. This is not an accusation I am leveling without evidence. The pattern is documented by Naomi Oreskes and Erik M. Conway in *Merchants of Doubt* — the same institutional architecture, the same playbook, the same recurring personnel — and confirmed by the 2015 InsideClimate News investigation into ExxonMobil's internal versus external climate communications. The fossil fuel industry deployed the same five-element denialism pattern the scholarly literature identifies across tobacco, vaccines, and evolution: manufactured controversy against established scientific consensus, funded by interested parties, repeated until the public discourse could not distinguish the industry's manufactured uncertainty from genuine scientific debate.

The industry manufactured doubt. The doubt delayed policy. The delay produced the conditions in which Pete Miller watches people collapse on an Omaha sidewalk while the heat index climbs past 100°F and the humidity makes thirty seconds outdoors a medical event.

This is the structural anatomy of a heat dome as a political-economic phenomenon: atmospheric physics does the trapping, but decades of deliberate institutional obstruction did the enabling. The fossil fuel industry extracted record profits from the energy source whose atmospheric consequences scientists had documented and the industry's own researchers had confirmed. The industry then spent a fraction of those profits funding the doubt apparatus that prevented the policy transition away from that energy source. The transition that would have reduced the frequency and intensity of the extreme heat events now affecting 40 million Americans did not happen because the fossil fuel industry made its non-happening profitable.

That is the cui-bono trace. Who benefits from continued fossil fuel combustion? The fossil fuel industry. Who bears the cost of the atmospheric consequences? The 40 million Americans under extreme heat warnings, with the distributional weight falling hardest on those with the least capacity to protect themselves — the people Pete Miller's organization serves, who have no air conditioning to retreat to, no cooling centers within reach, no employer who will let them stay home, no second home in a cooler latitude.

King's distinction between charity and justice was structural and unsentimental. In his 1967 address *Where Do We Go From Here*, he argued that true compassion does not fling coins to beggars; it restructures the edifice that keeps producing beggars in the first place. The heat dome is a beggar-producing edifice. Cooling centers and water stations are the coins. The structural intervention — the transition away from the fossil fuel combustion that drives the atmospheric conditions trapping 40 million Americans under dangerous heat — is the restructuring the edifice requires.

The industry knows this. Every energy company's own scenario-planning accounts for it. The question is not whether the transition will come but whether it will come on the schedule the atmospheric record demands or on the schedule the fossil fuel industry's extraction cycle prefers. The heat dome answering that question this weekend in Rapid City and Omaha and Shakopee, Minnesota — where Canterbury Park canceled its horse race on Sunday because the heat made outdoor activity medically inadvisable — is not an abstraction. It is the lived consequence of a decades-long institutional decision to prioritize extraction over atmospheric stability.

The atmospheric volatility the heat dome produces does not confine itself to heat. NWS meteorologist Bob Oravec noted that the moist air associated with the dome was generating severe thunderstorms, flash flooding, and hail risks around the Great Lakes. "If you have a heat dome in one part of the country, you'll have a different corresponding weather in another part," Oravec said. "It's all related." The structural point is the same in meteorology and in political economy: the system is interconnected. The fossil fuel industry's extraction produces atmospheric instability; the atmospheric instability produces cascading weather events; the cascading events produce compounding harm on the populations least equipped to absorb it. The heat dome is not an isolated event. It is the visible surface of a system whose deeper architecture was built by specific actors making specific decisions for specific beneficiaries over specific decades.

The record is not ambiguous. The continental United States experienced its [second-hottest first half of any year on record in 2026](/articles/2026-07-14-heat-dome-pushes-90-plus-overnight-temperature-records/). Heat domes have [intensified](/articles/2026-07-11-heat-dome-to-bring-dangerous-temperatures-to-two-thirds-of-us-forecasters-warn/) across the summer, each one carrying the documented fingerprint of fossil-fuel-driven atmospheric warming. The science was confirmed by the industry's own researchers. The doubt was manufactured by the industry's own apparatus. The profits were extracted while the doubt held. The 40 million Americans under extreme heat warnings this weekend are living in the gap between what the industry knew and what the industry's public-relations apparatus told the public.

---

The arc of the moral universe bends toward justice, King said, and the sentence is true. But the arc does not bend by itself. It bends when specific people, in specific moments, refuse to accept the manufactured doubt that delays the bending. The heat dome will pass this week — NWS forecasts relief toward the middle of the week — and the next one will come, because the atmospheric conditions producing them are not seasonal anomalies but structural consequences of an energy system the fossil fuel industry chose to protect over the objections of its own scientists and the evidence of its own internal research.

The people on Pete Miller's sidewalks in Omaha do not need another cooling center. They need the structural transition that would prevent the conditions requiring cooling centers. That transition was delayed by an industry that knew what its product was doing to the atmosphere and spent decades manufacturing doubt about the knowing. The 40 million Americans under extreme heat warnings this weekend are the diffuse cost-bearers of that concentrated extraction. The fossil fuel industry is the concentrated beneficiary. The heat dome is the receipt.

The question is not whether the arc bends. The question is whether it bends before the next heat dome kills the people this one merely exhausted.
