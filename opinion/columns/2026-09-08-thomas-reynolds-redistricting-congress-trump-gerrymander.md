Trump's redistricting operation is rigging House maps and stealing an election.

On Tuesday the Supreme Court declined, without explanation, to overturn a Missouri Supreme Court ruling that suspended a Trump-backed congressional map and forced the state to revert to its prior districts for the November ballot. The justices' denial — by the standard cert procedure — leaves in place the state high court's September ruling, itself building on the state supreme court's earlier order that a citizen referendum petition had automatically suspended the new map under Missouri law. Voters who cast ballots in the August primary under one map will vote in November under another.

It is the rare setback in a campaign that has otherwise proceeded largely unchecked. Since early last year Trump has urged Republican state legislatures and governors to redraw congressional maps mid-decade — outside the normal post-census cycle — to shore up a House majority the party did not win on the merits in 2024. Missouri was supposed to add to that edge. The state supreme court's ruling, now left standing by the Court's silence, takes it off the board.

The Elections Clause gives Congress authority to prescribe the "times, places and manner" of federal elections, and under *Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council*, 570 U.S. 1 (2013), that authority is paramount even where state law would otherwise control. Switching maps between an August primary and a November general, Hanaway argued, burdens the federal franchise — voters who cast ballots under one map would be voting in the general under another — and federal courts have authority to vindicate that federal interest. The structurally hardest version of her claim: a state-level referendum process, filed and given effect after a primary has already been conducted, has now displaced the legislatively enacted map for a federal election. That is not a frivolous federal question.

*Rucho v. The federal courthouse door has been closed to challenges to the rigging operation the Trump coalition is now running.

Missouri's referendum process worked. The state supreme court read its own referendum-suspension statute the way it had been read for decades and ordered the map held pending a popular vote. The Supreme Court's Tuesday denial leaves that ruling in place. But the federal courthouse door has been closed in a way that makes Missouri an exception rather than the rule.
