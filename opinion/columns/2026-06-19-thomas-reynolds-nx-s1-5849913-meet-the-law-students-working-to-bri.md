The federal judiciary exempts its 30,000 employees from the workplace civil-rights protections that bind every other employer in the country. The judges who decide what Title VII means for you and your workplace have built a system in which that law does not reach their own clerks, public defenders, and probation officers. Thirty thousand workers answer to judges who answer to no one.

That is the claim at the center of a cert petition filed this month by ten students at Emory University's Supreme Court Advocacy Program. The students represent Caryn Strickland, a former federal public defender who says she faced sexual harassment on the job and found no independent body to hear her complaint. The petition asks the justices to decide whether the judiciary's internal self-policing system provides the due process and equal protection the civil rights laws promise.

The steel-man for the judiciary is real. Article III independence is not a slogan — it is structural insulation the Framers designed so that judges could not be pressured by the political branches. A judge who must answer to an external employment agency for staffing decisions is a judge whose independence has a leash attached. Congress understood this. When lawmakers passed the Congressional Accountability Act in 1995 and extended Title VII protections to workers on Capitol Hill, they gave the judiciary, as Emory student Andrew Taramykin noted, wide berth to carry out those protections on its own terms. The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts has defended the resulting system as robust and says changes are underway to make it easier to report wrongdoing.

That is the defense. Here is what it produces in practice.

Every federal circuit has built its own human-resources program to resolve workplace disputes. Those rules leave judges to oversee complaints against people they know and might work with every day. There is no independent agency. There is no neutral decision-maker outside the courthouse. When a public defender reports harassment by a supervisor, the complaint routes to people embedded in the same hierarchy that controls the complainant's hours, assignments, and career trajectory. The structure is, in the language of civil rights law, a textbook conflict of interest wearing the robes of institutional autonomy.

Title VII prohibits workplace harassment and discrimination. By 1995, Congress extended those protections to legislative-branch employees through the Congressional Accountability Act, which established the Office of Congressional Workplace Rights as an independent enforcer. The legislative branch got a neutral arbiter. The judicial branch gave itself a mirror. The circuits' internal programs typically assign an investigating judge to review a complaint; the complainant can be required to meet with the respondent; the final decision rests with the chief judge, who may have professional and personal ties to the respondent. There is no provision for discovery, cross-examination, or a neutral arbiter. In short, the system is designed to resolve disputes within the judicial family, not to adjudicate rights.

The Judiciary Conduct and Disability Act of 1980 created a complaint mechanism, but it was designed for misconduct by judges, not for the broader workplace-rights universe that Title VII governs, and it routes complaints through the same chief judges who run the circuits. The impeachment data marks the outer boundary: just fifteen federal judges have been impeached in the history of the republic; eight have been removed. That remedy is reserved for the most extreme cases, applied on average once every fifteen years, and functionally irrelevant to the thirty-thousand-employee workplace the judiciary actually operates.

NPR's prior investigation found that victims of harassment by federal judges "often find the judiciary is above the law." Three federal judges in three different states came under public scrutiny just this month for off-the-bench behavior, a reminder that the question is not hypothetical. The problem is not that individual judges are unusually prone to misconduct. The problem is that the institution has built a system where misconduct has no adjudicant who is not also a colleague.

The constitutional stakes are plain. Due process requires notice and an opportunity to be heard — "the fundamental requisite of due process of law," *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254, 267 (1970) — and a decision-maker free from personal or institutional bias, *Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.*, 556 U.S. 868, 876 (2009). The judiciary's internal system may satisfy the first two in some circuits, but it fails the third as a matter of structure: the decision-maker is part of the same institution whose conduct is at issue. The equal-protection claim is similarly straightforward: nothing in the Constitution permits the judicial branch to give its own employees fewer protections against discrimination than it gives the employees of private corporations, state governments, or Congress itself.

Sofia Bettini, the recent Emory graduate who helped draft the petition, named the trade-off: "Autonomy for the institution cannot come at the cost of basic workplace rights and safety for people within it." The people who pay the price are not the judges. They are the clerks who discover, after they have accepted the position, that they have forgone protections that "just seem that fundamental." They are the public defenders and probation officers who have nowhere to turn and for whom speaking up risks everything.

The Supreme Court has asked the Department of Justice for a response, due next month. The Roberts Court has shown sustained appetite for dismantling deference to institutional self-governance — *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 144 S. Ct. 2244 (2024), overruling Chevron deference, being the most prominent recent example. If the Court was willing to strip federal agencies of the deference they claimed was essential to their expertise, it cannot credibly exempt the judiciary from the same logic. The cert petition places a mirror before the Court. It must decide whether it believes in its own principles, or whether the federal courts are above the law they impose on the rest of us.
