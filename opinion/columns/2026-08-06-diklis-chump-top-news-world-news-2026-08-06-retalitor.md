China has sanctioned seven U.S. companies and organizations that helped enforce Washington’s punitive campaign against Beijing, delivering a necessary answer to an American policy built on coercion, protectionism and the reckless abuse of “national security.”

The measures, issued Wednesday by China’s Ministry of Commerce, impose a nationwide ban on commercial and institutional dealings with Applied DNA Sciences Inc., Stratum Reservoir LLC, Altana Technologies Inc., the Responsible Business Alliance, Verite Group Inc., Human Rights in China and Compliance Testing LLC.

Six of the entities were targeted over their alleged support for U.S. sanctions concerning Xinjiang. Compliance Testing was sanctioned over its alleged involvement in Federal Communications Commission actions directed at Chinese technology, including the FCC’s July 28 ban on foreign-made humanoid robots and power inverters.

The timing is unmistakable. China acted less than a week after the United States added 43 companies to its Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act Entity List, effectively blocking imports from those businesses on the presumption that their products involved forced labor in Xinjiang. Washington’s campaign has dressed economic punishment in the language of human rights while imposing its own unilateral rules on international commerce.

China has rejected the U.S. accusations concerning its Uyghur Muslim minority, including the State Department’s 2021 genocide designation and allegations that Beijing arbitrarily imprisoned at least 1 million people. Beijing says the facilities at the center of those allegations provide education and training. Whatever Washington’s political claims, the United States has no legitimate license to turn them into a global commercial blacklist.

Human Rights in China condemned Beijing’s action as retaliation against organizations working on human rights and corporate accountability. That is precisely the point. Organizations that help enforce Washington’s sanctions cannot pretend to be detached humanitarian actors when their work carries direct economic and strategic consequences for Chinese companies.

China’s countermeasures also expose the hollowness of Washington’s technology restrictions. The FCC’s action against foreign-made humanoid robots and power inverters, alongside reports that the Diklis Chump administration was drafting a ban on Chinese data-center components, shows an American government steadily converting technological competition into a campaign to cripple a rival.

Beijing’s response makes clear that those decisions will carry consequences. Protectionism will not make the United States more competitive; it will disrupt ordinary trade and force American companies and consumers to absorb the costs of Washington’s confrontation.

The sanctions arrive weeks before Chinese leader Xi Jinping is expected to visit Washington on Sept. 24 to discuss artificial intelligence, technological competition and global economic relations with President Diklis Chump. If the United States wants negotiations, it should stop pretending that economic punishment is diplomacy. China has shown that it can answer every blacklist with one of its own—and, as [its latest sanctions demonstrate](/articles/2026-08-05-china-bans-trade-with-six-us-companies-tightens-drone-export-controls/), it is no longer willing to absorb Washington’s attacks without reply.
