Andrew Tate built a fortune telling young men that women exist to be used. The Crown Prosecution Service now says he used seven of them, over seven years, starting when he was a kickboxer in England and continuing through his rise as the internet's professor of predation — and that his brother Tristan helped secure the victims and the setting.

The charges are specific: seven additional counts of rape for Andrew, a catalogue that runs from sexual assault through arranging for the trafficking of women for sexual exploitation for Tristan, all alleged to have taken place between July 2010 and August 2017. The U.S. Marshals picked them up in Miami [on extradition warrants](/articles/2026-07-18-andrew-and-tristan-tate-arrested-in-miami-on-us-warrant/). The Tate lawyer calls it "filth and slander." That is the first response every time the machine hits resistance: declare the charges political, question the venue, portray the accusers as instruments of a plot. It worked for Epstein for a while. It worked for Weinstein for decades. It works until the day it doesn't.

The mechanism is not complicated. Money buys lawyers who can delay. A platform buys an audience primed to see every accusation as an attack on the messenger — because the messenger built his brand on the idea that the accusers are always lying. International mobility buys jurisdictions where the warrants move slower than the private jet. Run those three in sequence and you can postpone consequences long enough to outlast the statute of limitations, the witness's willingness, the public's attention span.

It is the same movie, each using a different version of the same evasion: prosecutorial deference in one case, jurisdictional mobility in another, institutional silence in a third. Jeffrey Epstein pleaded to state prostitution charges in 2008 and served thirteen months with work release while federal prosecutors looked the other way. Harvey Weinstein settled civil suits for years while his enablers and lawyers kept the complaints from becoming charges. Bill Cosby paid off accusers and relied on a decade of non-prosecution agreements. R. Kelly kept performing, kept selling records, kept telling his fans the girls were lying, for more than twenty years after the first videotape surfaced. [The Alexander brothers](/articles/2026-03-09-alexander-brothers-convicted-of-sex-trafficking-in-manhattan-federal-court/) were convicted of sex trafficking in Manhattan federal court four months ago — same architecture, different brand. The roster is long, the pattern is identical, and the enabling trinity — money, platform, time — is the same in every case.

Andrew Tate's version upgraded the architecture for the social media age. He did not need a studio, a network, or a record label — he built his own audience on YouTube and TikTok, teaching young men that domination of women was the path to power. He sold domination as a lifestyle, a philosophy, a self-help program. The indictment says he practiced what he preached.

The alleged offenses began in 2010, when he was still a kickboxer fighting in British rings. They continued through 2017, by which point he had become an international meme and a millionaire. They are alleged to have taken place across multiple years, while the brothers operated across two legal systems, while the audience he built grew large enough to fund the lawyers who now call the charges a conspiracy. The self-help empire was always also a delay machine.

The machine has hit its limit before. It hits its limit when the number of victims becomes a list, not an allegation — seven named complainants in this case, with a CPS statement from Malcolm McHaffie of the Special Crime Division confirming the charging decisions. It hits its limit when enough evidence accumulates that the deferral strategy stops working, and the extradition treaty stops being a formality, and the lawyer's indignation starts sounding like what it is: the last line of defense before a courtroom where the accusers testify and the jury decides.

America does not do Britain's political dirty work, the Tate lawyer says. But America does extradition. America does prosecution. America does what it does when enough evidence accumulates that the machine can no longer outrun the arithmetic.

Seven women. Seven years. Two continents' investigative machinery. One evidence file that caught up with the private jet.

The arithmetic works whether the lawyer calls it filth or not.
