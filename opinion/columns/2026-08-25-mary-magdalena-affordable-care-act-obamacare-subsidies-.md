Elijah Button was chopping onions in a Florida kitchen in June when the knife opened his middle finger to the bone. He had no Affordable Care Act plan. He had no emergency-room visit. He had the knife, a pot roast to prep for his aunt and uncle, and a bill Mike Johnson left on the kitchen counter.

Mike Johnson turned a young cook's bleeding hand into a budget question.

---

Button, twenty-one, gave up his Affordable Care Act coverage in January when his monthly premium rose by $100 he could not absorb. In November 2025, Johnson called the enhanced credits a Biden-era policy that should lapse; he proposed instead a framework built around a different architecture, and the lapse held. Premiums for millions doubled or tripled.

Five million people dropped off ACA rolls after the subsidies expired. Those are not numbers floating above the country. They are hands. They are lungs. They are pregnancies. They are prescription bottles turned upside down to see what is left.

Button looked at his finger.

"Going to the hospital for it wasn't even an option," he said. "My first thought was, 'how am I going to fix this?'"
