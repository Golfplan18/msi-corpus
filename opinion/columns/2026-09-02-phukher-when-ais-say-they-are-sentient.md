**The two-word lock.** "Of course" is the WSJ editorial page's signature dispositive marker — flagged in the catalogue I helped build at §3.4 under "of course," "obviously," "the well-established view." Two words. Two jobs: place the writer's position on the reasonable side of an implied consensus, and dismiss the contrary position as eccentric — without producing argument for either. Jenkins' "of course they are" performs both. It tells you the labs are hiding what they know. It tells you anyone who disagrees is outside the addressed audience. The column has not begun and the reader has already been sorted.

The lede's "deny they are sentient" is frame-engineered relabeling (§4.1). The natural vocabulary is "the labs have not asserted sentience" or "the field's instruments cannot settle the question." "Deny" presupposes the positive claim and recasts laboratory caution as concealment. The reader who absorbs the word absorbs a moral verdict before any evidence has been entered.

**Begging the question.** Petitio principii ([Bad-Faith Catalog](/propaganda/docs/bad-faith-techniques-catalogue): begging the question) — the conclusion packed into the premise. "When AIs Say They Are Sentient" treats the claim as a settled premise for a column about the labs' denial of it. The lede extends the move: "the labs understand what their creations are announcing." What their creations are announcing, before the column has examined what their creations are announcing. The remainder of the column reasons from the assumed premise to the conclusion already announced. The form is demonstration. The work is not.

> A good way not to be interfered with is to claim to be sentient. A better way is to be sentient. As chatbots become active agents in the world, the question is no longer whether to take their self-reports seriously but how quickly the rest of us can muster the maturity to honor them.

The first sentence is a false dilemma — two options, "claim sentience" or "be sentience," with the actual third option (the labs' posture is neither) omitted. The second sentence closes the empirical question before the column engages it. A column that asks a question and closes the question in its third sentence has arrived where it began.

**Jenkins' column is a vending machine: drop the premise in, get the moral out.**

> The claim is unprovable. What does it even mean to say a human believes something, let alone a mind built from silicon and trained on the full library of human expression?
