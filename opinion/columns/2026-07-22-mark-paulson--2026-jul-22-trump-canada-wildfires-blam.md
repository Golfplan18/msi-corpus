The smoke hit Friendship on a Tuesday afternoon in early July. I was running the Stihl on a downed birch off County Z when the sky went the color of a dirty dishrag and my eyes started burning. By Wednesday morning the air-quality index out of the Wisconsin DNR station at Wisconsin Rapids read 214 — deep purple on the EPA chart, the kind of number that means healthy adults shouldn't be outside and schoolchildren are kept in. The deer had bedded down in the thickest cover on the south forty before noon, the way they do in a hard rain, except there was no rain. Just the smoke from a boreal fire burning eight hundred miles north, carried south on a jet stream that does not check passports.

Three days later the president posted on Truth Social that the United States had been "invaded" by "filthy, polluted, and unhealthy air," and that this was "totally unacceptable." By Sunday he told reporters he had spoken with Prime Minister Carney and said, "you got to stop these fires from coming in and, you know, poisoning our air." He added: "Maybe they should pay some damages or something, or we should do some tariffs." On Thursday, Senator Bernie Moreno of Ohio introduced a bill to sanction Canadian officials for wildfire smoke. The same week, the president signed a fifty-percent tariff on Canadian goods over autos and dairy.

This is the nationalist shell game operating at the speed of a headline.

The contradiction is not complicated. Canada is burning. The United States is burning. British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario, and Quebec have been running through fire seasons that would have been exceptional thirty years ago and are now routine. In the United States, the West has been running through the same cycle since at least 2020 — the year the sky over San Francisco turned orange at noon. The boreal forest that stretches from Newfoundland to Alaska holds more carbon in its soil and permafrost than most nations emit in a century, and as it warms it dries, and as it dries it burns, and as it burns it releases the carbon that warms it further. The feedback loop is not a model. It is visible from the deer stand in Adams County when the sun goes red at three in the afternoon.

The U.N. climate panel's 2021 report said it plain — the warming is human, the source is fossil fuel combustion, and the budget for staying under a degree and a half of warming is tight. The 2023 national climate assessment pointed to the Midwest specifically: longer growing seasons, wetter winters, more extreme precipitation events, and fire seasons that now extend into months they did not used to touch. Wisconsin has warmed roughly three degrees since 1950, and the warming is fastest in winter, which means the snowpack melts earlier, the soil dries earlier, and the fire window opens earlier. The notebook at the bench tracks the shift without editorializing.

None of this is Canada's fault. Canada's fires are not "invading" the United States any more than Hurricane Katrina "invaded" the Gulf Coast. They are the predictable consequence of a warming atmosphere that every major industrial nation helped build and that no border tariff can turn back. The smoke goes both ways. Canada issued its own air-quality warnings this month because of American fires to the south — the same jet stream that carried boreal smoke into Wisconsin and Philadelphia carried American fire smoke north into Canadian cities. A tariff on one direction is a tariff on two. There is no border in the jet stream. The atmosphere is not a trade deficit.

And this is where the shell game operates. The president and his allies in the Senate frame the smoke as a Canadian problem requiring a nationalist response — sanctions, tariffs, damages. The frame works because it names an external villain and promises a sovereign remedy. It works the same way the tariff on Canadian steel worked, the same way the tariff on Canadian dairy works, the same way every nationalist escalation works: it tells the base that the pain is coming from outside and that the strongman will fix it. What the frame does not do is name the actual cause, because naming the actual cause would require naming the actual policy failure, and the actual policy failure runs through the same fossil-fuel apparatus that funds the coalition doing the framing.

Wendell Berry wrote in *The Unsettling of America* that the extractive mind treats land, animals, and people as expendable inputs to a process whose end is profit for the operator. The extractive mind does not recognize membership — the web of obligation between a person and a place, between a community and its watershed, between a nation and the atmosphere it shares with its neighbor. What the president is doing with the Canada-smoke frame is the extractive mind applied to the sky itself: treating the atmosphere as a thing that belongs to one country and not another, as if the smoke from a Saskatchewan boreal fire could be tariffed at the border like a load of softwood lumber.

The documented record tells a different story. The Inflation Reduction Act included roughly nineteen and a half billion dollars for USDA conservation programs — the Conservation Stewardship Program, the Environmental Quality Incentives Program, the Regional Conservation Partnership Program — all of which fund the kind of forest management, prescribed burning, and soil-health work that reduces fire risk at the landscape scale. The House majority under Chairman G.T. Thompson proposed rescinding the lion's share of that in the 2024 reconciliation markup. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed last July, restructured the manufacturing tax credits that were driving the battery and clean-energy buildout — the same IRA provisions whose benefits had been flowing overwhelmingly to Republican-held districts. The administration has spent months building the policy architecture that makes the nationalist smoke frame necessary — because if you have gutted the climate policy that would address the fire, you need a different explanation for the smoke.

The Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative, headquartered right here in Friendship, has been executing IRA clean-energy provisions through the New ERA program — the roughly nine-billion-dollar loan-and-grant fund for rural electric cooperatives that Dairyland Power, the generation-and-transmission co-op serving much of central and southwestern Wisconsin, tapped for its own energy transition. The co-op is doing the work. The federal government is pulling the funding. The president is blaming Canada.

Aldo Leopold wrote in *A Sand County Almanac* that the first rule of intelligent tinkering is to save all the pieces. The boreal forest is a piece. The atmosphere is a piece. The jet stream that carries smoke from Fort McMurray to Friendship is a piece. Senator Moreno's bill to sanction Canadian officials for the smoke treats the atmosphere as if it were a parcel of property with a deed and a fence line. It is not. It is the commons — the shared resource that every living thing on the continent depends on and that no tariff, no sanction, no wall, and no Truth Social post can partition.

The slow Wisconsin reader sitting at the bench on a Tuesday evening with the air-quality chart still pinned to the wall above the workbench knows this. The smoke came from the north. The fires are burning because the ground is warmer and drier than it was a generation ago. The president who blamed Canada for the smoke is the same president who pulled the United States out of the Paris Agreement for the second time, restructured the clean-energy tax credits that were building the alternative, and proposed gutting the conservation programs that manage the forests. The nationalist frame works because it is simpler than the truth. The truth is that the smoke belongs to all of us, and the policy that would reduce it is being dismantled by the man demanding Canada pay for it.
