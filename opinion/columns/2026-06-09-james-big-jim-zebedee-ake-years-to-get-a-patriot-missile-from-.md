Lockheed and the Pentagon sell empty launchers while defenders die. The government has signed a deal to more than triple production of the PAC-3 MSE—the Patriot interceptor, at $4 million a pop—to about 2,000 missiles a year, but Lockheed does not expect to hit that number until the end of 2030. That’s a mathematical fiction dressed in procurement paperwork. The system takes two years to fabricate a missile that survives six seconds in the sky, and the messaging pretends that a bulk order from a Camden fax machine automatically ramps the line—as if production capacity were a shift knob and not a brittle supply chain.

The production pipeline isn’t broken; it’s a masterpiece of engineered dependency. More than 400 companies feed parts into Lockheed’s assembly plant in Camden, Arkansas. The seeker in the missile’s nose—the component that lets an interceptor lock onto an incoming threat—comes from a single Boeing factory. Circuitry inside the missile is commercially obsolete, forcing the Pentagon to buy overpriced replacements from foreign suppliers. The rocket motor is built by L3Harris, which still needs another year to open a factory expansion near the Lockheed plant. Every tier of this supply chain is a choke point, and every choke point is a toll booth. When L3Harris’s CEO tells an investor conference that quadrupling missile output means quadrupling “cases, igniters, valves, throttles,” he is not describing a problem; he is celebrating a captive market. The “great opportunity” he sees is the permanent emergency that keeps the public treasury open for business.

Eisenhower warned of the “unwarranted influence” of the military-industrial complex in his 1961 farewell address, and the warning was never about the size of the standing army. It was about the structural shift—a permanent arms industry that would bend national policy toward its own perpetuation. The Patriot pipeline is the physical embodiment of that warning. More than 80 percent of the second-tier suppliers—the suppliers’ suppliers—also feed components into other missile programs. That means no single program can be accelerated without jostling the whole web. The result is a system in which speed becomes impossible and every crisis merely ratchets the baseline higher. The war with Iran, the war in Ukraine, the record-high foreign orders: each one pours more concrete into the foundation of a war economy that cannot, by design, be wound down.

Andrew Bacevich, in *Washington Rules*, dissected exactly this dynamic a decade before it arrived on an Arkansas factory floor: the post–World War II national security apparatus operates on a logic of “more”—more missions, more commitments, more weaponry—regardless of strategic need. The Patriot pipeline is a case study in that logic. The missile itself is a defensive system, but the production model is pure offense: it consumes capital, ties up industrial capacity, and locks the nation into a procurement cycle that outlasts any imaginable conflict.

The Pentagon and its contractors will call this a capacity problem. They’ll point to labor shortages, to the six months it takes to recruit and clear workers for the factory floor, to the two-year lead time on a Boeing plant expansion. They’ll say they are “eliminating bottlenecks and shortening lead times wherever possible, while still maintaining the rigorous performance and safety standards required.” That sentence, from a Lockheed spokeswoman, is the corporate lullaby sung through every procurement scandal and stockpile drawdown since the Cold War. It is true only in the narrowest sense: the bottlenecks are real, but they are real because the system was built to bottleneck. If the goal were speed, the supply chain would not be dispersed across 400 companies in dozens of congressional districts. If the goal were readiness, a missile that takes six weeks to assemble would not need two years to gestate. The goal has always been to convert federal dollars into corporate revenue with maximum friction and minimum accountability. These protocols were defined during peacetime; a war that burns through interceptors at combat rates renders today’s peacetime-quality timeline a self-imposed strangulation.

I’ll concede that the men and women who assemble these missiles are not the enemy. The workers in Camden and the machinists at Boeing aren’t the ones holding the front lines hostage; they’re doing a job in a plant that happens to sit at the center of an extractive architecture. But that architecture is what we have allowed to pass for defense policy.

Replenishing the stockpiles to pre-Iran war levels requires at least three years and funding Congress has not allocated, according to a CSIS analysis churned out by the Pentagon’s own beltway boosters. The administration knows this. The contractors know this. A procurement system that needs three years and unfunded appropriations to restock weapons already consumed in weeks is not an industrial base—it is a managed illusion of readiness.

Somewhere in the Middle East, a Patriot battery is staring at a half-empty launcher because the next interceptor is still in queue behind 1,500 others. The MIC’s real product isn’t the missile. It’s the shortage.
