Ed Miliband is laundering the cost of war through Britain’s household budget.

The foreign secretary says reopening the Strait of Hormuz is “essential” to bring down the cost of living. He says the British public is worried about petrol, diesel and energy bills, and that the “single biggest thing” the government can do is secure a ceasefire in the Iran war.

That is a remarkable sentence because it makes a war-related crisis sound like an energy-bill intervention.

The kitchen-table version is simpler. A family opens the petrol-station receipt, then the electricity bill, then the grocery account. The numbers are higher because a strategic shipping route has become a flashpoint and trade has been disrupted. The family did not choose the war, close the waterway or set the price of oil. It is still asked to absorb the result.

That is not a cost-of-living plan. It is a war-cost transfer.

We have already covered how [Iran and Oman sought to reopen the strait through narrow talks](/articles/2026-07-28-iran-oman-seek-reopening-of-strait-of-hormuz-in-narrow-talks/). The important point here is not that safe shipping does not matter. It does. Nor is it that fuel and food prices are imaginary. They are the first line items households cut around, and the hardest ones to avoid.

The true half of Miliband’s argument is real: reopening the route could reduce pressure on trade and energy markets. The suppressed half is who pays while governments turn a military crisis into a household-management problem.

The family pays first. The school run costs more when petrol rises, even though wages do not rise with every trip to the pump. Fuel surcharges reappear in grocery deliveries and transport costs, turning an international crisis into another charge on the household account. The companies with the greatest power to capture price increases remain elsewhere in the story. The war becomes the explanation; the household becomes the adjustment mechanism.

Anne Helen Petersen’s *Can’t Even* is useful here because it names the trick: structural pressure gets converted into a personal demand for better optimisation. Cut the driving. Delay the repair. Shop around. Move the children’s activities. Accept a colder house. Call it resilience. Call it prudence. Call it the price of living in the real world.

Taylor Swift’s “You’re On Your Own, Kid” supplies the emotional soundtrack of that privatised burden. The phrase is often treated as a song about personal endurance, but its sharper economic reading is institutional: “on your own” describes the American—and increasingly international—care infrastructure offered to families when institutions make necessities expensive. Petersen identifies the trick; Swift gives it a chorus. The lateral safety net is the group chat, the neighbour, the parent who can cover pickup, the partner who rearranges work. It is not a foreign-policy strategy.

But there is no spreadsheet cell labelled “make an international conflict cheaper.”

In my own household, I know the difference between a bill that can be moved and a bill that cannot. I can delay replacing a sofa. I cannot delay petrol when the childcare pickup is twenty minutes away and the workday does not end when the daycare closes. I can buy fewer discretionary groceries. I cannot make food distribution costs disappear by becoming more disciplined. The line item that does not appear in the press conference is the time and money required to keep the household functioning after prices rise.

Miliband is right that people are worried. He is wrong to make that worry do the political work of turning a war-related cost shock into a reason for deeper military dependence. The question is not whether reopening the strait matters. The question is why the public is being offered military diplomacy and household austerity as one package.

A serious cost-of-living policy would protect families from the shock instead of asking them to wait for geopolitics to improve. It would treat energy, transport and food as public economic infrastructure, not as private inconveniences that each household must manage alone. The corporal works of mercy begin with feeding the hungry and sheltering the vulnerable. They do not begin with telling the hungry to become better at absorbing the bill.

The kitchen-table conversation started with the petrol receipt and the electricity bill. The tea has gone cold because no policy arrived. War is still being sold as relief.
