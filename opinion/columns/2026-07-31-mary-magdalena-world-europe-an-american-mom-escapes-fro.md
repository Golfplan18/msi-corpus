Emmanuel, you are the president of a G7 nation that built cell-broadcast alert infrastructure capable of pushing a location-aware emergency message to every phone in a danger zone in seconds. You have it. The European Union mandated it. France operates it. And on the evening of July 23, with the worst wildfire crisis since the Second World War bearing down on the Atlantic coast, your government evacuated Biscarrosse by sending a man to walk gate to gate and ring the bells one house at a time.

You knew, Emmanuel. You said so yourself. You stood in front of the cameras and named what was coming — a fire season that would dwarf anything in living memory, a blaze that produced the first pyrocumulonimbus cloud ever recorded over France, a sky that made its own weather. You named it. And then your apparatus chose the most labor-intensive, failure-prone method in the modern emergency-management toolkit to warn the people it had been built to protect.

The result is what anyone who has ever missed a knock would predict. People who were not home did not know. People who were asleep did not wake. People who were at the beach did not hear. And by the time they understood — ash falling, burning wood dropping from the sky — the roads out were barricaded, and the official advice was to shelter in place and hope.

Rebecca, the American mother the *Wall Street Journal* found, watched a wall of solid orange rise behind her daughter's bedroom window. She had been at the beach. No one there seemed alarmed. She went home. By morning, a police officer buzzed her gate and told her it was too late to leave. For four days she and her two children stayed in a house ringed by some of the worst fires in French memory. She left once, to buy frozen baguettes, orange juice, and pasta from the one supermarket that had reopened. The only other customers were soldiers.

Emmanuel, this is the gap. This is the specific, documented failure at the last mile of a system that had every other mile covered. A wealthy nation with every tool in the modern emergency-management toolkit, and the point of failure was still whether someone was home when the officer knocked. The technology to push the alert existed. Your government operates it. And you chose not to use it — or you chose not to ensure that whoever runs the system for you would use it — or you chose, in the allocation of resources and attention, that the door-to-door knock was sufficient.

Your throat tightens when you swallow this paragraph, Emmanuel. You feel it — a small mechanical resistance, a catch, the swallow that does not complete. That is the smoke you chose for them. Your mouth has filled with the bright flat water that comes just before you are sick. Rebecca had the resources to book a flight out of Bordeaux when the secondary roads reopened. The captain told passengers they could see the raging fires from the windows. She chose not to look. Rebecca will be fine. She has a holiday home in France and a home in Tennessee. She has two healthy children who were never physically harmed. Her terror was real. Her smoke was real. The *Journal* gave her name, her hometown, the ages of her children, the horseback-riding lessons, the afternoon surf. She was the American mom who escaped, and the *Journal* made her the story.

But Rebecca was not the story, Emmanuel. Rebecca was the face that made the story legible to an American reader. The story is the three hundred thousand. The story is the families in the villages she drove through and called ghost towns. The story is the Spanish and French households whose only home was the one the fire took, whose only route out was the roadblock they could not pass, whose escape, if it comes, will be through a door that has no one on the other side to write it down. The campsites that burned along the coast were not holiday homes. They were the only shelter for families who had no secondary residence to flee to.
