Donald Trump is growing frustrated with Markwayne Mullin over repeated off-message remarks, but the conflict reveals how the administration uses Mullin to give mass deportation the appearance of orderly government.

Markwayne Mullin tried to say one true sentence. Immigration does not steal American jobs in every industry. In some places, immigration can help offset low labor-force participation. That is ordinary economic reality. It is also a betrayal of the administration’s chosen story, in which every immigrant is a threat and every deportation is proof of national strength.

Mullin answered the backlash with another sentence: “NO amnesty for illegal aliens. Ever.” The contradiction is the policy. A secretary may acknowledge the labor market in the morning, then preside over a system arresting between 1,500 and 2,000 immigrants a day. He may suggest that some Haitians complete paperwork and remain legally, then stand inside an administration promising to remove them. He may pause most vehicle stops after two fatal shootings, only for Trump to reverse the policy the next day. He may urge caution over Fourth of July fireworks amid lightning, only to be reminded that even prudence is disobedience when the president wants a spectacle.

The administration does not need a consistent moral vision. It needs obedience. Mullin’s remarks about jobs, his suggestion that some Haitians might pursue permanent status, and his temporary halt to vehicle stops all carried the same offense: they introduced judgment into a system that demands loyalty first.

That is why Mullin’s appointment has become a test of something larger than one Cabinet secretary’s discipline. Trump wants a department that looks humane enough to survive public scrutiny and acts cruelly enough to satisfy the demand for mass deportation. Mullin’s brief hesitation exposed the machinery. The arrest numbers reveal it.

We have already seen what happens when that machinery operates without even the appearance of restraint. Federal immigration officials shot and killed two American citizens in Minneapolis in back-to-back incidents within a week. After those shootings, Mullin temporarily halted most vehicle stops by federal immigration officials. Trump reversed the pause the next day. The deaths did not produce a lasting reconsideration of immigration enforcement; they made restraint politically dangerous.

You call this enforcement. I call it a deportation machine that turns human beings into daily totals and families into administrative problems. “Illegal” is not a natural species of person. Law produced that category, and officials now use it to make the person disappear behind the category.

The Torah says, “You shall not wrong or oppress a resident alien, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt.” Jesus says, “I was a stranger, and you welcomed me.” He also says, “I was a stranger, and you did not welcome me.” The question is not whether a secretary can repeat the proper slogan after saying something true. The question is what your policy does to the stranger standing before you.

Christian-right rhetoric performs a precise inversion here. It invokes Romans 13 and the language of order, law, and national protection, then detaches that language from the command to welcome the stranger and uses it to sanctify mass deportation as obedience to God rather than coercion against families. A government can call its violence lawful; it cannot make cruelty holy by placing a Bible verse beside it.

Those of us who live in this country have helped build the conditions that make this spectacle possible. We have accepted the labor of immigrants while demanding that their presence remain precarious. We have allowed administrations of both parties to expand detention, deportation, and family separation, then acted surprised when the machine produces cruelty. The machine is older than Donald Trump. Trump has simply given it a louder voice and fewer restraints.

Mullin’s choice of Lance Schroyer to lead ICE makes the same lesson plain. The nominee led Mullin’s Senate security detail, while rank-and-file ICE officials objected to his lack of federal or immigration experience and Tom Homan opposed the nomination. The Senate has not scheduled a confirmation hearing. The question is clear: will immigration enforcement answer to professional competence, lawful process, and human dignity, or to personal loyalty?

You can still choose another way. Protection can replace targeting. Family unity can replace administrative separation. A real path to lawful status can replace permanent precarity for long-resident neighbors. Employer accountability can replace a system that makes migrants bear the costs of employers’ dependence on vulnerable labor. A humane immigration policy would enforce the law without making cruelty the measure of success. It would treat borders as instruments of public responsibility, not walls around the human family.

Óscar Romero cried, “Stop the repression!” He spoke to soldiers as people capable of recovering their conscience. That door remains open to the officer, the secretary, and the president.

But the door is not an alibi. Until you stop turning strangers into targets and arrests into trophies, you are not protecting the country. You are teaching it to look away.
