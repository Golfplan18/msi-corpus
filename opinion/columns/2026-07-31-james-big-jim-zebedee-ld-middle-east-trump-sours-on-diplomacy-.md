The Trump administration is killing people in Iran and calling it a negotiation. When that does not work, it blames the other side for being unreasonable.

Six months into a war the administration told us would be quick and clean, the president held a televised cabinet meeting at Camp David to announce he plans to resume heavy military strikes — a strategy he was supposedly already running before he stepped back for "very friendly negotiations" that collapsed inside a week. "Iran says one thing and does another," Trump complained, as though the pattern of American ultimatums followed by American airstrikes followed by Iranian retaliation followed by American surprise at the retaliation were some kind of puzzle that just needs a sharper man in the room to solve.

I run an auto shop. When a customer brings me a truck that has been overheating and I replace the radiator, and the truck overheats again, I do not conclude the truck is crazy. I go looking for the cracked head gasket I missed. The administration has been replacing radiators for six months and the engine is still running hot, and its diagnosis is that the truck is dishonorable.

Now, the strategic problem here is not subtle. Thomas Schelling drew the distinction sixty years ago in *Arms and Influence*: deterrence works because the adversary can choose not to act and save face, but compellence — forcing someone to reverse course, to stop doing something they are already doing — requires the adversary to visibly capitulate under pressure. That means compliance looks like defeat. No regime with a functioning domestic politics absorbs that quietly, and the Islamic Republic, whatever its internal fractures, has functioning domestic politics. Trump is asking Iran to surrender its nuclear program and its position on the Strait of Hormuz because the bombs are painful. Schelling would have told him at the outset what any honest mechanic could have told him: pain and surrender are not the same thing.

The war is now in its sixth month. The June memorandum of understanding fell apart within weeks. The pattern since has been strike, ceasefire talk, breakdown, escalation, repeat. That is not a ladder toward resolution. That is what happens when a man keeps ordering the same wrong part and then cussing the truck for not running right. Every operation that was supposed to produce a negotiating position has produced instead a new grievance on the other side, a new domestic constituency against compromise, and a new reason for Iran's hardliners to argue that Washington was never negotiating in good faith. The American president told aides the Iranians are "crazy" and that the interim deal was "meaningless." The Iranian diplomats, for their parts, tell mediators there is no trust left and that relations worsen with every military threat. Two sides that each regard the other as fundamentally dishonest do not produce a settlement. They produce a war of attrition dressed up as a strategy, and the parts bill keeps growing.

Schelling's own framework identifies the core difficulty: compellence works only when the coercer has a clear endpoint the target can meet, a credible commitment to stop if compliance occurs, and enough patience to outlast the target's will to endure. Trump has offered no clear endpoint. "Winning" is the vocabulary, not a defined set of conditions Iran could actually satisfy. His military advisers counter with an inconvenient fact: the munitions are dwindling. We have been hitting them for weeks and the stockpile has a floor. That is what the administration of a country that spends more on defense than the next ten nations combined does not want you to know. The bombs are running out before the war aim is defined. There is no stockpile large enough to break a regime's will from the air, and the Joint Staff knows it. That is what "dwindling munitions" means in the polite reporting language of the national press. In the language of a shop that has run through its parts inventory without diagnosing the problem: we are using ordnance faster than we can build or buy it, against a target set that can replace its launchers faster than we can destroy them.

The president's theory of the case appears to be that if he hits them "very hard," they will eventually say, "We just can't take it anymore." That is not a strategy. That is a feeling performing as policy — a man slamming the hood because the engine will not turn over, and calling that a repair plan. It has no measurable progress toward any stated objective, no diplomatic off-ramp, and no timeline beyond the president's own frustration. Eisenhower, in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, warned that the military-industrial complex's "potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist." He did not warn that the misplaced power would also run out of precision-guided munitions halfway through the third week and have to admit it in a newspaper. Every logistics officer since Napoleon has known the corollary: a campaign without a sustainable supply line is a campaign with an expiration date, and the adversary knows it too. Iran's strategists are, by the reporting, banking on exactly this, calculating that domestic political opposition will force the president to abandon the effort before they must abandon their positions. That is not paranoia. That is the textbook American pattern. It is the pattern from Vietnam through the 2003 invasion's aftermath, the one Bacevich named in *Washington Rules*: the conviction that sufficient force will produce a political outcome, applied again and again against adversaries who understood the timeline better than Washington did. I have seen a man in my shop spend four thousand dollars chasing a two-hundred-dollar problem because he was sure the next part would fix it. This is that, at a national scale, with warheads instead of brake pads.

The factional picture inside Iran makes this worse, not easier. Some Iranian officials want to negotiate. Others want to prolong the conflict, viewing American entanglement as a gift. Escalation hands the second faction exactly what they want. Every strike that kills IRGC personnel or damages infrastructure gives the hardliners the evidence they need to argue that the Americans were never serious about peace, and it gives the negotiating faction less room to maneuver. Walzer's framework on proportionality and discrimination applies here not as abstract moral philosophy but as operational reality: strikes that are disproportionate to their military objective, or that produce civilian casualties the Iranian government can publicize, convert whatever remains of Iranian public tolerance for negotiation into Iranian public demand for retaliation. The IRGC's vow to renew attacks is not a surprise. It is the predictable output of a predictable input. If I put the wrong fluid in a customer's transmission and the transmission fails, I do not get to call it a surprise. The system responded the way the system responds.

The administration has spent six months cycling through escalation, negotiation, frustration, and re-escalation as if each iteration were a fresh discovery. The Gulf allies, who live close enough to the Iranian missile batteries to hear them, have reportedly begun asking for more interceptors and clearer guarantees that the United States has a plan. They are not getting one. American diplomats, regional officials say, have offered "no fresh compromises." The Iranian side has expressed openness to another meeting in Geneva, Doha, or Islamabad. Washington's answer appears to be another bombing campaign, then another set of surprised feelings when the regime responds, then another cabinet meeting at which the president calls them crazy. Some Emirati and Kuwaiti officials are suggesting the IRGC will not negotiate until the United States takes control of the Strait of Hormuz or considers ground operations. That last suggestion should stop every serious person cold. The United States does not have a ground-operation option against Iran that does not produce the very quagmire the Iranian hardliners are trying to engineer. The regime has read the history of the post-9/11 wars. It knows the United States has two gears — full invasion and disengagement — and currently occupies neither. It can wait. We cannot, or at least the munitions stockpile cannot, and the people whose sons and daughters would fill that ground operation certainly should not have to.

The domestic politics Iran is banking on are real. Congressional opposition to extended operations is growing. The war's cost is no longer abstract. The veteran cohort that served in the post-9/11 campaigns knows what a Middle Eastern war that was supposed to be short looks like when it is not short, and that cohort's families know it too. Phil Klay, in *Redeployment*, wrote about the dissonance between the strategic vocabulary that justifies a war and the lived experience of the people who fight it. That dissonance is now a political fact, and it does not care about the president's television performance at Camp David. The men and women who will carry this war's weight have heard "just a little longer" before. They know what it sounds like. It sounds like a shop owner telling a mechanic to keep swapping parts until the problem goes away, except the mechanic is the one bleeding knuckles and the shop owner is the one on television.

What Trump is doing, stripped of the cabinet-meeting production, is running a coercive diplomatic campaign on the assumption that enough violence will crack the other side's will before the coercer's own resources and patience run out. The historical record on that assumption is not ambiguous. Schelling himself noted that the most dangerous form of compellence is the one where the coercer's credibility erodes with each threatened strike that fails to produce compliance, because each unfulfilled threat makes the next one less believable. The interim deal was supposed to be the proof of concept. It collapsed. The two-week intensive campaign is the next proof of concept. Dwindling munitions suggest it may be the last one available at this intensity.

The honest assessment is this: Trump is not running out of patience because diplomacy is failing. He is running into the structural limit of what military coercion can produce against an adversary with sufficient will, sufficient territory, and sufficient time. The Iranians are not "crazy." They are calculating, and their calculation is rational within the framework Schelling described. When you tell a regime it must surrender its strategic position or face destruction, and you demonstrate that you lack the munitions to sustain the pressure, you have taught it precisely the lesson you did not intend. You have taught it to hold on.

What remains is the cold ledger. The Iran war has already killed people whose names will never appear in a news report. It has consumed munitions whose replacement cost will appear in a supplemental appropriations request some future Congress will either fund or refuse. It has generated no measurable progress toward the stated objective — not the nuclear program, which Iran has not weaponized; not the Strait of Hormuz, through which the world's oil still moves. It has produced a president who tells reporters his negotiating partners are "dishonorable" while his own military advisers flag that the bombs are running low. That is not a war aim. That is a mood. And moods are not a basis on which to send men and women to kill and die. I know what it costs to run a shop on frustration instead of a plan. The difference is that when I do it, the worst that happens is a truck sits in the bay an extra day. When the president does it, people who will never sit in a cabinet meeting pay for it with their lives.

Clausewitz wrote that war is the continuation of politics by other means. What is happening now is the reverse: politics has become the continuation of war, and neither side has a politics that can end the war it has started. That is not strategy. That is a trap. And the men who will pay for it are the ones who always pay for it — the ones whose names are not in the cabinet meeting transcript, whose service records are not on the press secretary's desk, and whose families will sit in a living room somewhere in this country waiting for a car that is not coming home.
