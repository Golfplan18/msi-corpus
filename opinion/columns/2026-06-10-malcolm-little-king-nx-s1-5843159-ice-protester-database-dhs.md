In January, Xenia Pantos pulled over in Portland, Maine, sat in their car ten feet from masked federal agents, and observed. They did not block a road. They did not confront an officer. They documented the operation. Hours later, their spouse, Carly Williams, answered a blocked number. The caller identified himself as Department of Homeland Security and told Williams, with the casual menace of an unaccountable bureaucrat, that her spouse should stop observing or risk being “added to a domestic terrorist watch list.” Williams recounted the call to NPR: “That was a pretty terrifying phone call to receive, as you can imagine.” When the couple crossed back into the U.S. from a weekend in Quebec months later, a Customs and Border Protection officer asked Williams—who wasn’t even driving—to describe her car and recite her license plate number. They realized then that the data hadn’t been casually noted and discarded. It had been entered into a system, tagged to their names, and was popping up on the screen of every border agent who scanned their passports. The threat was not hypothetical. It was operational.

The chilling effect is not an unintended side effect of the surveillance apparatus. It is the design principle. DHS and ICE operate on a premise that requires total information dominance over the communities they police. If a community organizes an observer program, the state’s response is not to enforce lawful limits on that program; the state’s response is to mark the observers, link their license plates to their identities, and store the data in systems that trigger downstream flags at the border and in daily life. The goal is to make the cost of observing federal operations so high that ordinary people stop doing it. The apparatus achieves what a direct ban would cost too much political capital to enact, and it does so by manufacturing a shadow infrastructure of terror tags and data retention.

Acting ICE director Todd Lyons wrote to Congress in April denying the existence of a “protester database,” and DHS issues the same boilerplate press statement to every outlet that asks: *There is NO database of ‘domestic terrorists’ run by DHS.* The newly disclosed letter from Lyons, obtained by NPR, admits that ICE collects “essential biographic and biometric information and situational details” on anyone its agents even suspect of interfering with operations. The agency maintains records on people who were never arrested, never charged, never found to have violated any law. It just doesn’t store them in a “separate, standalone database.” They’re folded into existing data systems, which the department can access with a keystroke.

This is the classic motte-and-bailey pattern. The agency advances the strong claim of total surveillance authority; when Congress asks about a database, it retreats to the easily defensible motte that no “standalone” database of domestic terrorists exists, then resumes the strong claim in its operational posture by keeping the data alive in CBP systems and ICE encounter logs. The motte-and-bailey is a structural lie. It tells the public what the agency wants to be true while operating on what the agency knows is true. The denial is a semantic trick—the administration’s signature move of parsing words while building the machinery it claims doesn’t exist.

Let’s name the bad-faith technique at work. This is *pre-emptive legitimacy-withdrawal*: before any specific violation can be proven, the administration has delegitimized the act of observing as inherently suspect—“doxxing,” a “threat to officer safety,” potential “interference.” The rhetorical frame ensures that any subsequent surveillance is framed not as political suppression but as officer protection. It is the same logic by which a regime declares the press an enemy of the state and then claims every investigation is a matter of national security. And when pressed, the administration shifts the goalposts: from “no database” to “no *standalone* database,” from “we don’t surveil protesters” to “we only collect information on those who may have violated the law”—a standard that Lyons’ own letter reveals is as elastic as any agent’s suspicion.

The receipts are everywhere. In Minneapolis, federal agents were explicitly instructed to collect license plates, IDs, and images of protesters. Observers in several states—Minnesota and Tennessee among them—complained that agents photographed their faces and license plates, ran the plates through vehicle registration records, determined identities and addresses. A number of observers have reported having their Global Entry status revoked after interacting with federal immigration officials. Across the country, the pattern is identical: agents photograph faces and plates, run them through facial recognition and vehicle databases, determine identities and addresses, and then—quietly, with the plausible deniability of a blocked number—let the target know they’ve been marked. The goal is to make watching feel unsafe.

The root cause is an enforcement paradigm that equates the act of recording with the act of obstructing, and the act of observing with the act of threatening. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin testified last week about using facial recognition on people gathered outside Delaney Hall, declaring zero tolerance for verbal assault and vehicle damage. The declaration is true in the narrow sense: assault and damage are felonies. But DHS and ICE have systematically broadened their definition of the criminal category until First Amendment conduct sits inside the perimeter. As ACLU senior staff attorney Scarlet Kim documented in litigation over Minneapolis and Memphis observer programs, DHS leadership explicitly equates video recording, information-gathering about federal agents, and public posting of that footage with potential crimes against officer safety. This is a deliberate frame-engineered relabeling: protective rights are recast as hostile acts. When the agency’s definition of “potential violation” includes the core of the First Amendment, every observer is a domestic terrorist in waiting, and the apparatus gets to treat the chilling threat as routine risk management.

The Department of Homeland Security has built its own Imperial Security Bureau—compiling files on citizens not because they’ve committed a crime but because they might one day be inconvenient. Cassian Andor identified the tell: power that is truly confident doesn’t need to panic. “Power doesn’t panic,” he said. But the administration’s surveillance blitz is a panic—a frantic attempt to ensure no one is watching while it conducts the mass deportations it promised, to silence the kind of documentation that has already mapped enforcement patterns in Tucson and borne witness to the real consequences of this enforcement. The surveillance apparatus is the exhaust port: the regime’s vulnerability is the truth that it cannot operate cleanly in the light.

“With the first link, the chain is forged,” Admiral Satie warned in *Star Trek: The Next Generation*’s “The Drumhead,” the franchise’s clearest depiction of how a security state devours civil liberties. “The first speech censored, the first thought forbidden, the first freedom denied, chains us all irrevocably.” The DHS argument that recording agents is “doxxing” is the first link. The phone call threatening a terrorist watch list is the second. The quiet revocation of trusted-traveler status is the third. And the chain is being forged in plain sight, link by link, while the department’s lawyers draft press releases denying the blacksmith exists.

The benefit line on this apparatus lands on the enforcement agencies themselves. ICE and DHS benefit from the chilling of observation because it reduces the public evidentiary record of their operations, which reduces the political capital cost of aggressive enforcement tactics. The broader administration benefits: it sends a message to every progressive organization, every legal observer group, every concerned citizen that showing up to document state violence carries a personal cost—a note in a government file, a loss of travel privileges, a menacing phone call. There are no second-order beneficiaries in the citizenry. There are only diffuse cost-bearers: every person who decides not to stop and watch; every family that crosses a border and encounters the flag; every community that learns that observing federal agents requires a lawyer on retainer. The public framing is officer safety. The distributional impact is the quiet erosion of the First Amendment’s checking function.

Martin Luther King Jr. taught us that the chief obstacle to justice is not the overt extremist but the moderate who accepts the absence of tension as peace. The chilling effect is that moderate’s peace. It is purchased at the cost of the community’s capacity to witness its own policing.

The constitutional discipline required here is not a new oversight committee. It is the public refusal of the chilling effect. The watchers are counting on the observer’s fear that the flag will follow them to the border, to their job, to their family. The arc of justice bends toward accountability only when specific people, in specific moments, refuse to stop watching. The apparatus can flag the license plate. It can run the facial recognition. It can keep the record in a system that lives forever. What it cannot do, without the community’s complicity, is make the observer look away. We name the surveillance posture as surveillance posture. We name the terror tag as a bureaucratic weapon against the First Amendment. We keep the receipts. By any means necessary—the means being the analytical and political instruments available to us—we do not stop naming until the surveillance stops.

The people on that list are not terrorists. They are Americans who watched. We will not forget their names, even if the government will not tell us where they are stored. We keep watching.
