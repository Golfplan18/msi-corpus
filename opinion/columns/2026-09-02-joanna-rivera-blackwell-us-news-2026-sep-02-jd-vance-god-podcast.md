JD Vance has made a felt sense of spiritual evil an instrument of U.S. foreign policy.

On a podcast hosted by a 22-year-old evangelical YouTuber with more than a million subscribers, the sitting Vice President of the United States told his audience that his body registers "every spiritual alarm bell" in meetings with foreign leaders. He said he would not be shocked if the Antichrist were walking among us. He said he is at peace if the work God has given him hastens the end of the world. He brought these remarks to The Bryce Crawford Podcast — a forum that exists to confirm the convictions of its viewers, not to test them — and he addressed them to a host whose YouTube thumbnails include "Debating a 'gay' pastor" and "Preaching the gospel in Chicago's deadliest neighborhood."

The question is not whether a private American may hold apocalyptic convictions. The First Amendment protects that right fully. The question is what a sitting Vice President does when he installs those convictions as an official epistemology in rooms where the United States negotiates treaties and weighs sanctions. That is an Establishment Clause question, not a theological one. The First Amendment's bar on religious tests behind public acts applies when the official who claims the test is the one who sits in those rooms.

By Vance's own account, his office has been operating under a felt-sense-of-evil filter for some time. He did not announce this as a private devotional posture. He announced it as the body's response to meetings where the United States makes consequential decisions. When the alarm bells ring, what does an office carrying the constitutional duty to advise on foreign policy, to participate in Senate deliberations on treaties, and to stand first in the line of presidential succession do? Register suspicion? Soften commitment? Walk away? Vance has not said, and his viewers are not asking.

A claim of that kind imposes a religious test on the exercise of public authority. The Office of Government Ethics requires executive-branch officials to act on the basis of law and the public interest, not on private religious conviction. The Hatch Act bars officials from using their office for partisan or sectarian purposes. The Establishment Clause bars the government from placing religious authority behind official acts. Vance has not merely expressed religious belief. He has placed religious discernment between himself and the duties of his office, and he has done so on a forum with a million-strong evangelical audience that now believes the Vice President of the United States reads diplomacy through a spiritual alarm system.

Three classes of persons are placed at risk by this claim.

First, the diplomatic counterparts of the United States. Vance has been a central figure in negotiations with Iran, with European allies over Ukraine, and with other foreign governments. When he sits across from an Iranian negotiator whose spiritual standing his body has pronounced upon, he is no longer negotiating — he is witnessing. The Iranian counterpart has been reduced from a policy actor to a soul whose posture Vance's office has been trained to read. The same applies to any counterpart whose presence trips the alarm. Their standing in the room has been reduced to Vance's discernment. The United States loses the capacity to act on its interests when its officer acts on his chest.

Second, Iranian Americans, Jewish Americans, and the broader class of non-Evangelical Americans whose place in Vance's apocalyptic register is, by historical pattern, the default identity of the unnamed Antichrist. The Antichrist has been variously identified with Jewish leadership, with Catholic clergy, and with secular heads of state across two thousand years of Christian commentary. Vance has not named the figure. He has named the office's commitment to recognizing him. His viewers will. The unnamed Antichrist becomes, in the hands of his audience, whichever non-Evangelical community is most available to suspicion at the moment — and a million subscribers with a felt-sense-of-evil epistemology already installed by the Vice President do not require further instruction.

Third, the career civil servants in the State Department, the National Security Council, and the intelligence community whose policy memoranda are reviewed by an official whose framework is spiritual discernment rather than analytic expertise. Those officers are not Vance's congregation. They are his colleagues. They have taken oaths to advise on the basis of the Constitution, the law, and the national interest. Vance's alarm-bell claim puts them in the position of either concurring in a framework their oaths forbid, or registering dissent against an office that has now publicly declared it does not share their framework. The professional civil service cannot function when the principal it serves has privatized the criteria of judgment.

The remedy is not complicated. Vance must formally recuse from foreign-policy deliberations in which he intends to invoke spiritual discernment as the basis of his judgment. The White House Counsel and the Office of Government Ethics should issue guidance clarifying that executive-branch advisory judgments must rest on policy analysis, law, and the public interest — not on the officer's perception of spiritual evil. And the Vice President owes the country a retraction of the alarm-bell claim as a description of how his office works. He is free to believe what he believes. He is not free to make his belief an instrument of the office.

The Bible does not need to settle this. But the Bible, read plainly, does not leave Vance without a witness against his own claim. When a man in the crowd asked Jesus to arbitrate an inheritance, Jesus refused: "Man, who made me a judge or divider over you?" (Luke 12:14). The same Jesus, when the officers came to take him in the garden, told Pilate, "My kingdom is not of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would fight" (John 18:36). The chasm between the Bible's plain language and Vance's reading is the chasm between an office that declines to be a court of discernment and an office that has made itself one.

I will not supply the name Vance withheld. But the doctrine he has installed requires him to be the judge. A Vice President who runs diplomacy on a felt sense of evil is no longer an officer of the Constitution. He is a pastor who has wandered into the Situation Room.
