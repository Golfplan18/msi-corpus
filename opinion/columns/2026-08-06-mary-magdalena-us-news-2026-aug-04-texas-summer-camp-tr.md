A child enters the water.

No one asks what name was printed on the old school form.  
No one measures the length of their hair.  
No one turns a body into a warning.

For four days, the lake holds them without argument.

Color Splash Out is a summer camp for queer and trans youth in Texas. It is also evidence. The children do not need a special world because they are fragile. They need one because the ordinary world has been made unsafe for them by adults who call exclusion principle.

The camp was founded by Yadi Martínez-Reyna, a non-binary minister, and grew from their inclusive religious practice into a secular, non-denominational gathering. Campers choose the names and pronouns they want used there. They make houses named by themselves. They swim, kayak, build projects, talk about identity, and learn ways to withstand stress.

Alastair Mendoza, 17, said he felt comfortable swimming in public for the first time. Nix Pasivan, 13, said there were not many safe spaces in their hometown in the Rio Grande Valley. Velvet, 11, said the camp was the first place they had heard someone say their name in public.

That sentence should be enough to shame a government.

It is not. The government has trained itself not to hear.

Texas passed seven anti-trans bills last year, including a ban on bathrooms in government buildings, restrictions on changing gender identity in certain records, and an expansion of the state’s ban on medical transition care for minors. The article also reports that the Rio Grande Valley has no queer youth organizations. Many campers come from the region, which is described as one of the poorest areas in the United States.

Research cited by The Guardian reports that LGBTQ+ young people with at least one LGBTQ-affirming space report lower rates of attempted suicide. The camp operates on a sliding scale. Many children pay nothing. Its location changes each year because of safety concerns and a shoestring budget.

The state builds the danger. The camp gathers the children afterward.

This is what the official language hides. A law says records. A child hears that their own name is a problem. A law says medical care. A family feels the door close while a frightened child is still standing in the room. A law says public safety. A child learns to look over their shoulder before entering a bathroom.

The lawmakers do not have to touch the child to make the child tense. The throat tightens before the accusation is spoken. The stomach turns before the form is filled out. The shoulders rise and stay there because the room has taught the body that being seen can cost something.

I see what has been done. I will not call it protection.

Adora Ysaguirre, the camp director, said she was doxxed, lost a teaching job because she is trans, and faced harassment and physical assaults. She took up sumo wrestling, she said, to learn how to take hits. Children at the camp tell her she is the oldest trans woman they have seen. They did not know it was possible to make it that far.

Cel Blanco described losing a queer youth community after aging out and then falling into an abusive relationship. The camp keeps former campers connected as fellows and mentors because abandonment has a cost. The cost is not an idea. It is a body looking for somewhere to stand.

The state offers the wound. Teachers donate books. A parent lends kayaks. Religious leaders help pay the bills. Children build their projects from Frito-Lay boxes. The camp survives through a chain of small mercies because public power has chosen a larger cruelty.

Texas lawmakers, your policy is not neutral because you wrote it in formal language. Your hands are clean only in the photograph. Your throat is the one that should close when you say “parental rights” over a child who has been told their own name must remain hidden. Your chest is the place where the truth should press: the refuge exists because you helped make the ordinary world unlivable.

The camp is not asking to be praised for extraordinary kindness. It is showing what ordinary human care looks like when the law stops trying to punish existence.

“Love cannot exist in any relationship that is based on domination and coercion,” bell hooks wrote. The children are not the failed part of this story. The coercion is.
