The thing producing Michigan’s “socialist surge” isn’t a fever. It’s an economy that stopped working for the people it is supposed to serve — and thirty million dollars trying to keep them from saying so.

In [Liz Peek’s Fox News opinion column](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/liz-peek-michigan-stop-socialist-fever-rage-watch-swallow-senate), “Michigan can stop the socialist fever rage — or watch it swallow the Senate,” she warns that Abdul El-Sayed’s platform — abolishing ICE, a wealth tax, tuition-free college, and Medicare for All — is a socialist contagion that Michigan voters must contain before it consumes the Democratic Party and the country. The piece frames the primary as a choice between “normal” and “crazy,” then urges Michigan to choose normal.

I’ll start with what Peek gets right. El-Sayed’s platform is genuinely far to the left of where Michigan voters have historically landed. His résumé is thinner than Haley Stevens’s, and the political risk in a purple state is real. RealClearPolling averages show Stevens leading former Republican Rep. Mike Rogers in a potential November matchup while El-Sayed trails him. That is a real gap. It matters.

But Peek is not making an electability argument. She is making a civilizational one. And the civilizational argument requires a diagnosis she never provides.

The piece calls El-Sayed’s platform “socialism” and treats the label as the explanation. It isn’t. It is a scare word doing the work of an argument. The usual incantation. The catechism of the comfortable.

Michigan is an auto state. Its workers watched productivity rise while pay stopped keeping up. They watched the manufacturing base erode for four decades. They watched healthcare premiums eat the raises their contracts did not deliver. They watched their children leave college with debt and return to towns where the jobs that degree was supposed to buy had not materialized. The “normal” Peek wants to preserve is normal for people whose portfolio returned twelve percent last year. It is not normal for the worker whose plant moved to Monterrey in 2016 and whose replacement job pays fifteen dollars an hour without benefits.

For that worker, a candidate promising healthcare and affordable college is not offering an exotic ideology. He is offering the floor the old arrangement took away.

The patient is not healthy.

Now add the money. AIPAC and allied groups poured roughly $30 million into a single Democratic primary, according to federal filings — the most expensive in the party’s history. Thirty million dollars. In a primary. For one Senate seat.

Not to explain a policy. Not to answer a specific argument. To make sure one candidate wins and another loses because the winner is expected to vote a certain way on questions that have nothing to do with Michigan’s roads, schools, or hospital budgets.

Read that number again.

That is not democracy responding to ideas. It is concentrated money overriding a Democratic electorate. In the old-fashioned sense, it is the most socialist thing in this race: a small group capturing machinery that is supposed to belong to everybody else.

The polls say what they say. El-Sayed is ahead in the primary. Voters appear to want what he is offering. That should be the starting point for understanding why, not a problem to be solved by bigger outside spending.

The label is a decoy because the policies themselves are not mysterious. Every rich democracy subsidizes childcare. Every rich democracy guarantees some version of universal health coverage. Denmark treats a year of paid parental leave the way Americans treat the interstate: boring, useful, and theirs. The Norwegian sovereign wealth fund, built on oil revenue and invested for the future, owns a slice of major public companies around the world. The Mondragon cooperatives in the Basque Country employ more than 70,000 people, conduct more than €11 billion in business, and set top executive pay at roughly five or six times what the lowest-paid worker earns.

None of this is the gulag. None of it is a command economy. It is capitalism with the trap doors welded shut.

The 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit gives us an American receipt. Child poverty fell from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent in one year — a 46 percent decline — and 2.9 million children were lifted out of poverty. Then the expansion lapsed, and the gains reversed. We ran the experiment on ourselves. It worked. Then we turned it off.

That is not socialism. That is plumbing.

A 2019 study in the *American Journal of Public Health* found that medical bills contributed to nearly 60 percent of personal bankruptcies. Families are not going broke because they have read too much Marx. They are going broke because a hospital bill can arrive with the force of a foreclosure notice.

The status quo is producing the evidence.

Peek dismisses the United Auto Workers’ endorsement of El-Sayed as proof that the union has been “hijacked by leftist elements.” Perhaps it is evidence that the union’s members looked at their paychecks and healthcare bills and decided the old arrangement was not delivering. The UAW endorsed because its members asked it to. Its members are asking because the machine stopped working.

The “hijacked” framing is the tell. When workers organize in their own interest and inconvenience donors, the organizing gets called an infiltration.

The UAW now includes 100,000 higher-education workers. Those workers are not a foreign body inserted into the union. They are workers. They have wages, tuition bills, healthcare costs, and employers. A union representing them is doing its job when it asks what an economy is for.

Peek also calls El-Sayed’s résumé “paper-thin.” It is not. He ran the Detroit Health Department from 2015 to 2017, in the years after the city emerged from bankruptcy, with a real budget and a real bureaucracy to fight. He later ran Wayne County’s health services. Peek reaches for one fact-checker’s complaint about a county juvenile facility and never engages the public-health work itself.

Because engaging the work would mean engaging the policy.

And the policy is less frightening once you remove the costume.

Here is the strongest version of Peek’s case: perhaps Stevens is the safer general-election candidate. Perhaps a centrist has a better chance of holding the seat. RealClearPolling may support that view. Fine. That is an argument about electoral strategy. It is not an argument that Michigan voters are feverish. It is not an argument that their demands are irrational. And it is not a reason to let thirty million dollars decide which argument the party is permitted to hear.

So what would a Michigan Senate race look like if it were actually about Michigan?

It would ask whether families receive the child-care support every Nordic country provides. It would ask whether working people can bargain across entire industries instead of firm by firm, while the nonunion shop down the road undercuts them. It would ask whether a public option can give families a real choice instead of forcing them to accept whatever insurance their employer offers or nothing. It would ask whether worker cooperatives can keep profits in Michigan towns rather than shipping them to distant shareholders. It would ask whether a senator answers to Michigan voters or to a spending account.

Sectoral wage boards could set a pay floor across an industry — fast-food and hotel workers negotiating with employers at one table instead of each franchise racing to the bottom. Australia and New Zealand already do versions of this. A public health option could compete with private insurers. Worker ownership could keep the gains of production where the work happens.

These are not utopian asks. They are the plumbing that other wealthy democracies installed while we were busy calling the pipes subversive.

The economy stopped producing broadly shared prosperity. Voters noticed. The “socialist fever” panic exists to make sure they never ask why.

The question is not whether Michigan voters are reaching for the wrong label. The question is whether the rest of us will fix the machine — or keep calling the symptom a disease.
