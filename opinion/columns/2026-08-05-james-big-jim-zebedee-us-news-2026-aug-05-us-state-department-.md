Trump and Rubio are dismantling American diplomacy and handing China the field.

A consulate is not a polished storefront for diplomats. It is a working office where trade gets handled, visas get processed, local officials get known, and American workers gain somebody on the other end of the phone when a deal goes bad. It is where a government learns the difference between a country as described in a briefing book and a country as it actually lives.

The State Department plans to close missions in St. George’s, Grenada; Nagoya, Japan; Medan, Indonesia; Douala, Cameroon; and Winnipeg, Canada. The department calls the closures cost-cutting and efficiency. That is the language of a balance sheet being used to justify a strategic retreat.

The bill will not be paid only by diplomats. It will be paid by the people who load the trucks, work the plants, run the farms, staff the small businesses, and depend on cross-border trade for a paycheck. A tariff hits at the loading dock and then moves through the warehouse, the supplier, the payroll, and the family budget. When the government closes the office that understands a region's trade and local relationships, the people left to absorb the confusion are not the men who wrote the spreadsheet. They are the workers standing beside the machine when the order disappears.

The Winnipeg mission opened in 2001 and handled trade and investment issues mainly involving Manitoba. The administration is now pressuring Canada with 50% tariffs on a range of goods while closing an office that helped American officials understand that province and its economy. That does not pencil out as defensible foreign policy. It does not pencil out for the workers on either side of the border.

Brad Kirbyson, who worked at the Winnipeg consulate for 20 years, warned that closing it would mean losing touch with a broad swath of Canada and leave an excessive distance between the remaining American consulates in Calgary and Toronto. That is what a diplomatic closure looks like when you take the cable-news varnish off it. A man who spent two decades building relationships is told to pack up. A region that had a nearby American point of contact is told to drive farther. The distance is physical before it becomes political.

The same is true elsewhere. The mission in Douala facilitates trade for American firms in central Africa. The post in Nagoya connects the United States to one of Japan's major industrial regions. The mission in Medan keeps contact with a large and consequential part of Indonesia. The American presence in St. George’s carries full embassy status. These are not decorative rooms. They are tools used by ordinary people trying to move goods, secure permits, resolve disputes, hire workers, and keep a business alive.

Close the office and the work does not go on standby. It disappears. The relationship goes cold. The unanswered call sits there.

The larger numbers show that this is not five isolated closures. The department fired more than 1,300 employees last year, including 246 foreign service officers and 1,107 civil servants. More than half of the United States’ 195 ambassadorial posts remain unfilled, including posts in Germany, Cuba, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan. The White House Office of Management and Budget has argued for closing as many as 30 foreign missions. USAID, the main overseas development body, has already been drastically reduced and folded into the State Department.

The five announced closures are the first tranche of a program the administration wanted to be much larger.

The administration says great-power competition with China is the organizing principle of American foreign policy. The 2017 National Security Strategy said so. The 2018 National Defense Strategy said so. Every administration since has kept the vocabulary. But competition is not a word you repeat at a podium. It is a choice about where you show up, who you know, what you understand, and whether people in another country have a reason to answer your call.

China already maintains a diplomatic presence in three of the five locations. It is present in St. George’s, Nagoya, and Medan. It does not have a mission in Winnipeg, which makes Winnipeg even more revealing. The retreat is not limited to places where Beijing is already present. The United States is also walking away from places China never had to contest.

Influence is built through repeated contact, commercial ties, scholarships, port relationships, local knowledge, and the patient accumulation of trust. It is not built only by aircraft carriers. A navy ship can sit in port. A consulate can spend years learning which local official can solve a problem, which business dispute is about to become a political dispute, and which community is tired of being treated as a line on somebody else's map.

That work is quiet. It does not generate cable-news segments. It is also the work that keeps a tariff fight from becoming a permanent break, keeps a trade relationship from becoming a lost contract, and keeps a local disagreement from becoming a national insult.

Andrew Bacevich’s *Washington Rules* describes the permanent-security habit that treats military presence as the default answer to every foreign problem. The Trump administration is not abandoning that habit. It is cutting away the civilian machinery that keeps it from becoming the only habit left, the presence that identifies interests, builds agreements, and gives a president alternatives that do not end in force. It maintains a military establishment funded at roughly the $900 billion level in the FY2026 defense authorization framework, even as broader national-defense totals move above that figure, while hollowing out the civilian work that makes military power less necessary.

A country that keeps closing the offices where it listens should not be surprised when it starts speaking only in threats.

Clausewitz wrote in *On War*, Book VIII, that war is an instrument of policy. The point was not that armies are the whole of foreign policy. The point was that force has to serve a political purpose. Diplomacy is how a government learns what political purpose is possible before it sends soldiers, imposes sanctions, threatens an ally, or stumbles into a war it did not know how to end.

Sun Tzu understood the same thing from another direction. The supreme art is to subdue the enemy without fighting. That requires knowledge, patience, relationships, and alternatives. It requires more than a weapons budget. When an administration dismantles the offices that build those conditions, it is not making American power sharper. It is making American power blinder.

The State Department spokesperson says the department is focused on ensuring that its diplomatic footprint is efficient, effective, and delivers results for the American people. The record says the footprint is getting smaller, less staffed, less present, and less capable of doing the work the administration claims it wants done.

Senator Jeanne Shaheen, the top Democrat on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, asked whether the yearly operating costs of these facilities, a tiny fraction of the State Department’s budget, are worth pulling back from places where China is already there. The question is not merely what these posts cost. The question is what it costs to lose the relationships, local knowledge, trade access, and human contact that took years to build.

Eisenhower, in his farewell address on January 17, 1961, warned against the acquisition of unwarranted influence by the military-industrial complex. He also said that “only an alert and knowledgeable citizenry can compel the proper meshing of the huge industrial and military machinery of defense with our peaceful methods and goals, so that security and liberty may prosper together.”

The administration is executing the inverted version of Eisenhower’s warning. It is preserving the machinery of defense while dismantling the peaceful methods. It is asking workers and communities to trust a foreign policy that keeps the weapons and closes the doors.

Congress should reject these closures. It should require the administration to explain the strategic purpose of each one, restore a properly staffed diplomatic service, and treat consulates as national instruments rather than line items waiting to be cut.

A consulate is a door. A relationship is a bridge. A tariff fight, a trade dispute, or a local crisis does not become safer because Washington has fewer people on the ground.

Trump and Rubio are closing the offices that carry American interests into the world. The people left standing outside that empty office will not be the ones who approved the closure.
