Holman W. Jenkins Jr. launders the pandemic-was-overblown story through Fauci’s diary.

The artifact under the glass is his *Wall Street Journal* op-ed, “There’s Movie Potential in Dr. Fauci’s Diary,” published August 7, 2026. The operation is score-settling. The genre is the alibi. Jenkins concedes the vaccine’s “true utility,” then spends the rest of the machinery arguing that quarantines “did next to nothing,” contact tracing “wastes resources,” the press was in “moral panic,” and an 85-year-old official who pleaded the Fifth before a Senate committee deserves a shrug and a “Who needs it?”

I recognize the machine because I built versions of it in the cable years: the sympathetic profile as indictment, where the subject’s own words are arranged so the subject does the dirty work and the author keeps both hands clean. I am bitter about having built it. The reader does not need to credit the bitterness. The piece is on the page.

Let’s walk it.

> At 61, I was a prime subject for the Covid vaccine. It cut my risks by 90%—which sounds like a lot except it actually meant cutting my death risk from 1% to 0.1%. For many younger people, the vaccine could manage only to cut their risk from almost zero to almost zero, yet try finding an example of this being explained at the time.
>
> The vaccine had true utility but not for everybody, and soon ideology took over as it did with everything else related to Covid.

This is the relabel scam run in arithmetic — frame-engineered relabeling, [WSJ Catalogue](/propaganda/docs/wsj-technique-catalogue) §4.1, with the suppressed-variable engine underneath. The numbers are true. A 90% risk reduction for a 61-year-old can mean a reduction from 1% to 0.1%. That is exactly why the construction matters. “Which sounds like a lot except” makes the reader feel that the 90% was oversold, and “from almost zero to almost zero” finishes the job for anyone young enough to recognize himself in the sentence.

The number is true. The frame is a lie.

What disappears is what the benefit was for. The vaccine’s utility was never only the jabbed person’s individual death risk. It also concerned transmission, hospital load, and the odds that one person’s “almost zero” became a vulnerable person’s entirely real danger. The vaccine trial record on symptomatic Covid efficacy is absent. So are the columns that turned vaccination from a private purchase into a public-health intervention.

“Try finding an example of this being explained at the time” is a dare, not a finding. Assert the suppressed explanation and the audience supplies the memory of trust betrayed. This is how operators talk when they want a national intervention to die quietly: change the unit of account until the benefit disappears, then call the people who keep the old columns ideologues.

Watch the concession do its laundry. “True utility but not for everybody” sounds measured. By the last third of the column, it has shrunk the vaccine’s value enough to call quarantining “manic” and death-counting “obsessed.”

> The vilification of Anthony Fauci goes too far. His diary is exactly what you would want, recounting who he saw and what they said. It dwells on the conversations anybody would want dwelled on, like with President Trump and Chris Christie as each recovered from near-death Covid experiences.
>
> His much-criticized media attentiveness is a red herring. The important word in public health is public. The most un-Fauci-like moment? FU, he says with respect to a writer at the Atlantic.

This is the likeability shield — [WSJ Catalogue](/propaganda/docs/wsj-technique-catalogue) §4.18 in the working catalogues, and Bandura’s advantageous comparison in practical dress. The subject’s private words stand in for a character reference so the policy record can float free.

Two paragraphs establish that Fauci is charming. He keeps a diary “exactly what you would want,” sat with President Trump and Chris Christie as they recovered from near-death experiences, and once, gloriously, told an *Atlantic* writer FU. The reader is invited to like him. A liked man’s misjudgments become foibles. His public record stops being evidence.

And the humane vignettes are not accidental. Defend the man so the attack on the institution reads as fair criticism rather than score-settling. The sympathetic profile becomes the indictment. The diary is made to testify against the apparatus that produced it.

“The important word in public health is public” is a lovely sentence and an evasion. The criticism of Fauci’s media attentiveness was not that he talked to the press. It was that public messaging had to carry the weight of policy, uncertainty, and changing evidence. The FU anecdote supplies two paragraphs of argumentative work while attending to none of that.

There is no public-health substance here. Only the man, made fond.

> Washington’s manic quarantining of cruise-ship passengers, its restricting of foreign entry, probably did next to nothing to slow spread until a vaccine and the less-virulent omicron variants arrived, which took a year.
>
> But a problem jumps out of the Fauci diary. He and other officials zeroed in on, and became obsessed with trying to control, death- and case-count indicators while ignoring every other variable relevant to national well-being.

“Manic quarantining” is euphemistic labeling doing the work Albert Bandura catalogued: precautionary measures become a mood disorder. “Probably did next to nothing” is a counterfactual asserted without the modeling required to support it. The travel-restriction literature, which found that border measures could buy time in the early months before community transmission took hold, is not consulted because the column is not building an argument. It is assembling an attitude.

Then comes the timeline fudge. “Which took a year” makes it sound as though nothing changed until vaccination and Omicron arrived on a tidy schedule. The first U.S. vaccine authorization came in December 2020, within a year of the March 2020 emergency declaration. Omicron was identified in November 2021, much later than that smooth phrase permits. The padding turns “nothing worked until nature saved us” into a clean story.

The second paragraph is the same grievance wearing a diary citation. Officials were “obsessed with death- and case-count indicators.” Tracking deaths during a novel respiratory pandemic was not a bureaucratic tic. It was the job. By the time of the change in administration, more than four hundred thousand Americans had died. More than a million died in the first three years. “Every other variable relevant to national well-being” is code for reopening sooner, the position this column has defended by its own account.

The move is simple: blame the thermometer for the fever.

> This should have been Dr. Fauci’s moment of real leverage, advising how to protect the most vulnerable while meeting the need of the rest of America to get back to normal. Instead, he keeps lecturing young people to curtail their lives. Bizarrely, he insists on more contact tracing, as if oblivious to the fact that identified cases are a trivial proportion of the true pandemic. Contact tracing wastes resources while offering zero leverage over spread.
>
> This is weird. The diary shows him pressing early for random testing (which we never got) to show the real extent of the virus and criticizing (accurately) the CDC for incompetence. In the *New England Journal of Medicine*, he predicted (accurately) that Covid would likely prove as deadly to individuals as a severe flu.

This is selective-receipt vindication — [WSJ Catalogue](/propaganda/docs/wsj-technique-catalogue) §4.14 — followed by a confident counterfactual. Two true early calls, random testing and criticism of CDC incompetence, are held up as the receipt for everything the column wants, including the flu comparison, which gets “accurately” in parentheses as if a parenthetical could settle the matter.

More than a million Americans died of Covid in the first three years. That cumulative fact does not prove every restriction wise, every official competent, or every message honest. It does make “severe flu” an inadequate solvent for the scale of the national emergency. The column uses one true early prediction to normalize the later death toll.

And contact tracing? The honest argument would be that American testing and tracing were insufficient, badly executed, or mistimed. Instead, the column declares tracing itself useless: “zero leverage over spread.” That is not a receipt. It is a counterfactual smuggled in as a verdict.

Contact tracing had suppressed a novel respiratory pandemic before — in 2003, with SARS, when the case count was low enough for tracing to be built at working scale and it worked. It failed here because the country never built it at working scale, not because the instrument had no leverage. Fauci presses for testing; the testing fails to arrive; the column uses that failure to declare the strategy worthless.

One hand of the diary slaps the other.

This is also the Great Barrington position — the focused-protection proposal advanced in October 2020 by three epidemiologists and opposed in a documented open letter, the John Snow Memorandum, signed by thousands of public-health experts. Their objection was not that protecting vulnerable people was unimportant. It was that you cannot simply cordon the vulnerable while running a novel respiratory virus through everyone else.

Jenkins does not argue that case. He wears it.

That is the it-was-all-theater con, which always reads better to the audience that did not work in a hospital.

Not for our news media anything resembling the real story—bumpy, unhappy trade-offs as humanity adapts to a new bug.

Note what is being analyzed here: not the virus or the policy, but the motives of the people doing the reporting. That is the tell. When a column starts diagnosing the interior life of the press, it has stopped disputing the facts.

“Bumpy, unhappy trade-offs” is the euphemism of euphemisms. A year in which American life expectancy fell by the largest single-year margin since the Second World War becomes a bump in the road. The dead become the cost of “adapting to a new bug.” The press is blamed for making policy look like policy.

“I’m not kidding” is the confession. Operators use it when they know the claim is extravagant and want the reader to mistake extravagance for courage.
