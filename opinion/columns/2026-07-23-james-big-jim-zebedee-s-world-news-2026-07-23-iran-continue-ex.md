The Trump administration is killing American soldiers in an undeclared war against Iran.

CENTCOM announced early Thursday that U.S. forces had conducted a twelfth consecutive night of strikes against Iranian military and maritime targets. Naval forces had turned back nine commercial ships and damaged one to prevent them from entering or leaving Iranian ports. Iran struck back: the IRGC claimed attacks on Camp Udairi in Kuwait, on Ali Al Salem Air Base, and on U.S. facilities in Jordan, reporting damage to a Patriot battery, a THAAD radar, and a C-RAM system.

Eighteen killed in three weeks. Four hundred and forty-seven wounded. The Department calls this Operation Epic Fury. The Department also announced, as it always does, that casualty data was delayed days or weeks, the lag described as protecting service members.

Protecting service members. The men and women these numbers represent are the same people who have always filled the ranks in wars like this one — from the towns where the recruiter's office sits in the strip mall next to the tire shop, where the signing bonus is more than a year's wages at the feed store, where the GI Bill was the only road to a degree their parents never got. They are the Abrams crews and the infantry squads and the logistics troops keeping a blockade running around the clock. They are from the zip codes that produce more soldiers per capita than the zip codes where the policies are made. That is the arithmetic of the all-volunteer force, and it has not changed since the draft ended in 1973.

At the current rate — twenty-six dead and six hundred and forty wounded per month — the Department of Defense is consuming the young men and women of small-town America one name at a time, on a schedule designed to keep the numbers from compounding into a question. The twenty-five-to-one ratio of wounded to killed is what high-explosive ordnance does to human bodies: the geometry of blast injuries, the force that kills at close range and maims at distance, spread across a three-week air campaign. The 447 wounded will come home to VA hospitals still running behind on PACT Act claims. Their families will navigate a disability-claims system that still takes months. The towns they came from will absorb the losses the way small towns always do — quietly, at funerals on Saturday, back to work on Monday.

Clausewitz warned that war's momentum tends to escape political control — that once begun, its logic serves itself. That is the structure here. The blockade of a sovereign nation's commerce is an act of war under any reading of international law. Twelfth-night strikes on a nation-state's military infrastructure are not self-defense; they are a campaign. The IRGC hitting U.S. bases in Kuwait and Jordan is what happens when you strike a nation's military architecture and expect the response to stay inside the lines you drew. Each cycle of retaliation locks in the next. The trap was set the first night.

Who authorized it is the question the operational logic does not answer. Article I, section 8 gives Congress the power to declare war. The post-9/11 authorization was written for stateless terrorist networks, not for sustained strikes against a sovereign nation's ports, military bases, and commerce. Refusing to ask Congress is not strategic discipline; it is the refusal to submit the cost to a vote — because a vote would make the cost visible in a way a CENTCOM press release does not.

The cost is visible from the shop floor. It is visible in the towns that produce the enlistees, the families that wait, the VA hospitals that will be processing these troops for years. It is visible in every dollar that flows to a twelfth night of strikes instead of to the communities those soldiers came from — the towns that need the infrastructure, the schools, the clinics, the economic development that a government waging an undeclared war cannot seem to find money for. Eisenhower, in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, warned of the acquisition of unwarranted influence by the military-industrial complex — a machinery of war that runs on its own logic, funded by the labor of people it never asks before it spends. He said the potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist. He was speaking about this.

The two soldiers whose remains arrived on U.S. soil yesterday came from towns where everybody knew their names. They died in a war one person authorized. Four hundred and forty-seven of their brothers and sisters are wounded, the real number delayed because the Department protects service members by not telling their towns how many are coming home hurt. The towns they came from will bury them with full honors. The question is whether anyone with the power to stop this notices that the people paying for it are the people who can least afford to, and always have been.
