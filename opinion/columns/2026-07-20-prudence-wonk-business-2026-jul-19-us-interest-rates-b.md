Gene Marks is laundering small-business credit stress through a single $500,000 equipment loan.

Here is what Mr. Marks did. He took a single instrument — a five-year $500,000 bank loan at roughly today's terms — computed the annual payment under three rate scenarios ($120,942 at the current rate, $121,658 after a 25-basis-point increase, $123,099 if the prime rate reached 8.5%), and concluded that "a 25-basis-point increase doesn't meaningfully change borrowing costs for most established businesses. In fact, it changes nothing at all." The numbers check out for that specific borrower with that specific loan at that specific maturity. The numbers do not check out for the argument Mr. Marks is making with them.

Mr. Marks chose a borrower who is established, bank-financed, borrowing a sum large enough to indicate real capacity, on a five-year fixed term, for productive equipment. The borrower's existence proves the borrower's financing is available. The framing assumes, throughout, that the question on the table is whether an established business will buy a $500,000 piece of equipment next quarter. That is not the question on the table. The question on the table is whether the Federal Reserve's next move will produce a credit cycle that disproportionately damages small businesses, which is the historical pattern the Federal Reserve's own research has documented repeatedly — and which the Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey exists to track, because the rate move on the marginal new loan is not the rate move that determines aggregate small-business credit conditions.

The SLOOS is the documentary record that answers Mr. Marks' question. The aggregate tightening/loosening signal the SLOOS reports is distributionally misleading without disaggregation: bank lending standards fall disproportionately on smaller businesses and lower-credit-score consumers. Mr. Marks did not cite the SLOOS. The borrower his illustration puts on the page is the borrower the SLOOS tells us is least affected by the move he is describing.

The Federal Reserve's research staff has documented the distributional incidence of monetary policy — the finding that contractionary effects fall disproportionately on smaller and less capitalized borrowers — in working papers roughly that vintage, among the related working papers still in the Fed's archive. Mr. Marks cited neither the Fed's distributional research nor the SLOOS. He cited his own calculation. The calculation was correct. The argument it was made to support was not.

The 67% jump in small-business bankruptcy filings the American Bankruptcy Institute reported this quarter, which Mr. Marks acknowledges, is the data series his illustration excludes. The $5.1 trillion in leveraged finance Mr. Dimon flagged at JPMorgan, which Mr. Marks cites without contesting, is another excluded series. Its transmission to small-business conditions runs through the regional and community banks whose loan portfolios overlap with the leveraged-finance exposure. After the March 2023 bank failures, regulators moved to heightened scrutiny of leveraged-loan portfolios at community and regional banks — and whose senior examiners at the Federal Reserve and the FDIC have been signaling concern about exactly this overlap since the spring 2023 supervisory-cycle changes. Mr. Marks acknowledges the $5.1 trillion figure; he does not connect it to the small-business credit conditions he is describing. The connection is the analytical work his column was supposed to do.

The Mr. Moynihan and Mr. Scharf statements — that consumer spending remains resilient and that credit quality is stable — are accurate descriptions of their own bank's portfolio, and they are the statements banks are institutionally obligated to make in the weeks before a rate decision unless they want to talk their own book into a haircut. The fact that both CEOs made them is itself data. The fact that Mr. Marks cites them as evidence that small-business credit is fine is a category error — consumer credit quality at the largest money-center banks is not the same series as small-business credit conditions at community and regional banks, and the Wells Fargo and Bank of America loan books have substantially less small-business exposure than the regional banks whose credit-loss provisions Mr. Marks did not discuss.

The Marks column is not dishonest. It is selective in the way that consultancy journalism is structurally selective: the borrower who exists in the illustration is the borrower the consultant's readers are; the borrower who exists in the aggregate data is someone else, and is not the column's problem. The same shape of move produced [the optimism headlines earlier this month](/articles/2026-07-14-u-s-small-business-optimism-improved-in-june-as-fuel-costs-eased/) — sentiment indexes survey owners answering about their own firm, while the bankruptcy data counts firms that have stopped answering surveys. Both can be true. One is the leading indicator the Federal Reserve watches. The other is the lagging indicator Mr. Marks is using to claim the cycle is not what the lagging indicator says it is.

The pattern is the same one the residential-mortgage industry ran for a decade before the 2008 subprime collapse — owner-occupier prime borrowers do fine on a quarter-point move, the subprime borrower at the margin of approval does not, and the aggregate picture is set by the second group, not the first. The distributional incidence methodology exists for a reason. Mr. Marks wrote past it for a reason.

The right instrument for the question Mr. Marks is ostensibly answering is the Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, disaggregated by borrower size and credit score; the Federal Reserve research-staff distributional incidence methodology; and the aggregate small-business filing data the American Bankruptcy Institute has been publishing quarterly for years. Mr. Marks used none of those products. He used his own calculation. The arithmetic on the calculation was correct. The arithmetic on the question was not.

The score is the score. The author of the column does not get to grade it.
