Trump's second-term economy is squeezing the discretionary dollars of the voters who bought the hat.

The receipts are on the shelves. Donald "Whitey" Taylor, seventy-six, runs a MAGA merchandise store out of a former church in Boones Mill, Virginia. In 2024, the store did $15,000 a day — the best pace in its eleven-year history. By September of this year, Taylor told The Wall Street Journal's Terell Wright, the same store is doing $1,500 a day. That is a ninety percent decline. Taylor voted for the president twice. So did most of his customers.

The Trump Store Manchester, Tennessee, closed recently, citing weak sales and high operating expenses. Its final seventy-two hours drew about twelve customers a day, even with steep discounts to clear inventory. NT Souvenir Wholesale, a Washington, D.C. supplier, has cut back on purchasing Trump merchandise and lets its inventory — much of it made in China — run low. Lisa Hall, an independent sales representative in Syracuse, New York, told the Journal that MAGA apparel sales "took a nosedive" about six months ago, just after the United States entered the war in Iran.

Seth Hansen at Trump Store Branson, Missouri, told the Journal he is doing well and suggested the struggling owners lack business sense. The outlier is the outlier. The Journal's source base — wholesalers, sales representatives, business owners, and employees across multiple states — documents a pattern that is structural, not operational.

The Journal names four proximate causes: a stagnant economy, higher gas prices tied to the Iran war, persistent inflation, and weakened MAGA enthusiasm from voters who felt they had already done their part. Read that list again.

Two of the four — gas prices and inflation — are the same household-budget squeeze wearing different labels. A working-class family paying more at the pump and more for imported goods, including the merchandise on these shelves, much of which NT Souvenir acknowledges is made in China. The two pressures land on the same discretionary dollar — the one that decides between the hat and a toy for the kid. Hall told the Journal that when it comes down to a toy for a child or a MAGA hat, the customer chooses the toy, when in a better economy they would have bought both. The pinching at the MAGA register is the same pinching the consumer economy extracts at the checkout — gas, groceries, and imported goods all running through the same squeezed household budget.

This is a demand-side signal, not a business-failure story. Hansen's store is doing well because Branson draws tourist traffic, not local working-class shoppers making a budget trade-off. The stores that are struggling sit in communities where the household budget is the binding constraint. The merchandise register is the scoreboard: a ninety percent revenue decline in a store that was doing fine thirteen months ago, a shop in Tennessee that could not give its inventory away, a supplier in Washington letting its shelves go bare because there is no point stocking what people cannot buy.

The customers who can no longer afford the hat are not disillusioned with the movement. They are broke. That distinction matters. This is not a story about MAGA enthusiasm fading. It is a story about discretionary income disappearing — and the movement's own merchandise becoming the surface on which the squeeze becomes legible. Brisco, eighty-one, who lives near Taylor's store in Roanoke, told the Journal the store is a vital landmark for preserving Trump's legacy. She talks to her grandchildren about conservative values. The people who care most about the cause are the ones who can no longer afford the merchandise.

Taylor is waiting for January 20, 2029, betting nostalgic supporters will return once Trump leaves office. He is offering more America 250 merchandise and Confederate memorabilia to fill the gap. It has not helped. The structural problem with the bet is the structural problem the Journal's four causes identify: the economic squeeze — the Iran war driving gas prices higher, persistent inflation, a stagnant economy — does not reverse on Inauguration Day. The household budget that cannot afford a hat in September 2026 will not suddenly have room for one in January 2029. The pinching does not care who is in the Oval Office. It cares what is left in the wallet.
