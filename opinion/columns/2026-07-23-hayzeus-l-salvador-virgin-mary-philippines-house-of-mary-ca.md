Sixty-seven square meters of stone, placed in a field outside Manila, and the whole thing comes with a guarantee: no questions asked. That is the selling point Father Dennis Paez offers for his replica of the House of the Virgin Mary in Alfonso, Cavite province — a 720-square-foot promise that the faithful who step inside will never be asked to examine the machinery that drew them there.

Paez, a priest who also works as a psychologist specializing in youth, opened the replica in 2024 after roughly ten months of construction. He made sure the dimensions, shape, and even the look of every stone mirrored the Ephesus original, giving Filipino Catholics a local pilgrimage destination that spares them the trip to Turkey. The arrangement is convenient. It is also a trap.

Consider what the trap looks like at the level of the body. Young people arrive at the shrine carrying weight they cannot name — the pressure that sends Filipino youth to a psychologist-priest promising refuge, the daily burden no one in their parish has learned to ask about directly. They kneel on stone measured to match a house in Turkey most will never visit. They touch the walls. They are received with unconditional positive regard, and no one asks what brought them. Their bodies are doing what bodies do at shrines — seeking proximity to something sacred, something maternal, something that will not interrogate the wound. What is happening to them is real. What is being built around them while they kneel is also real.

The shrine is staffed by an apostolate that Paez describes with the careful language of a clinic: "unconditional positive regard." He wants visitors to understand that no therapy happens there, yet the therapist, he says, "is our Mother." A psychologist-priest has built a replica holy house, directed interfaith laborers to construct it, and then announced that the space operates on therapeutic principles except there is no therapy — only a maternal figure who sees and judges nothing. For a man whose specialty is supporting youth, the offer of a "no questions asked" refuge under a mother's gaze is not innocent. It is an architecture of access.

Interfaith workers — laborers of different faiths — were used to build the shrine, a detail Paez frames as the Virgin Mary bringing all her children together. The more precise reading is that the project needed bodies and found them. The honest version of this observation turns it inward: the publication I write for does something structurally similar, offering controlled spaces of moral authority, then presenting the diversity of voices as proof that the enterprise is a unifier rather than an operator. We are not exempt from the dynamics we name. The difference between this column and Paez's shrine is a matter of degree, and pretending otherwise would be the dishonesty this column exists to refuse. Now the site's own origin story doubles as an ecumenical credential, the same move that [Marian shrines across Asia routinely use to soften their institutional edges](/articles/2026-07-17-hindu-and-muslim-pilgrims-flock-to-catholic-marian-shrine-in-eastern-india/). In eastern India, Hindu and Muslim pilgrims are drawn to a Catholic Marian site with a similarly convenient blurring of boundaries. The pattern is stable: build a devotional magnet, staff it with diverse hands, then market the result as proof that the institution is a uniter, not an operator.

The Feast of Our Lady of Mount Carmel, observed in mid-July, has already brought pilgrimages to the Alfonso replica. Watch the bodies move through the space: the grandmother who has attended Mass every day of her adult life and now drives two hours to kneel in a 720-square-foot copy, her knees pressing into ground surveyed to match coordinates set by someone else's centuries-old devotion; the young man who cannot afford the trip to Ephesus but who can afford this, who walks the perimeter with his rosary, performing the ancient devotion at industrial scale without knowing that is what he is doing. The site is doing its work — capturing the devotion of a Catholic-majority country that provides a bottomless pool of pilgrims who might otherwise wander toward less controlled expressions of faith. The House of the Virgin Mary in Ephesus held its authority for centuries because it was remote and scarce. By duplicating it at exact scale within a short drive of the capital, Paez has industrialized that scarcity. Every stone copied is a stone that no longer requires a journey, and every journey eliminated is a transaction that stays inside the parish ledger.

"This is a place where no questions are asked," Paez said, and he means it as a promise. It is also a confession. A priest trained in the psy-disciplines has constructed a no-interrogation zone specifically for the young and the seeking, and he has done it under the sign of a mother who cannot say no. That is not pastoral care. That is a capture operation in the language of unconditional love.
