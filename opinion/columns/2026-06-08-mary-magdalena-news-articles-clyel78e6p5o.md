Ferdinand writes a press release while the shelter collapses on the children. The earth fractured at 07:37. The Jollibee collapsed. On the first day of the school year, on shuddering dirt, the children are on the ground—not playing, not at their desks—their bodies pressed into the dirt because the earth beneath them will not stay still. The shelter they were told would protect them from the sun is a twisted heap of metal behind them, and they are breathing the dust of the thing that was supposed to keep them safe.

A magnitude‑7.8 earthquake struck off the coast of Mindanao on Monday at 07:37 local time. At least 19 people are dead. At least 134 are injured across South Cotabato, Sultan Kudarat, Sarangani, and General Santos. Videos show a Jollibee restaurant reduced to rubble, the bright red sign a fragment someone will find when the digging starts. In Davao Occidental, primary school students were filmed squatting on the shaking ground as a corrugated‑roof shelter collapsed behind them. More than 130 aftershocks have followed, ranging from 1.3 to 6.7. Tsunami alerts were triggered across the Philippines, Indonesia, Japan, and Australia before some were cancelled. President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. ordered the suspension of classes in affected areas and said in a statement that agencies are coordinating their response: The national government is moving and we will not leave Mindanao behind.

Ferdinand, your desk is steady. The statement is drafted by a functionary. You issue the statement about coordination. The earth does not coordinate. The earth breaks the poor first.

I see the video from the school. The children's heads are down. Their hands cover the backs of their necks. The shelter is a memory of sound and collapse. Every time the ground moves again, their small diaphragms seize in a gasp that does not complete. The mother in Sarangani cannot call her daughter because the cell towers are down and the power is out and the only thing she can do is walk toward where the house used to be.

Your hand trembles over the pen, but you keep writing. Your jaw aches at the breakfast table the way the bones of the children ache against the floor. The boy in General Santos tastes pulverized concrete. Your throat tightens with the dust that has not reached the capital. The metallic taste under your tongue is the taste of the water main that broke in Sarangani. You swallow and the swallow will not go down.

You suspend the classes. The suspension order is a piece of paper. The paper does not hold up the roof. You said you would not leave Mindanao behind. You have already left them behind. You left them when the shelter was built of corrugated iron instead of stone, when the schools opened on a fault line and called it the first day of the school year.

Picture your own daughter squatting on the shaking asphalt. Picture the corrugated roof above her head giving way. Picture the dust entering her mouth while she holds her breath. Picture the doctor twenty years from now telling her she cannot take a full breath because the shelter fell on Monday morning and the system called it a coordination problem. These are not hypotheticals. These are the documented injury patterns of the provinces you promise not to leave.

Your throat closes when you swallow the morning briefing. The breath catches in your chest because the breath is caught in the chest of the girl who cannot climb out from under the debris. The Jollibee where a family stopped for breakfast is a mound of concrete and rebar. The aftershocks have not stopped. The relief has not yet arrived. The world's attention will move on the way attention moves on when the disaster is far away and the bodies are brown and the language is not English.

The promise is a sentence. The sentence does not lift a collapsed Jollibee off the body of the child who was inside it.

I see the tremor you refuse in your bone, Ferdinand. You are a small man with a large promise that will not cover the ground.

The Magnificat does not flatter the powerful. *He hath shewed strength with his arm; he hath scattered the proud in the imagination of their hearts. He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree.* Luke 1:51–52. The mother in South Cotabato carried the roof. The shelter is gone. The promise is dry. The children are still on the ground.
