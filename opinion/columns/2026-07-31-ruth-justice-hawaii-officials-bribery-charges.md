The sitting lieutenant governor of Hawaii was indicted for bribery, pled not guilty through her lawyers, and said nothing in court — and nobody asked where the money went. A kid gets a record for shoplifting; a lieutenant governor gets a date. Three months out, the calendar of privilege.

Four current and former officials stood beside their attorneys before Judge Ronald Johnson on Thursday. The attorneys spoke for them. The judge accepted the pleas. The date was set. The defendants stood silent, and the system moved on as if the indictment were a scheduling matter. Lt. Gov. Sylvia Luke did not say a word. Former state Rep. Ryan Yamane did not say a word. Ford Fuchigami did not say a word. Leo Asuncion did not say a word. The attorneys said not guilty, and the judge accepted that, and nobody in the courtroom asked where the money went.

This is not four bad apples. This is the class of officials who take money while the people they serve pay the price. The mechanism is the same in every case: the official holds the power to write rules, to grant contracts, to steer money. Someone with an interest in those rules, contracts, or money hands the official something — cash, a job, a favor, a promise. The official uses the power. The public doesn't see the exchange. The indictment is the moment the exchange becomes visible. And then the machinery starts: the plea, the date, the silence.

The contrast lives one courtroom over. [The former Jackson mayor](/articles/2026-07-07-former-jackson-mayor-pleads-guilty-to-bribery-wire-fraud-money-laundering/) pleaded guilty to bribery, wire fraud, and money laundering. He spoke. He took responsibility. The system moved fast, because the system rewards a man who plays its game. The Hawaii officials stayed silent, and the calendar rewards them too — slowly, with three months of freedom, three months of representing no one, three months of letting the public forget what the indictment named. The takers who stand silent get a date. The one who speaks gets a sentence. The calendar of privilege is the same calendar either way, but it ticks at different speeds depending on what the defendant has to say.

The officials said nothing. The money can't either. But the trial is set for September 28, and silence runs out.
