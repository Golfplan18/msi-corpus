*The compliance department is now the crime.*

Two federal prosecutors from Jeanine Pirro's office toured the Federal Reserve's construction site in April. They demanded a tour. The visit was a flex—a signal that the U.S. Attorney for Washington, D.C., can reach into any institution, for any reason, and make its leaders squirm. Now the same office is sending subpoenas to America's largest banks. The demand: hand over lists of customers whose accounts you closed, and explain why. Especially if one of those customers is Donald Trump.

This is not an investigation. It is a court-ordered shakedown, an escalation of the executive branch's war on the financial apparatus. The subpoenas to JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, and Wells Fargo recast basic compliance risk-assessment as "debanking." They recast anti-money-laundering protocols as political persecution. The mechanism is the subpoena. The target is any institution that applies the law to the president, and the message is simple: if you enforce the law against us, we will enforce the law against you.

The structural anatomy of this campaign follows a clear trajectory. The president signed an executive order in August directing regulators to find "politicized or unlawful debanking." The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, operating under Treasury pressure, released a preliminary report alleging early evidence of such practices against oil-and-gas operators, firearms manufacturers, and adult-entertainment businesses. It cited "public reports released by banks about their environmental and social commitments, including efforts to fight climate change and racial inequality"—as though a bank's decision to phase out lending to fossil-fuel extraction amounts to political discrimination rather than routine risk management. The OCC itself, in other words, confessed: the thing the White House calls debanking is, in large measure, a bank deciding whom it wants to do business with, for reasons that have nothing to do with the First Amendment and everything to do with the bottom line and public reputation.

But the OCC never made a referral for prosecution. So Pirro's office opened the investigations itself, pairing them with the president's own private lawsuits. Trump sued JPMorgan and Jamie Dimon personally. His family sued Capital One. Now his U.S. Attorney wields criminal subpoenas to do in government what his lawsuits aimed to do in private: compel the banking industry to bankroll his political operation and its patrons, on pain of prosecution. The merger erases the firewall. Once the DOJ takes the president's complaint and converts it into a federal investigation, every commercial bank receives the message: you exist to serve as the financial guarantors for the president's coalition.

Apply the *cui bono* trace. Who benefits? The president and his inner circle benefit by reframing their own financial isolation as evidence of a deep-state conspiracy. They benefit by forcing banks to expend legal resources and compliance hours defending standard procedures against a federal prosecutor's demands for the names of flagged customers, the internal reasoning behind every closure, and the proprietary risk models that keep the financial system from laundering proceeds for a political machine whose senior figures have already been indicted and convicted on federal charges. They benefit by creating a threat environment so noisy, intrusive, and exhausting that the next time a bank worries about its public commitments on climate or racial equity, it pauses—because Jeanine Pirro's prosecutors might turn up with subpoenas, and a tour of the premises is never just a tour. The cost falls upon the financial system's baseline integrity, upon the rule of law, and upon the independence required to prevent fraud and laundering.

The administration's framing deploys three techniques from the bad-faith catalog. The first is preemptive legitimacy-withdrawal: the executive order declares that banks are engaged in "unlawful debanking" before citing a single statute that compels a private bank to extend credit or retain a customer it has deemed a compliance risk. The withdrawal of legitimacy precedes any evidentiary finding; it grounds the investigation in the identity of the bank—as an alleged enemy of "conservatives"—rather than in a specific violation. The second is motte-and-bailey. The aggressive claim—the bailey—is that financial institutions conspired to silence political opponents and defund the president. When pressed on why the subpoenas demand reasoning for closures that include a former head of state, the administration retreats to the motte: regulators are simply asking whether risk-management software accidentally flags certain industries. They do not retreat permanently. They survive the first wave of scrutiny, then resume the bailey in public statements, insisting the banks acted politically, that the banks discriminated, that the banks owe an accounting to the executive branch. The third is frame-engineered relabeling. "Debanking" replaces "compliance" in the administration's vocabulary, reframing the decision of a risk manager who reviews a post–January 6 profile, observes federal indictments for seditious conspiracy and obstruction, and declines the liability. By relabeling this act as "debanking," the administration shifts the burden of proof: the bank must now defend why it *did not* take the client, rather than the client defending why they *should* be banked. Both terms refer to the termination of a commercial relationship, but the relabeling injects a political charge that transforms a private contract into a civil-rights violation.

And the whole apparatus amounts to what RAND analysts would call flooding the zone. The White House has already been shoving banks into a new role as immigration enforcement arms, demanding they flag customers' citizenship status. Now, apparently, banks must also serve as the financial guarantors for the president's political machine. The message is simple: you can refuse to bank a coal baron or a firearm manufacturer because you fear reputational damage, but if you do, a federal prosecutor may come looking through your internal decisions, and she may find a fraud statute you didn't know applied to branch closures.

That statute is the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989—FIRREA—a broad law designed to chase the kind of fraud that blew up the mortgage market in 2008. There is no known instance of it being used to punish a bank for declining to hold a customer's money. The stretch is audacious because the statute specifically targets fraudulent asset misrepresentation, not the routine, discretionary closure of depository accounts. FIRREA exists to protect the public from institutions that misrepresent asset quality or hide exposure. It does not exist to punish institutions that refuse to hide exposure. The administration uses FIRREA to threaten the very compliance mechanisms the statute was designed to protect, consistent with the Pirro office's pattern: the earlier investigation into Fed Chair Jerome Powell's building renovations—which included that goonish construction-site tour—was so obviously retaliatory that even Democrats called it out. This is not a U.S. Attorney's office pursuing crime. It is a collection agency for a president's grievances and a donor class's preferences.

The root cause is the personalization of the administrative state. When a political faction treats every regulatory agency as a subsidiary of its campaign operation, the distinction between public law and private grievance collapses. Pirro and the prosecutors in her office—Carlton Davis and Steven Vandervelden—operate on the premise that the state exists to audit the private sector for political orthodoxy. The investigation into the Federal Reserve's renovations, like the banking subpoenas, follows the identical posture: locate an institution that operates independently of the executive branch, locate an ordinary administrative decision, and reclassify that decision as evidence of conspiracy. The mechanism functions as a deterrent. It works by exhausting the target's resources. It works by creating the appearance of guilt before evidence exists. And when a regime that feels secure suddenly demands the entire apparatus kneel, it is not operating from strength. It is operating from exposure. *Power doesn't panic.* The executive branch is panicking, deploying the full weight of federal law to audit the private sector for political orthodoxy because it knows its own isolation.

When Martin Luther King wrote from a Birmingham jail cell, he argued that tension is not the enemy of justice, but the necessary condition of it. He wrote that injustice must be exposed to the light of human conscience, that it must be lanced like a boil so that the underlying infection can be cleared. The tension the president manufactures is not the tension King described. King's tension exposed systemic injustice; it forced the powerful to reckon with the reality of the marginalized. The tension the executive manufactures is a distraction. It is a manufactured controversy designed to exhaust the institutions that stand between the president's grievances and absolute authority. The boil that requires lancing is not in the banks. The boil is in the executive branch. The infection is the conviction that the president is entitled to the resources of the private sector, and that any private actor who declines that entitlement is an enemy of the state.

The Department of Justice once used FIRREA to hold financial institutions accountable for blowing up the global economy. Now it is being used to make sure those same institutions keep hosting the president's checkbook. The banks know what this is. The public should too. It is a protection racket wrapped in legal paper. And the only way it stops is if people call it exactly what it is, and refuse to mistake a shakedown for the law.

The arc of the moral universe bends toward justice, but only when the mechanisms of justice are preserved. The mechanism requires an administrative state that can operate without fear of retaliatory subpoenas. It requires a Department of Justice that prosecutes crime according to the evidence, not the preferences of the president. It requires a financial sector that can close a fraudulent account without fear that the decision will become a federal investigation. The structural reality of this moment is the conversion of public authority into private power. The president uses the Department of Justice to punish the banks. The president uses the subpoenas to enforce submission. The horizon remains open. The work of preserving the distinction between public law and private grievance is the work of the moment. The arc bends only when we push it. Push it now. Push it with the receipts.
