Mary Anastasia O’Grady’s Monday column on Colombia’s election crisis is a remarkable piece of journalistic architecture. She has built a structure that stands on two legs—one stamps its foot at Gustavo Petro for refusing to accept his party’s loss, the other kicks at Donald Trump for the same offense. The reader is supposed to admire the building’s symmetry. But the foundation is the same old WSJ sand: the selective outrage of the Latin America desk, laundered through a “we can see our own sins too” posture that is just a more sophisticated version of the grift.

> South America’s longest-standing democracy is staring down the barrel of a constitutional crisis, and that isn’t good for the Western Hemisphere. Unfortunately President Trump, who won’t acknowledge that he lost the 2020 U.S. election, isn’t much help because at the heart of the matter is another election denier, Colombian President Gustavo Petro.
> *— O’Grady, paragraph 1*

This is the opening move, and it is a small masterpiece of what we used to call “the circles of accountability.” By naming Trump’s denial in the first paragraph, O’Grady buys a very expensive form of credibility. It says: *I am not a hypocrite; I see the beam in my own country’s eye.* The reader’s guard drops. The reader thinks: *Finally, a principled conservative voice.*

But the sentence is doing double duty. The “unfortunately Trump isn’t much help” clause elbows the American president out of the way, clearing the stage for the *real* target: the Latin American leftist who didn’t get the memo that the WSJ’s global mission is to police the progeny of the Bolivarian revolution. The acknowledgment of Trump’s denial is a passcode, not a principle. It primes the reader to accept the rest of the indictment as even-handed, when every subsequent paragraph is a WSJ greatest-hits album on leftist Latin America.

> Because Mr. Petro was constitutionally barred from running for a consecutive second term, his Pacto Histórico party put up Sen. Iván Cepeda as its presidential candidate. In the June runoff election, according to Colombia’s electoral authority, Mr. Cepeda lost to lawyer and businessman Abelardo de la Espriella. Mr. Petro refuses to recognize the president-elect.
> *— O’Grady, paragraph 2*

Clean, factual setup. The facts are likely correct. The issue is what she *chooses* to do with them. She frames this entirely as a “power obsession” without a single sentence acknowledging the deeply compromised nature of the Colombian electoral authority, the documented history of paramilitary influence on Colombian elections, or the very real fear that de la Espriella represents a business-as-usual restoration of the Uribe-era right. The *context* of the election is suppressed. The *conclusion*—Petro is a power-crazed Chavista—is presented as the only possible reading.

> After four years of mediocre economic performance and growing public insecurity, Colombia can ill afford the political instability that Mr. Petro’s intransigence is likely to cause. But then this isn’t about the well-being of the nation. It’s about an obsession with power.
> *— O’Grady, paragraph 4*

“It’s about power” is the lazy operator’s dismissal. It’s the rhetorical equivalent of a shrug. It allows the writer to foreclose the possibility that Petro might have a rational, if self-serving, legal or political strategy. Every losing incumbent in a polarized system cries fraud. The operator’s question is not “are they obsessed with power?”—the answer to that is always yes—but “what is the structure of the play they are running?” O’Grady doesn’t answer that because she doesn’t have to. The “power obsession” frame is a terminal diagnosis, not an analysis. We used this frame all the time in the cable years. It works because it rings true on the surface and requires no evidence.

> When Mr. Petro was elected in 2022, his ideological allies likely thought they were in the presidential Casa Nariño for the long haul. After all, Venezuela’s Hugo Chávez was democratically elected in 1998 and by the early 2000s had destroyed many of the institutional checks that might have blocked his one-man rule.
> *— O’Grady, paragraph 5*

Here is the rubber hitting the road. O’Grady cannot write a column about a Latin American leftist without invoking the ghost of Hugo Chávez. It is the mechanic’s reflex. The rhetorical move is the “advantageous comparison” in Bandura’s moral disengagement framework, applied here in reverse: Petro is bad because he is like Chávez, who was very bad. In the operator’s register, this is a “known-quantity anchor”—converting an unfamiliar actor into a familiar threat to reduce cognitive load. The comparison does the work of proof. The reader is not asked to judge Petro’s four years of governance on their own terms—the 0.6% growth, the 23% minimum wage hike, the security deterioration. They are asked to see the seed of the Bolivarian template. The technique is the same one the WSJ has used for thirty years to sell every intervention, every trade deal, every political endorsement in the hemisphere. It works on the audience because the audience has been trained to see Chávez in every leftist the way a trained dog sees a bird.

> Mr. Cepeda came much closer to winning than polls predicted, perhaps because he benefited from Mr. Petro’s over-the-top populism… But last year a public-spending blitz led to a 2.6% economic expansion. Colombians went to the polls feeling better about their pocketbooks.
> *— O’Grady, paragraphs 8 & 9*

This is a deeply revealing paragraph. O’Grady admits that the economy picked up, and Colombians felt better about their pocketbooks. The piece cannot leave this hanging. It must poison the good news. The next paragraph immediately pivots to “Mr. Petro’s dismal security record.” The structure is classic “yes, but.” The pocketbook improvement is a fleeting sugar high from a “spending blitz.” The “real” story is security. And note: the numbers themselves are editorial selections—0.6% and 1.6% stand without regional benchmarks or post-pandemic context, while the 2.6% is dismissed as a “spending blitz.” Data selection is editorial architecture, not neutral presentation. This is the WSJ’s core economic theology on display: growth that comes from wages and public spending is illegitimate; it must be scored against the “real” ledger of security and fiscal rectitude. The operator’s-eye-view here is that the frame is designed to deny the legitimacy of any economic outcome that defies the WSJ’s preferred model.

> On election night the initial tally was close. Messrs. Petro and Cepeda immediately called for the count to be scrutinized. They still came up short. Now Mr. Petro alleges electoral fraud by the Israelis, who he says manipulated the voting machine software. It’s an indication of how desperate he is to undermine the credibility of the new government. Lawfare against Mr. de la Espriella can’t be far off.
> *— O’Grady, paragraph 11*

The “Israelis” accusation. This is a gift to O’Grady. A wild conspiracy theory handed to her on a silver platter, perfectly tailored to confirm every bias the WSJ reader has about the desperate, irrational leftist. She uses it to close the frame on the “election denier” label. The tag “lawfare” is a nice touch—a term the WSJ deploys reflexively against any legal action against its favored political players, here deployed against Petro. The irony should be thick enough to taste, but the piece trusts the audience won’t taste it.

> Mr. de la Espriella wants to make Colombia a destination for investment. The country needs decentralization, and the new president will need to build a governing coalition. Working with regional politicians outside the capital can deliver both. His plan to hold his inauguration at a military garrison in the south of the country rather than at the traditional Plaza de Bolívar in Bogotá seems a good start.
> *— O’Grady, paragraph 12*

And here is the unmagical ending. The new president wants to make Colombia a destination for investment. The WSJ’s candidate. The story is written. The frame is closed. The column is nothing more than a very well-written transmission of the approved narrative: the Latin American leftist is a power-drunk Chavista who steals elections, and the business-friendly conservative is the legitimate heir to democracy. The fact that the U.S. president is the hemisphere’s most famous election denier is used as a shield—“see, even we admit it!”—not as a mirror. The column is a receipt for how the *cui bono* of the Latin America desk operates. The audience gets the satisfying feeling of having their worldview confirmed. The Colombian right gets a column of legitimacy in the most influential newspaper in the Western Hemisphere. The Venezuelan Chavista template, which the WSJ has been selling for a generation, gets another full-ride scholarship.

So here is what this column actually is, taken together. A piece of machinery engineered to extract maximum investment legitimation from a single political crisis. It concedes the bad actor in its own camp to armor itself against the charge of hypocrisy, then runs the standard-issue Latin America desk script without a single moment of self-awareness about the structural role the WSJ editorial page plays in creating the very conditions it decries.

The column is not wrong about Petro’s conduct. It is wrong about what it is. It is not a piece of journalism. It is a piece of the apparatus. The WSJ has played the Latin America game for a century. In the cable years, I helped write the playbooks for this game. The “operation” is the suppression of the U.S. empire’s own role in creating the conditions for the strongman leftists it now condemns. The column is theater. The audience is supposed to applaud the principled condemnation of an election denier, and miss the fact that the applause rig is powered by the same fuel that powers the denial it is pointing at. The column is the mirror. Petro is its reflection. And the WSJ editorial page is the face that looks into the mirror and does not recognize itself.

1. **Inoculation Opener** (paragraph 1): Conceding the U.S. election denial to preempt the charge of hypocrisy, then pivoting to the real target. A cable-years standard for credibility laundering.
2. **Known-Quantity Anchor** (paragraph 5): Recasting an unfamiliar political figure (Petro) as a familiar threat (Chávez) to bypass evaluated judgment and trigger conditioned threat response. Bandura’s “advantageous comparison” rendered in operational terms.

5. **Lawfare Transfer** (paragraph 11): Deploying the term “lawfare”—normally reserved for legal actions against WSJ-favored actors—against Petro’s threatened legal challenges, reframing his defensive legal strategy as an offensive weapon.
