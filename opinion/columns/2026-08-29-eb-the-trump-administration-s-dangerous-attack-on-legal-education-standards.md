The Trump Administration is dismantling the accreditation system that has protected American legal education for nearly a century. Its pressure on the American Bar Association, dressed up as reform, is a political assault on institutional safeguards that keep legal education honest and accessible.

Last week the Education Department released an 86-page staff report recommending that the ABA lose its longstanding role as the recognized accreditor of roughly 200 law schools. The pretext is that the ABA has been slow to abandon its diversity and inclusion guidance since the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. The motive is unmistakable: punish a professional body that refused to bend quickly enough.

The ABA had asked schools to demonstrate "concrete" efforts toward diversity and inclusion. According to the Education Department's own report, the ABA anticipated in 2022 that the Justices might shift the legal landscape, yet continued through the 2024–2025 review cycle to train reviewers and evaluate schools on the existing standard before suspending the work. That is what an institution with memory and respect for precedent does. The Administration is punishing the ABA for following the rules it was given.

The Administration's chosen exhibit is the Justice Department's accusation this month that Duke Law School engaged in illegal racial discrimination in its 2023–25 admissions. White and Asian applicants denied admission had higher median LSAT scores than black students accepted. Duke says it is reviewing the allegations and committed to following the law. The Administration treats one open investigation as proof that the entire accreditation system is broken and the institution under investigation as guilty until proven compliant. That is not enforcement. That is coercion.

Texas, Florida, Washington, and Alabama have moved to reduce the ABA's gate-keeping function in licensing. Tennessee is considering the same step, with support from the Federal Trade Commission and Justice Department. In a recent letter, those officials wrote that the ABA "is dominated by law school faculty and administrators with strong incentives to thwart lower cost alternatives for legal education." Standards are not monopolies. They are the reason a law license means anything to the public.

State-by-state fragmentation will leave law students with degrees that mean different things in different states — no portable quality assurance, no national floor on who may call themselves a lawyer. Removing national accreditation invites a race to the bottom in which the cheapest, most ideologically compliant programs proliferate and the JD credential loses its meaning.

Pressure is producing results. The same day the Education Department report dropped, the ABA's accreditation arm voted to meet in September to consider rolling back its diversity and equity mandates. That is capitulation, not reform. A coerced accreditor is not an independent accreditor — which is the very defect the Administration claims to be correcting.

The Administration's campaign will not produce better legal education. It will produce a fractured, politicized, lower-quality system — and the public will pay the price every time an unaccountable law school graduate crosses a courtroom threshold.
