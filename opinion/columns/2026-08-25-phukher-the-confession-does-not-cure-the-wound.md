The industrial world's political classes are admitting, however grudgingly, that the policies they pursued have produced wreckage. The admission changes nothing for the victims.

James Freeman's Monday column "Trump Persuades Europe" (WSJ Opinion, August 24, 2026) is the spine of the operation I want to walk you through. I drafted versions of the technique it deploys for two decades on cable. The work now is to name what the column does and where the wound is. The wound is not in the column. The wound is the deindustrialization the column relabels as a Trump victory.

> *— Freeman, "Trump Persuades Europe," WSJ Opinion*

Asymmetric concession — [Bad-Faith Catalog](/propaganda/docs/bad-faith-techniques-catalogue) ID `goalpost_shifting`, the standard-shift embedded in the concession frame. The adversary concedes the deindustrialization script was wrong. The column grants the concession its full rhetorical weight and withholds from it its substantive content. The policy was wrong because it produced wreckage; the column grants the wreckage while reclassifying it as a Trump-vindication story. The adversary's admission is the win. The wreckage is not separately addressed. It was the price of resistance, and the resistance is now over. The frame is: the damage was caused by delay, not by the policy. The policy was right; the timing was wrong. The goalpost moves from "did the policy produce harm?" — which the wreckage answers — to "who recognized the harm first?" — which the column can answer in its own favor.

I built this move. It is the move where you take the other side's confession of error and convert it into vindication of your prior position. The Bandura mechanism is moral justification — the prior position is rehabilitated by the adversary's belated recognition of the same facts you had identified. I sat in the cable greenroom in the mid-2010s and watched this move get loaded into the dock for both climate and trade coverage. We called it "the late convert's confession," and we knew — we knew — that the confession was the cover for the continuation of the policy under a friendlier label.

The damage is not undone by the admission. Every factory that became uneconomic to operate on the Continent is still uneconomic. Every permitting regime that locked in higher electricity costs is still locked in. The grid instability that drove energy-intensive industry to relocate is still there. The capital that exited did not return on a Brussels press release, and it will not return on this one either. Europe's confession is a damage assessment conducted years too late for the workers, the communities, and the industrial base that were sacrificed.

The Chicago Tribune dispatch from A.D. Quig is the reminder that some political classes do not retreat. They repeat. You have to hand it to Chicagoland politicians for their resolve. The northern-suburban homeowners profiled are caught in the familiar compound — rising tax burdens chasing declining property values chasing tighter budgets chasing higher tax rates — and the political class has shown no inclination to break the cycle. If they intend to continue the fiscal discipline until prosperity returns, the patience is paying off. This is especially true for homeowners in the northern suburbs, who are sharing in the sacrifice.

The Bank of America note from Joe Wadford and Vanessa Cook confirms the diagnosis at the institutional level. Cook County, DuPage County, and the collar counties are not recovering from a fiscal problem; they are operating one. The tax base is the target. The tax base is also the revenue. The politicians will not fix this, because fixing it would require admitting that the problem is the structure, not the rate.

Lisa Fickenscher's New York Post reporting lays bare the third exhibit. Faced with the option of allowing a major grocery operator to open stores in underserved neighborhoods, the mayor's office chose instead to subsidize politically connected competitors — guaranteeing higher prices, fewer choices, and a captive customer base. Many New Yorkers may wish the bodega owners well while also wondering why the mayor insists that new grocery competition must come from out-of-town corporate chains rather than from local tax-subsidized entrepreneurs.

The consumer in the affected neighborhoods pays the difference at the register. The taxpayer subsidizes the operator through the city's program. The politically connected owner collects the margin that competition would have compressed. The arrangement is the arrangement, and the neighborhoods that needed affordable groceries receive instead the municipal version of the same protectionism that has made the rest of the city's retail landscape uncompetitive.

Each story is a local failure that the political class responsible will not acknowledge, will not fix, and will not pay for. Together they form a pattern. The era in which advanced economies treated economic growth as a side effect to be tolerated rather than a goal to be pursued is not yet over — it is merely slowing, in some jurisdictions, while accelerating in others. The pragmatic pivot is not a recovery. It is a slower rate of decline. The capital, the households, and the consumers that leave the affected jurisdictions do not return on a press release. The political classes responsible for the wreckage have yet to pay a price for any of it. And the consumers, the workers, and the homeowners who absorbed the costs are still waiting for someone to call it what it was.

The confession does not cure the wound. The trick is to relabel the confession as the cure. The surface: the other side admitted error. The substance: the wreckage remains and the bill remains with the people who paid it. The page has won the argument. The page has not paid for the wreckage.
