Russia and North Korea are sending soldiers to die for battlefield lessons.

That is the human mechanism beneath this partnership. Governments trade troops, missiles, and ammunition for experience. The soldiers absorb the danger. Their families absorb the telegrams, the empty chairs, and the years of uncertainty. Ukrainian civilians absorb the missiles. Korean communities absorb the future risk when North Korean units bring home what they learned in Russia.

South Korea’s National Intelligence Service says North Korea sent roughly 15,000 troops to support Russian forces in the Kursk region. About 2,000 were killed, according to the agency. Volodymyr Zelensky says Pyongyang is preparing to send another 30,000 to 50,000 personnel and additional ballistic missiles. South Korea’s Foreign Ministry has not confirmed that larger estimate. The precise size of a future deployment remains uncertain. The existing human cost does not.

North Korean soldiers have already fought in Europe alongside Russian forces. Whatever they learn about drones, artillery, air defense, communications, logistics, and surviving under fire can be carried back to the Korean Peninsula. That knowledge will not remain on the battlefield. It will enter training rooms, weapons programs, command plans, and exercises aimed at South Korean cities.

Clausewitz wrote in *On War*, Book I, Chapter 7, about friction, the small failures that turn plans on paper into danger in the real world. A manual can describe how a unit moves. Combat shows what happens when the radio fails, a vehicle breaks down, a bridge is destroyed, a power station is hit, or a hospital loses electricity. It shows soldiers how long a convoy can survive, how quickly a command post can be found, and how families live when missiles make ordinary work impossible.

That is what Russia is providing North Korea: not a classroom, but a war.

The bargain is plain. North Korea supplies soldiers, missiles, and munitions. Russia supplies access to a modern battlefield. Russia receives manpower for a war it cannot sustain alone. North Korea receives combat experience it cannot safely obtain through exercises. Both governments make the trade. The deaths, displacement, damaged power systems, and future danger are pushed onto people with no seat at the table.

The enlisted family in North Korea does not negotiate the deployment. The Russian military family does not set the terms of the bargain. The Ukrainian power crew repairing a damaged line does not decide which missiles arrive from Pyongyang. The nurse working in a hospital under attack does not get to vote on whether Moscow needs reinforcements. Those are the people who pay for the agreement.

The United States and South Korea are right to account for this experience in their next major joint exercise. U.S. Forces Korea spokesman Col. Ryan Donald said North Korean soldiers deployed and fought in Ukraine, then brought their lessons back to North Korea. The exercise must account for a North Korean force changed by combat, not merely a force described by old intelligence estimates.

But an exercise is not protection by itself. A plan does not keep a hospital running when its power fails. A briefing does not protect a family sheltering under a missile warning. A table-top scenario does not repair a factory, restore a port, or keep a communications crew alive after an attack. Strategy has to reach the people who will stand beneath it.

We have already covered [North Korea’s warning that a physical clash on the peninsula is only a matter of time](/articles/2026-07-31-north-korea-warns-physical-clash-on-peninsula-a-matter-of-time/). That warning matters less as a slogan than as a question of preparation. What happens when the state making those threats has gained combat experience, received foreign missiles, and learned how another army uses drones and artillery against cities and infrastructure?

Ukraine and Korea are different places. The terrain, alliances, weapons, distances, and political conditions are not the same. No responsible planner should copy one war into another. But that difference is not an excuse to dismiss battlefield learning. A mechanic does not need to rebuild the same truck to understand what a broken transmission teaches him. A military does not need to repeat every condition of Ukraine to benefit from lessons about targeting, concealment, logistics, air defense, and command under pressure.

This is why South Korea’s current policy has reached its limit. Seoul has provided humanitarian and nonlethal assistance in energy, infrastructure, health, medical care, and education while declining to supply lethal weapons. That assistance matters. It also does not stop missiles.

Ukraine has asked South Korea for air-defense systems. Air defense is not a decorative contribution to a foreign war. It protects hospitals, power crews, apartment buildings, factories, ports, and the families living near them. It gives civilians a better chance to remain in their homes and gives workers a better chance to keep water, electricity, food, and medical care moving.

South Korea’s constitutional and legal restrictions are real. They must be addressed through lawful public decisions, not waved away by executive convenience. But a legal restriction is not a moral conclusion. South Korean leaders still have to decide whether existing policy protects exposed communities or leaves them carrying the bill.

Seoul should expand lawful cooperation with Ukraine on air-defense knowledge, counter-drone defense, battlefield medicine, intelligence, training, and critical-infrastructure protection. Washington should support that effort through the alliance instead of treating every answer as another weapons package. The purpose should be specific: protect civilians, strengthen defensive capacity, and reduce the military value North Korea is gaining from its partnership with Russia.

The United States and South Korea also need to tell their own citizens what this preparation costs. More exercises, more air-defense networks, and more readiness demands place burdens on service members, military families, factory workers, port communities, and taxpayers. Those burdens may be necessary. They must not be hidden behind the clean language of deterrence. Leaders owe the public a plain account of who is exposed, who benefits, who pays, and what protection the policy actually delivers.

Russia and North Korea made this bargain. U.S. and South Korean leaders now decide whether Ukrainian civilians, enlisted families, and Korean communities will face its consequences without adequate protection.

The soldiers are being used as tuition. The families and civilians are paying the bill.
