A column making the rounds blames socialist labels and sneaky organizing for a string of Democratic primary upsets. David Marcus, writing in [DAVID MARCUS: In Middle America, socialist politics ages like milk](https://www.foxnews.com/opinion/david-marcus-middle-america-socialist-politics-ages-like-milk) for National Review, reports from Detroit and Denver that DSA-backed candidates like Melat Kiros and Abdul El-Sayed are winning through a three-step trick: year-round vote harvesting, nontraditional media, and a last-minute mainstream introduction that blindsides moderate voters. The thesis is that socialism is a con — the candidates hide, the organizers scheme, and by the time the nice people find out, it's too late.

Let me concede the real thing in here: some of these primaries have been low-profile races where the eventual winner had little name recognition going in. That is true. It also happens to insurgent candidates of every ideological stripe, including Republican ones. AOC beat Joe Crowley in 2018 because Crowley stopped showing up to his district and she organized door-to-door for months — volunteers from DSA and allied groups knocked on thousands of doors in a district where the incumbent had stopped doing any organizing at all. That's not socialism. That's a primary election.

But here's what Marcus never does, in the entire column: he never mentions a single policy position. Not one. He doesn't tell you what Melat Kiros wants to do for Denver. He doesn't tell you what Darializa Chevalier's platform looks like. He doesn't tell you what Abdul El-Sayed's healthcare position actually is. He tells you they're socialists. He tells you they're sneaky. He tells you they'll blindside you.

The word "socialist" is doing all the work, and it's doing it alone. And that's the trick — not the organizing. Marcus never asks the question that would collapse his thesis: if these policies are so unpopular, why does the DSA run on them? Because the answer — that they are popular, and that the label is the only thing that makes them look dangerous — is the one thing the column cannot afford to say.

What are these candidates actually running on? You'd have to look somewhere other than this column to find out. But here's a hint: rent stabilization exists in New York. Medicare exists in Florida. State-funded health insurance exists in Massachusetts. Labor protections exist in every state with a minimum wage above the federal floor. If wanting to extend these things makes you a socialist, then half the country has been socialist for decades, and the other half is about to be.

Marcus describes a three-step DSA playbook: organize year-round, use nontraditional media, introduce the candidate late. He calls this a trick. I'd call it a campaign. The year-round organizing is called showing up to community meetings. The nontraditional media is called talking to voters on the platforms they actually use. The late introduction is called a primary election with low turnout where motivated voters show up. Every successful insurgent campaign in American history has run this playbook. The only thing "socialist" about it is the label on the door.

And the label is the whole argument. Marcus never has to engage with what these candidates want because the word "socialist" does the engagement for him. It triggers the Cold War reflex. It conjures the gulag. It makes the reader flinch before the policy starts. That's not analysis. That's a reflex being pulled — by the column, not by the DSA.

The tell — the one that makes the whole column collapse — is the detail about Erick. Marcus tells us Erick lived in England for fifteen years. England. Where they have the National Health Service, universal coverage from cradle to grave, and where no Conservative government has ever proposed abolishing it. Erick lived inside the working model for a decade and a half and came home to Denver where his neighbors go bankrupt from a hospital bill. And Marcus wants you to understand that Erick thinks the people trying to fix that are crazy.

The craziness is a system that lets a hospital charge eight hundred dollars for a bag of saline. The craziness is a country where losing your job can mean losing your insurance, where a child's strep test is a sales transaction, where the richest nation on earth has decided the arithmetic of healthcare is unsolvable when every nation poorer than it solved it decades ago. The candidates Marcus calls crazy are running on the proposition that it shouldn't work that way. The word "socialist" is the only rebuttal, and it isn't one.

Here's what the organizing Marcus calls a trick actually looks like, when you strip the label off: neighbors talking to neighbors about what's broken. Candidates running on rent that devours half a paycheck and schools funded by property taxes that guarantee the rich kids get the best teachers. Voters in low-turnout primaries deciding they'd rather have someone who shows up than someone who hasn't held a town hall in six years. That's not a conspiracy. It's democracy. It's working exactly the way democracy works when people organize.

The thing Marcus can't say — the thing the label is designed to prevent him from saying — is that these candidates might be winning because they're right. Not right about everything. Not right in every detail. But right that the economy is a set of choices, not the weather, and that the people making those choices have been making them for people who don't live in Denver or the Bronx or Jackson Heights.

What to build instead: a politics that asks what people actually want — rent that doesn't devour a paycheck, a doctor that doesn't cost a year's savings, a primary that doesn't belong to the donor who shows up. The candidates Marcus calls socialists are the ones asking. The label is the answer to a question nobody asked.
