*The *Bruen* container expands for gun rights. It contracts for the drug war.*

The federal government criminalized tens of millions of Americans for behavior forty states have legalized. The Court knows it and does not care. *United States v. Hemani*, decided Thursday by a unanimous Supreme Court, holds that the prosecution of Ali Hemani — a routine marijuana user — under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(3) was inconsistent with the Second Amendment. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote the opinion. The Court that produced it has spent four years building a Second Amendment doctrine whose logic, applied honestly, compels results like this one.

But the press-corps coverage that calls this a victory for gun rights will miss the larger story. It is a victory for gun rights. It is also, and more consequentially, the Roberts Court’s latest deployment of the *Bruen* framework to dismantle the architecture of the drug war — not as a matter of drug policy, which the Court has never been willing to touch, but as a matter of gun policy, which the six-justice supermajority has made its own. The New Deal’s ghost haunts the Second Amendment. The Court is deploying a constitutional test it invented three years ago to undo, category by category, the regulatory edifice the New Deal Congress built.

The case arose from the 2022 search of Ali Hemani’s Texas home, where federal agents found a pistol and sixty grams of marijuana. Hemani told the agents he used marijuana “about every other day.” The government argued that this made him an “unlawful user of a controlled substance” under § 922(g)(3), a statute that does not define the term. Someone who uses once a year might qualify. Someone who uses every other day might not. The statute does not say. As this column noted when the Court signaled its skepticism at oral argument in March, the law was an unfixable mess. On Thursday, the Court unanimously held that it cannot be applied to this defendant.

Under *New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen*, 597 U.S. 1 (2022), a firearm regulation is constitutional only if the government can show it is “consistent with this Nation’s historical tradition of firearm regulation.” The government pointed to colonial and early-republic laws disarming “habitual drunkards.” Justice Gorsuch, writing for a unanimous Court, found those laws not “relevantly similar.” The founding generation disarmed a narrow category — persons whose habitual drunkenness was public and demonstrably dangerous — not every person who used alcohol. Section 922(g)(3) sweeps in every person who uses marijuana, including infrequent or private use, without requiring dangerousness. The gap is unbridgeable.

The steel-man for the government’s position, reconstructed in terms a working-bar attorney aligned with the government would recognize, runs like this: The founding generation disarmed persons it judged dangerous by habit. Colonial statutes disarmed “habitual drunkards” and persons “addicted to drunkenness.” The 1968 Congress made the same judgment about drug users, a category the founders could not have anticipated. The relevant tradition is not limited to specific substances but to the principle that the government may disarm the habitually dangerous. The firearm is the constant; the intoxicant is the variable. Modern marijuana use is the variable.

The Court’s audit was devastating and brief. The historical analogues are too narrow. The colonial drunkard laws disarmed those whose habit was demonstrated, not everyone who drank. The modern statute prohibits firearm possession by anyone the government designates an “unlawful user” — a term that, as applied, has no definitional boundary. The distinction between a finding of habitual dangerousness and a categorical status prohibition is the distinction between a specific evidentiary showing and a legislative fiat. The founders required the former; Congress enacted the latter. Gorsuch’s opinion explicitly reserves judgment on whether Congress could disarm addicts, the presently intoxicated, or those who receive individualized dangerousness findings. The opinion is narrow in its holding, as the justice writes, but its reasoning is the kind of narrow-but-not-really opinion this Court has perfected: the rationale’s logical reach extends far beyond the case at hand.

The asymmetry is instructive. *Bruen*’s history-and-tradition framework is not a neutral methodology. It is a doctrinal container that expands and contracts to serve the outcome. When the Court wants to invalidate a regulation, the historical analogues must be “relevantly similar” at a level of specificity the government cannot meet. When the Court wants to uphold one — as in *United States v. Rahimi*, 602 U.S. ___ (2024), sustaining a domestic-violence restraining-order prohibition — the analogues need only establish a general principle. The container is whatever serves the outcome. In *Hemani*, the outcome served the coalition’s gun-rights commitments. The drug-war architecture that fell was incidental, and the Court did not pause to mourn it.

The architecture that fell is significant. The statute is the same law used to convict President Biden’s son Hunter in 2024, illustrating the bipartisan sweep of a prohibition that can turn both a president’s son and a Texas construction worker into federal felons for conduct a large fraction of the country considers ordinary. The government argued for a law that catches both. The Court, unanimously, said no.

What the Court built in *Hemani* is not just a ruling for Ali Hemani. It is a template for dismantling the modern federal gun-control apparatus category by category. Section 922(g) contains nine categories of prohibited persons. The felon-dispossession statute, § 922(g)(1), is the most consequential — and the most vulnerable. Under one prominent reading, the founding generation did not disarm convicted felons as a categorical matter but only those judged dangerous by specific findings. The gap between that practice and the modern statute is at least as wide as the one the Court condemned in *Hemani*. The litigation is already in the pipeline, and the Court has signaled it may be receptive. The doctrine will not stop here.

The Department of Justice should have seen this coming. The constitutional answer to a federal statute that criminalizes conduct forty states have legalized is that the statute, as applied, violates the Second Amendment. The Court has now unanimously said so. The six-justice supermajority has made its choice. The drug war’s last gun prohibition has fallen. The rest of the New Deal’s edifice is next.
