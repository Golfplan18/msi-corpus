Let me be plain about what happened in a Detroit meeting room this month. A Muslim public-health doctor and Rhodes Scholar who is also a serious candidate for the United States Senate sat down with a roomful of Christian pastors, a rabbi, and an imam, and asked them a question straight out of both the Hippocratic Oath and the Sermon on the Mount: if you can stop someone else from going hungry, what excuse do you have not to? Then he answered his own question in language my own tradition recognizes instantly — the obligation to act flows from the obligation to care.

The faith tradition he invoked indicts him and his party more than it helps them.

---

Amos 5:21–24 is the passage Democrats love to deploy against the Religious Right. The prophet speaking for God:

"I hate, I despise your religious festivals; your assemblies are a stench to me. Even though you bring me burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them. Though you bring choice fellowship offerings, I will have no regard for them. Away with the noise of your songs! I will not listen to the music of your harps. But let justice roll on like a river, righteousness like a never-failing stream."

Democrats cite this passage and feel vindicated. But let me read it plainly, without the political lens. Amos is not issuing a policy correction. He is delivering a divine rejection of religious performance divorced from justice. The worship is real. The festivals are real. The offerings are real. And God says He will not accept them. Not because the ritual is wrong, but because the justice is missing. The Hebrew is concrete. *Mishpat* is the work of setting things right — not speeches about setting things right, not campaign platforms about setting things right. The hungry fed. The stranger welcomed. The naked clothed. The work itself.

If Democrats invoke Amos to justify their economic platform, they have opened themselves to the judgment of the passage they are citing. The passage does not say "let your policy positions roll on like a river." It says justice. And justice is not a position; it is a practice.

---

Here is the convergence neither party wants to name. The Evangelical Right cited Scripture while its policies produced the very conditions the prophets condemned. I have spent thirty years inside that machine. I know what it built. I know what it cost. Between 1979 and the present, the Southern Baptist Convention was captured through a coordinated trustee-and-presidency strategy conceived by Paul Pressler and Paige Patterson; the 2000 Baptist Faith and Message revision restricted the office of pastor to men; the 2022 Guidepost Solutions investigation found the Executive Committee had maintained a list of approximately 700 named offenders for two decades, told survivors their abuse was a private matter, and threatened with litigation those who tried to surface the pattern. At the state level, the Michigan-specific evidence is concrete and traceable: the 2023 work-requirement provisions of the state's Medicaid expansion (signed December 2023, Healthy Michigan Plan), the 100-month lifetime cap on cash assistance tightened in 2024, the rejection of federal child-care subsidy expansion in three successive Mackinac Center–briefed legislative cycles. The pattern is the same as the Southern one — a captured operation with named institutional carriers, deploying specific texts in specific captured readings, against specific named populations the operation is built to harm.

The Democratic cities Progressive faith rhetoric claims to champion have produced the same poverty the prophets condemn. That is not a talking-point claim; it is a documented record. Detroit's poverty rate exceeded 30 percent across the 2020–2024 American Community Survey five-year estimates; the city's homelessness services budget was cut 14 percent in the 2023 fiscal year while the police budget grew; the water shutoff moratorium ended in 2020 and roughly 9,000 households have faced shutoffs since. Baltimore's poverty rate held near 20 percent across the same window, with the city's vacant-property count exceeding 13,000 in the 2024 housing department inventory. Philadelphia's poverty rate sits near 22 percent; the Kensington open-air drug market operates a block from a city-council district whose representative campaigns on Matthew 25. Chicago's poverty rate runs near 17 percent citywide and exceeds 35 percent on the South and West sides in the same ACS series. The hungry go unfed, the stranger is unwelcomed, and the homeless sleep under overpasses in cities whose elected leadership has spent decades invoking the very prophets who condemn the neglect.

The Evangelical Right did the same: cited Scripture while its policies concentrated wealth and shredded the safety net, while children went hungry and the sick died without care. Both sides instrumentalize faith. Both sides use it as political cover. The prophetic tradition indicts both.

Matthew 25 is the standard Progressive text — "whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me." But read it plainly, without the political lens. The criterion is not policy position. It is the hungry fed, the thirsty given drink, the stranger welcomed, the naked clothed, the sick visited, the imprisoned visited. Not advocated for in a resolution. Done. In the same Detroit whose poverty rate exceeds 30 percent. In the same Baltimore whose vacant-house count exceeds 13,000. In the same Kensington whose open-air drug market operates under a Democratic city council. In the same Chicago whose South and West sides have poverty rates above 35 percent. Progressive Democrats have invoked this tradition for decades. They have presided over the failure the passage judges.

And here is what the Democrats especially do not want to hear: the text they are invoking does not exempt them. It never did. The text does not care about your political tribe. It asks only one question: did you do justice?

---

The prophets do not distinguish between sincere and instrumental faith. They distinguish between faith that produces justice and faith that does not. El-Sayed's question in that Detroit room — what does accountability to something bigger mean for what you do for the people around you? — is a real question. But it is a question the prophetic tradition answers with a standard, not a speech. The Hebrew is *mishpat* and *tsedaqah* — justice in the gates, the active defense of the vulnerable, and righteousness as right relationship, the fabric of mutual obligation that holds a community together. The prophet is standing in the royal sanctuary at Bethel, the state-adjacent shrine of the northern kingdom, and saying that God is disgusted by the worship of a nation that feeds the rich and starves the poor. The plain reading is not subtle. The captured-operation's reading, which has been deployed for half a century to baptize tax cuts and block Medicaid expansion and gut the social safety net, is the opposite of the plain reading. The gap between them is the subject of my life's work.

And here is what the captured Evangelical machine does not want to face: a Muslim public-health scientist who reads the CDC's *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report* the way I read the Gospel of Luke, who frames feeding the hungry as a direct moral obligation, who talks about what holding yourself accountable to something bigger than you requires of you toward the people around you — this is a candidate the machine does not have a box for. The prophetic tradition does not take the blessing without the judgment. It does not distinguish between sincere and instrumental faith. It distinguishes between faith that produces justice and faith that does not. El-Sayed's question in that Detroit room is a real question. But it is a question the prophetic tradition answers with a standard, not a stump speech.

And if the Democrats cannot let the text demand something of them — something harder than a meeting with faith leaders, something harder than an invocation of the least of these on the campaign trail, something harder than a budget that grows the police bureau while it cuts the homelessness services line — then they have made the text into a campaign pamphlet. And Amos has already told us what God thinks of that. So has the homeless person sleeping under an overpass in a city that invokes him. So has the diabetic whose A1C climbed past 12 because a pastor told her the medication was a lack of faith. So has the patient with sickle cell disease who cannot get into a hematologist in a state whose legislature refused the expansion that would have funded the clinic.

The prophetic tradition indicts both operations. The question the Detroit meeting room asked is the right question. That answer is not the final one. The texts are still there. The prophets are still readable. The hungry are still hungry. And the standard still asks the same question, of the same operation, with the same answer:
