The American military forced Korean civilians into bomb shelters on Thursday afternoon because it could not be bothered to tell the border guards a U.S. drone was coming.

That is what happened near Cheorwon, a small city in Gangwon province, south of the Demilitarized Zone. People who live in that corridor, under the constant shadow of North Korean artillery, received emergency alerts at about 2:30 p.m. telling them to move to shelters. A drone had been detected. Nobody knew whose it was.

These are people with routines. People with dinner on the stove, with kids just home from school, with shifts that start at six in the morning. They got a message saying get underground now. They got underground. That is what you do when the military tells you to move and you live within artillery range of Pyongyang.

It took one hour for the Joint Chiefs to confirm the aircraft was a U.S. drone running a combined training exercise with South Korea's 1st Marine Division. One hour. In a shop, if I told a customer his brake job would take an hour because my parts guy forgot to tell the service writer a caliper was coming in, that customer would rightly wonder what else we are forgetting. An hour on the most heavily armed border on the planet, with civilians in shelters, is not a scheduling hiccup. It is a failure of the most basic coordination that any combined operation requires.

U.S. Forces Korea said the Marine Corps was "assessing the circumstances" and "remains in coordination" with its South Korean counterparts. That is the language of people who know they owe an explanation but do not yet have one.

South Korean officials said further investigation was needed to figure out the drone's specific unit and why nobody could identify it right away. In plain terms: nobody is sure whose aircraft it was or whether the paperwork reached everyone who needed it. It remains unclear whether the flight plan had been shared in advance with the South Korean units responsible for monitoring the border. The Joint Chiefs themselves acknowledged that incomplete communication about the exercise may have caused the confusion.

So let me say that plainly. A combined military force running a training exercise near the most dangerous border on the Korean Peninsula did not confirm, before sending the aircraft up, that every unit watching that border knew it was coming. The flight plan, or the lack of one, is the whole story. Not a weapons malfunction. Not an adversary. Not an intelligence failure. A failure to pass a piece of paper from one desk to another.

I have spent twenty years working on machines. The lesson is always the same: the expensive part breaks less often than the cheap part everyone assumes will just work. A gasket costs three dollars. A transmission costs four thousand. When the gasket fails, the transmission is useless. Communication between allied forces on the DMZ is the gasket. The drones, the radar systems, the logistics exercises, the F-35s and HIMARS batteries, those are the transmission. The transmission was working fine on Thursday. The gasket was not.

This month, the U.S. and South Korea launched their largest-ever joint logistics exercise. More platforms, more systems, more coordination demands layered on top of each other. Each new system needs the one next to it to know it exists. Each new exercise adds another sheet of flight plans, another set of units that need to be told what is coming. And on a Thursday afternoon, in one of the most surveilled corridors on earth, the system could not tell a friendly drone from a threat in the time it took a town to clear its shelters.

The defense contractors who build these systems sell them as solutions. They are solutions, for the piece of the problem they address. What they do not sell, because there is no profit margin in it, is the communication architecture that makes the whole machine work as one. The drone works. The radar works. The alert system works, as Cheorwon learned. What did not work was the piece in between, the part that says to every unit on the border, every morning, here is what is flying today.
