ICE hired David Brouillette despite a documented history of domestic violence and serious mental illness, handed him a badge and a gun, and on July 13 he shot Joan Sebastián Durán Guerrero dead at a traffic stop in Maine. As reported on July 16, Brouillette's ex-wife had already described a pattern of violent behavior to relatives. Now she has told NPR the same thing: the man she was married to should never have been given a badge, and the agency that gave him one either didn't look or didn't care what it would cost someone else.

This is not an isolated mistake. ICE says it has hired more than 12,000 officers in under a year. Gil Kerlikowske, the former commissioner of U.S. Customs and Border Protection, told NPR flatly: "This is a person that never should have been hired." Kerlikowske knows what a real vetting process looks like, because he ran the agency that became ICE's sister operation. He had to resist Congress when it demanded faster hiring — "The complaints would always be like, 'Well, you still have a thousand agents you need to hire.' You could explain, though, to members of Congress, to the appropriators, that, 'Look, we don't want to rush the hiring process,'" Kerlikowske told NPR. He understood that cutting corners on who you arm and put on the street produces exactly the kind of catastrophe we are now seeing.

Kenneth Corey, who oversaw psychological screening at the NYPD, described what proper vetting requires: contacting former employers, pulling disciplinary records, checking with every police department in every jurisdiction a candidate has ever lived. That means flagging domestic incident reports, and records of any hospital transport for a mental-health crisis. Brouillette's history was not hidden. His ex-wife knew it. The records, if anyone bothered to look, would have screamed it. ICE either ran a vetting process so hollow that it missed what a phone call would have surfaced, or it saw the red flags and decided the quota mattered more. Either way, the agency armed a domestic abuser with a government-issued firearm and turned him loose on a public it is supposed to protect. The man who paid for that decision with his life was a 27-year-old immigrant whose family says he was on his way to work.

The agency's public response is a hand-waving sentence about applying "proper personnel vetting regulations" with no specifics, no admission that anything went wrong, and no promise to find out how many other Brouillettes it has hired in the last year. That is not a statement from an organization conducting an honest review. That is an organization running the clock, hoping the next news cycle buries the bodies.

There is a reason police departments that have been in this business for a century have learned, through disaster after disaster, that rushing hiring is a machine for wrecking public trust. When Washington, D.C., rushed to hire hundreds of officers in the late 1980s, members of that class were disciplined, arrested, and fired in record numbers. When Miami doubled its force in two years to fight crime, dozens of newly hired officers were later convicted of stealing drugs from traffickers. Chuck Wexler, executive director of the Police Executive Research Forum, put it this way: "What police departments have learned over the years is that one officer hired without a proper background investigation, including a thorough psychological examination, can severely damage a police department's credibility with its community." And after the September 11 attacks, CBP itself hired so fast and so recklessly that it later admitted it had put drug cartel members into the ranks. The pattern is unbroken: speed and scale, when imposed by political pressure, produce officers who should never have been near a badge. That is what happens when political pressure overrides vetting.

That is the machine the administration built. It demanded 12,000 new officers in a year because it wanted a deportation apparatus, not a law-enforcement agency. It wanted volume. It got volume. Last week another ICE officer killed another man in Texas, and the mother is still waiting for accountability — the same pattern, the same shielded agency, the same refusal to name what the body count means. The price is paid by the Brown and Black communities who become the surplus in the arithmetic, and by any American who believes that the people the government gives a gun should be screened with more care than it takes to open a warehouse.

In 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. eulogized the four children murdered in the Birmingham church bombing by saying: "We must be concerned not merely about who murdered them, but about the system, the way of life, the philosophy which produced the murderers." David Brouillette pulled the trigger. The system that put him in position to pull it is an agency that chose speed over safety, that promoted volume over vetting, and that treated the lives the swelling officer corps would encounter as acceptable losses — and the beneficiary of that design is the political project that values removal numbers over human lives, the same project that spent the last year campaigning on turning ICE into the country's largest police force.

Until the agency names every officer hired in that blitz whose background check, if one was done at all, missed what Brouillette's records would have shown, every traffic stop this force conducts is a roll of the dice that someone else won't survive.
