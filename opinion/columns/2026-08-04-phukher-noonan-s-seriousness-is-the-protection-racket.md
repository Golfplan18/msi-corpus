Peggy Noonan launders a protection racket as seriousness.

That is the operation in her *Wall Street Journal* column, “Socialism Is Here—and It’s Serious,” published August 6, 2026. She opens by announcing that socialism “must be taken seriously as a factor in our politics and in the Democratic Party,” then spends the next eight hundred words dismissing the people who hold it as “colder, less human, fiercer and occasionally sociopathic.” The seriousness is the costume. The scare campaign is the content.

I built versions of this move in the cable years, so I know the machinery. Noonan’s piece walks the reader through a sequence of rhetorical deployments: define the threatening category before anyone can examine it, select the most extreme available example, acknowledge the grievance without answering it, naturalize the existing arrangement, borrow literary authority for the conclusion, and close with a civilizational alarm disguised as understatement.

> “Socialism is not a passing thing but an arriving thing. It must be taken seriously as a factor in our politics and in the Democratic Party. A CNN/SSRS poll issued this week reported 34% of Democrats and Democratic-leaning adults identify as ‘democratic socialists.’”
>
> *— Peggy Noonan, “Socialism Is Here—and It’s Serious,”* Wall Street Journal*, opening paragraphs*

The opening move is the declaration of seriousness, and it is the single most load-bearing sentence in the piece. Watch what it purchases. By announcing that socialism “must be taken seriously,” Noonan earns the right not to take it seriously for the remainder of the column. Every dismissal that follows arrives wearing the credential of the opening concern.

The technique is **frame-engineered relabeling**, catalogued in the WSJ technique inventory at §4.1 and in Frank Luntz’s *Words That Work*. “Democratic socialists” are folded into the single word “socialism,” and that word is made to carry two histories that are not the same: Scandinavian social democracy, with national health care and generous benefits, and Soviet communism, with shortages and secret police. The 34 percent are quietly turned into Stalinists without the reader noticing the substitution.

The same sentence runs the **multiple-audience-targeting analytic** catalogued at WSJ §4.3. The technocratic reader gets a poll citation. The populist reader gets a scare word. The establishment reader gets alarm. The political class gets a respectable warning that can be repeated in serious rooms. Nobody is invited to ask what those 34 percent actually want.

The object of the exercise is to make you afraid of a number instead of curious about it. The “we must take this seriously” is the long con’s opening handshake. Noonan has named the category and already decided what the category means. That is not taking it seriously. That is installing the frame before the argument starts.

> “I am sorry to say they never strike me with their warmth, as, say, your great-uncle Billy was warm when he spoke about the Industrial Workers of the World. Today’s socialists strike me as colder, less human, fiercer and occasionally sociopathic. In general, they appear to have an idea of history rather than attachments to existing people and institutions.”
>
> *— paragraph 6, the character sketch*

Here is the paragraph the whole piece is built to license. It is **dehumanization**, Bandura’s seventh mechanism of moral disengagement, with an ad hominem doing the visible work. “Less human” is on the page. “Sociopathic” is on the page. The argument has been relocated from policy to the alleged moral character of the people who hold the policy.

Operators have a name for this move: the **humanity tax**. You levy a tax on the target’s claim to full personhood, and the reader who absorbs “less human” no longer owes the argument a single honest look. The bad-faith catalog files the substitution under ad hominem; Bandura supplies the mechanism. The character hit arrives before policy engagement because the character hit replaces policy engagement.

The great-uncle Billy comparison adds an **advantageous comparison**. The old socialists were warm, harmless, and safely historical. These new ones are cold, fierce, and possibly disordered. The acceptable dead are separated from the monstrous living. It is an invidious contrast dressed as nostalgia.

Noonan is not analyzing socialists in this paragraph. She is converting political opponents into a psychological problem. Once the reader accepts the label, no further engagement with their ideas is required. That is how the dismissers get to feel virtuous.

> “Our current socialists have never created or ‘liberated’ anything. They are dreaming it up as they go along—ban prisons, ban prisoners, ban crime.”
>
> *— penultimate paragraph, the policy reduction*

This is the **selectional strawman**, the technique catalogued at WSJ §4.6. Noonan’s own piece has already quoted the actual exchange from *Fox News Sunday*, in which Megan Romer, the DSA national co-chair, answered questions about abolition “as part of a long-term program.” Then, near the close, all of that is flattened into “ban prisons, ban prisoners, ban crime.”

“Ban crime” is the giveaway. No one proposed banning crime. It is a nursery-rhyme reduction designed to make engagement look absurd. The qualifier disappears. The long-term program disappears. The organization’s actual bills, budgets, programs, and arguments disappear. What remains is the loudest fragment, stripped of context and presented as the whole platform.

The receipt is on the page twenty inches above. Noonan quotes the qualifier and then proceeds as if it had never been said. This is hostile cropping: not merely selecting an excerpt, but selecting it in a way that defeats the meaning of the excerpt she selected.

I know this move. The preamble is always magisterial — normally the radicals are evasive or euphemistic; this one was refreshingly honest — and the audience is meant to feel that it has just heard a confession. It has not heard a confession. It has heard a selection.

The operator’s version is simple: stack the segment with the most extreme formulation you can find, drop the qualifier, and let the audience’s outrage do the analytical work your argument refuses to do. “Ban crime” is not a proposal anyone made. It is the strawman’s strawman, erected so the reader can feel that engaging the young would be beneath them.

> “A voter who is 25 was in grade school during the 2008 economic crash, and saw everything—frightened parents, lost jobs. In the years after, he saw bailouts and no one on Wall Street paying a price. It lessened his allegiance to the free-market economic system. A voter who is 25 also sees AI rising, and wonders if the economy as it exists today has a place for him. Will he be able to afford a house someday?”
>
> *— the diagnosis section, paragraphs 9–10*

This is the most honest paragraph in the piece, and the honesty is itself the operation.

Concede the strongest version of the case. The 2008 crash happened. The bailouts happened. No one on Wall Street paid a price, as Noonan’s account puts it. A 25-year-old really did watch frightened parents lose jobs. AI really is rising. Housing really is unaffordable for many young people. The question of whether the economy has a place for them is not adolescent theater. It is the question.

Noonan also names the collapse of confidence in Congress, the media, universities, churches, and courts. She notes that when people stop believing institutions can be repaired, reform movements grow more radical. She observes that the Cold War has receded from living memory, so “socialism” no longer means the Soviet Union to many young people; it means national health care and a society that catches those who fall. She notices the decline of religion, the rise of political frameworks organized around power, oppression, and intersectionality, and the way social media rewards ideological intensity on both sides.

Every one of those observations can be true. That is why this is more dangerous than a simple rant. A scare campaign that cannot see its target is merely noise. One that can see the target clearly is a threat.

Watch what the concession buys her. The 25-year-old is made into a case study to be understood and set aside, not a citizen to be answered. The nearest thing to an answer is Noonan’s mixed-economy paragraph: “This mixed economy is a mess—imperfect and costly, always in need of reform, always capable of being made better and, in terms of history, maybe as good as it gets.”

That is not an answer to a person who cannot afford a house. It is a defense of the present dressed as humility.

The technique is **smuggled-premise grievance acknowledgment**. Acknowledge the pain, deny the prescription, and trust the reader to stop at the acknowledgment. The grievances exist in the column for one purpose: so the reader can feel reasonable while rejecting the conclusion the evidence points toward.

It is the rhetorical equivalent of a doctor acknowledging your symptoms and then prescribing the disease. The young voter’s wound is named so the wounded can be dismissed. The dismissal is prepared six paragraphs earlier: the people who want change are “less human,” “fiercer,” and “occasionally sociopathic.”

The diagnosis is real. The answer is withheld. That is the smuggle.

> “It’s our view here that free-market capitalism is organic and flows upward from the human person—man wants to build, trade, invent, provide for and rise, and he erects laws and a way of economic being that allows the realization of those desires. Economic freedom works with human nature. The rules and regulations it produces, the economic framework it creates, are like what Churchill said of democracy: the worst system except for all the others. Socialism is a more abstract and ideological vision of justice that doesn’t flow up but must be imposed from the top down. It isn’t based on the natural, or on man as he is; it’s a construct that involves coercion.”
>
> *— the “organic” passage, paragraphs 14–15*

This is **appeal to nature**, the bad-faith catalog’s naturalistic-fallacy entry, combined with **naturalization of a constructed system**.

Capitalism is called “organic,” something that “flows upward from the human person” and “works with human nature.” Socialism is “abstract,” “ideological,” “imposed from the top down,” and “a construct that involves coercion.” The entire argument is carried in the adjectives. Natural is good. Constructed is bad. Upward is human. Downward is coercive.

The receipt is the paragraph’s own vocabulary. “Organic” and “from the top down” are not evidence. They are gravity being installed.

Then comes Churchill’s “worst system except for all the others,” the pre-armed rebuttal. Noonan concedes the imperfection in advance so no one can raise it later. The paragraph gets to have its naturalism and its realism at once.

But capitalism is not gravity. It is a human arrangement, built through centuries of law, property rules, coercion, enclosure, litigation, subsidy, public infrastructure, and bailout. The “natural” arrangement was constructed by people and defended by institutions. The welfare state Noonan concedes as humane is also a human construction. The distinction is not natural versus constructed. It is which construction she wants the reader to experience as nature.

Operators call this the **gravity trick**. Make your preferred arrangement feel like a force of nature, and deviating from it feels like defying physics rather than making a different human choice. The freedom of property is renamed human nature. The freedom from poverty is renamed ideology. The arrangement that keeps wealth where it is survives one more year.

> “I’ve been reading André Malraux this summer. He was a brilliant, compelling and tricky fellow … At a Gaullist campaign rally in 1968, Malraux put his finger on a weakness of conservative economic thought. ‘Capitalism’ does not carry us in its heart,’ he said. By inference, it’s also hard to carry capitalism in your heart. It isn’t a great belief, only an economic system. Socialism is a belief, and it summons feeling. Capitalism is a thing of reason, a disadvantage in an age of sensation.”
>
> *— the Malraux passage, paragraphs 17–20*

This is **authority laundering**, an appeal to authority with literary gloves on. Noonan summons André Malraux — brilliant, compelling, tricky, disentangled from communism, and later associated with de Gaulle — to license a partisan scare argument as cosmopolitan depth. The source is Olivier Todd’s *Malraux: A Life*, and the column also borrows Malraux’s contempt for Mitterrand as “a man who has never liberated anything,” a figure of “touching aspiration and predictable demagogy” living in “an attitude of eternal intention.”

The move is not subtle. A literary figure’s credibility is borrowed to reframe socialism as an aesthetic and spiritual failure rather than a policy response to the six grievances Noonan has just listed and refused to engage.

But the borrowed authority cuts the other way. Malraux’s line — “Capitalism does not carry us in its heart” — is supposed to demonstrate socialism’s emotional weakness. Instead it reveals the weakness of the tradition Noonan is defending. Capitalism is presented as a thing of reason, “a disadvantage in an age of sensation.” The page has nothing to carry in the heart, so it inflates the thing it fears.

The reformed believer is the one you call when you need an appeal discredited from the inside. The genius of the escape hatch is that the admission arrives as a quotation, so the writer can disown it as borrowed. The Malraux wrapper is the laundering. The content beneath it is fear, and the fear is the most honest thing in the piece.

The literary passage also supplies a characterological echo. American socialists, Noonan writes, have “never created or liberated anything,” as though the young people who watched a financial crash, bailouts, AI displacement, and housing costs are merely dreaming. The quotations perform sophistication while the underlying argument remains the same: these people feel too much, think too little, and have never made anything worth hearing.

> “So far they’ve never proved a thing—except that they can win elections. And thus carry the future. Which is rather a lot.”
>
> *— the closing line*

This is the **threat-inflation closer**, catalogued at WSJ §4.13 and carried by the lifting-line cadence described at §3.5: the short, apparently modest sentence designed to be posted, repeated, and lodged in the scanner’s memory.

“They carry the future. Which is rather a lot” escalates a disagreement over policy into a transformation of America’s fundamental nature while performing a shrug. The understatement is the cover for the inflation. The column opens with “must be taken seriously” and closes with “they carry the future.” Seriousness resolves into alarm.

The page’s own editor, William H. Grimes, wrote in 1951 that “on our editorial page we make no pretense of walking down the middle of the road.” That honesty deserves to be retained. This is not neutral analysis. It is a definite point of view, and the point of view has a recurring structure: protect the freedom of property, describe the freedom from poverty as coercion, and call the arrangement that preserves the first one human nature.

So here is what the eight hundred words actually are, taken together. They are a permission structure for the people who have sat on top of the last forty years. The column tells its own readership that the rising generation’s legitimate grievance is the product of sociopathy, that one word — “socialism” — can stand in for every argument, and that no reader ever has to ask why a 25-year-old who watched the bailout watched no one pay a price.

Saying “we must take this seriously” and then calling the people who hold it “less human” is the exact move the operators used to run. Dress the fear in concern, and the reader mistakes the concern for the substance. Name the wound, deny the cure, and call the patient irrational for reaching toward one.

I helped write sentences like this. I know the machinery because I ran the machine. This column takes a real number — a third of a party saying it wants something different from the economic arrangement it has — and converts that number into the Gulag on the Potomac.

Name the six grievances. Check. Validate them. Check. Then treat the only available fix as totalitarian madness while the causes of the grievances — the bailouts with no one paying a price, the displacement, the institutions that cannot be repaired — stay exactly where they are.

That is not seriousness. It is a con.

The freedom being defended on this page was never for the people who cannot afford the next test result. It was for the people who own the hospital. And it always was. The 34 percent Noonan fears did not wake up one morning wanting to abolish the Senate. They woke up wanting to afford a hospital visit.

The difference between what they want and what she says they want is not an argument. It is the entire operation.

And here is the mirror: Noonan’s own last honest line says capitalism is “a disadvantage in an age of sensation.” The operator on the page already knows the heart war is lost. The scare campaign is what remains when the argument is gone — the last resort of a tradition with nothing to carry in its heart except the fear of losing a future it has already lost.

Noonan is not afraid of socialism.

She is afraid of being answered.
