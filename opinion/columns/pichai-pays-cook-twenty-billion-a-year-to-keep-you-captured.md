Sundar Pichai pays Tim Cook twenty billion dollars a year to keep you captured. The figure is not estimation; it is the figure the Apple executive Eddy Cue testified to in the *United States v. Google LLC* trial record, anchored to fiscal year 2022, and it is the figure on which Judge Amit Mehta's August 2024 monopolization ruling under Section 2 of the Sherman Act — the antitrust statute that bars monopolization of a market by exclusionary conduct rather than by competition on the merits — turns. Twenty billion dollars per year, paid by one company to another, for the single purpose of arranging that the search box on every iPhone in the world arrives pre-filled with the name Google. The arrangement at the center of the case is not exotic. It is a distribution deal. The thing being distributed is the default; the thing being foreclosed is everyone else.

It is true, and Google's lawyers were at pains to say it, that nothing in the technical architecture of either the iPhone or Safari prevents a user from changing the default search engine. The point Mehta's 174-page opinion makes in detail — the opinion that found Google liable on the general internet search and the search-text advertising markets — is that the technical possibility of changing the default is not the same as the practical reality of search-market competition, because the default is the market. The user whose phone arrives with the box already filled in, who has not been asked, who would have to perform a deliberate act to change it, and who has been given no reason by the surrounding interface to suppose that the deliberate act would be rewarded with a better outcome, does not change the default; the trial record's behavioral data on this point is unsparing. What Google has bought, with the twenty billion dollars a year, is the right to convert distribution into share, and share into a defense of share. Anything else would be cheaper if it worked. It does not work, because the alternative — making a search product so much better than what is on offer that the user would seek it out — is not, in this market, the cheaper option. It is by some orders of magnitude more expensive. So the twenty billion dollars a year is paid, and the search box stays Google.

The thing that has to be named precisely, because the press summary papers over it, is that Google's case is not that the default did not have the effect alleged. Google's case, made at trial and rehearsed in the September 2025 remedies briefing, is that the company would have won the search-market share anyway on the merits, and so the default-distribution agreements are not the cause of the share. Mehta's August 2024 opinion takes this argument seriously and rejects it on the record. The exclusive default agreements with Apple, with Samsung, with Mozilla, and with the U.S. wireless carriers collectively cover, in Mehta's own enumeration, every meaningful avenue by which a user might encounter a search engine for the first time. A rival search engine, in such a market, has nowhere to be defaulted to; nowhere to be tried; nowhere to accumulate the user-interaction data that would let its ranking model improve. The foreclosure is not a side effect. It is the mechanism. The twenty billion dollars a year is what the foreclosure costs.

What Mehta then did with this finding, in the remedies decision of September 2, 2025, is the part of the case that has to be sat with carefully, because it is the part that decides whether the liability finding produces any consequence at all. The Department of Justice asked the court to order three structural remedies: divestiture of Chrome, on the reasoning that the browser is the choke-point at which Google routes its search traffic and that no behavioral order can sever the integration without the structural break; a ban on the default-search payments to distributors, on the reasoning that the foreclosure mechanism is the payment itself and that the payment will reconstitute the foreclosure under any other label; and data-sharing requirements that would give rival search engines access to the search index and user-interaction data on which competitive ranking models could be built. Mehta denied the first. Mehta denied the second, allowing the default-search payments to continue subject to annual renegotiation rather than fixed multi-year contracts — a procedural adjustment that leaves the substantive arrangement intact. Mehta granted the third, narrowly, requiring Google to share portions of its search index and aggregated user-interaction data with "qualified competitors" — a designation that a five-person technical committee will sort out over a six-year supervisory framework — and not with everyone who would build on it. The remedy, in shape, is the Microsoft 2001 consent decree dressed in slightly different clothes; the doctrinal architecture Cory Doctorow named in his *Pluralistic* post the morning after as the worst possible outcome, and that Tim Wu had named seven years earlier in *The Curse of Bigness* as the precedent that prophesied this one. Behavioral remedies, in platform-monopoly cases, reproduce the conduct they nominally constrain. The company that has paid twenty billion dollars a year to be the default will, under the remedy, continue to pay twenty billion dollars a year to be the default, renegotiated annually with the same counterparty for the same purpose. The data-sharing requirement gives a few competitors access to a few slices of the data the company has accumulated over a quarter-century of being the default; it does not give them the default.

The cross-appeal the Department of Justice filed by the February 3, 2026 deadline is the document of record on what the second Trump administration's antitrust division is asking the D.C. Circuit to reconsider; the Notice of Appeal Google filed on January 16, 2026 is the document of record on what Google is contesting. Assistant Attorney General Abigail Slater's public statement on the day of the remedies decision called the Mehta order a department win — which, on the narrow ground that the court found liability and ordered something rather than nothing, it was — while reserving the right to seek "additional relief." The "additional relief" is the structural remedy the remedies decision declined. What the second Trump administration has signaled is that the symmetric-application discipline the case requires — the discipline of treating Google in 2026 the way the Department of Justice treated Microsoft in 1998, on the same theory of foreclosure-by-exclusive-default — is partly available and partly withheld. The Biden administration's antitrust division under Jonathan Kanter brought the case at the liability stage and pressed for the structural remedy at the remedies stage; the second Trump administration's antitrust division under Slater accepted the behavioral remedy and is now appealing on the narrower question of whether the behavioral remedy is enough. The continuity is real. The substantive question — whether the case will produce a competitive search market or merely a documented monopoly — turns on what the D.C. Circuit does with the cross-appeal, and on what the cross-appeal asks for.

Lina Khan's 2017 *Yale Law Journal* note "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" is the load-bearing scholarship the remedies decision declines to adopt. Khan's argument is structural: the consumer-welfare standard the U.S. antitrust apparatus inherited from the Chicago revision of the 1970s cannot capture platform-monopoly conduct, because the platform monopoly does not extract its rent through prices the consumer pays at the surface; it extracts the rent through the architecture, through the default, through the foreclosure of the alternatives the consumer cannot see. The substantive remedy, under this analysis, is the structural break — the divestiture that severs the integrated mechanism — because behavioral remedies leave the architecture intact and the architecture is what produces the rent. The Mehta remedy adopts the consumer-welfare-frame conclusion without adopting the Khan-frame premise: behavioral remedies are inadequate to the foreclosure the same opinion documented at the liability stage, but the structural remedy is denied on the reasoning that the rise of generative-AI competitors (ChatGPT, the perplexity-style entrants) has "changed the course of this case." It has not. The generative-AI entrants are competitors for a different product, downstream of the same search-data substrate Google controls, and any of them that grow large enough to constrain Google's search-market conduct will themselves face the same default-distribution choke-point and will themselves be candidates for the same twenty-billion-dollar arrangement. Anthropic is already partnered with Amazon. OpenAI is partnered with Microsoft, whose Bing the original case identified as the failed counterfactual. The substitution of one default arrangement for another is not competition. It is rotation among the same small number of firms with the capacity to pay the default fee.

The right remedy in a foreclosure-by-default case is to end the foreclosure, which means structural relief that severs the default-distribution arrangement from the search-product business, an interoperability mandate on the search-index and user-interaction data that would let any competitor — qualified or not — build a rival search product on competitive terms, and a right-to-leave for the user, encoded in the device's interface, that does not require the user to perform the deliberate act of changing the default against an interface designed to discourage the change. None of these are in the Mehta remedy. The cross-appeal does not, on the public-record portions of its filings, ask for them. The window in which the United States might have produced a competitive general-search market closed in 2024; what is open now is the question of whether the documented monopolization will produce any consequence at all.

The mill where this columnist's father worked was bought in 1995 by a holding company with no operational interest in steel and a strong operational interest in the depreciation schedule. The mill kept running; the workforce was cut by a third; the surviving production was extracted as cash flow to the holding company until the cash flow ran out. The technical specifics differ. The mechanism does not. A company that has the resources to pay twenty billion dollars a year to be the default has no incentive to produce a search product worth choosing, and a court that lets the twenty-billion-dollar payment continue under annual renegotiation has authorized the rent to keep flowing. The September 2 remedies decision is the moment at which the antitrust apparatus, having looked at the architecture and named it accurately, decided not to disturb it.

The search box on the device in the reader's hand was filled in at the factory, in an arrangement the reader was not party to, by a payment the reader did not authorize, to a destination the reader has not been asked to evaluate. The remedy that was supposed to open the box has decided to keep it closed.
