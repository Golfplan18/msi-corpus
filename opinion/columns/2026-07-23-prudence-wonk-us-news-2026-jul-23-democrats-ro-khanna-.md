Ro Khanna is selling a $15 billion annual spending program as tariff revenue redirection. The accounting does not work.

The Industrial Bank for American Manufacturing Act, introduced this week by Representatives Khanna, Suozzi, and Dingell, would divert up to 50 percent of Section 301 tariff revenue — capped at $15 billion per year — from the Treasury general fund into a new federal entity that would make grants, loans, and equity investments to small and medium-sized manufacturers. The bill requires congressional approval for individual loans exceeding $100 million. It caps single loans at $500 million. Khanna calls it "the boldest industrialization proposal since FDR."

The communities Khanna names are real, and the pain is real. Johnstown, Pennsylvania, lost its steel economy decades ago and has not recovered. Lordstown, Ohio, watched General Motors shutter its plant in 2019. The Downriver region of Michigan has bled auto jobs for a generation. The Lower Bucks county Delaware River corridor where Khanna grew up has seen the same hollowing. A small manufacturer outside Cleveland told Khanna on his recent heartland tour that it needed about a million dollars in capital to make a part domestically instead of importing it from Asia. The diagnosis — capital does not flow to small manufacturers in the industrial Midwest — is not wrong. The bill's treatment is.

On a current-law baseline, [the bill's funding mechanism is a bookkeeping exercise](/articles/2026-07-14-graham-backed-bill-would-target-china-india-with-100-russian-oil-tariffs/) that obscures a $15 billion annual spending decision. Tariff revenue already flows into the general fund; CBO scores it as federal revenue in its baseline; its disposition is a spending choice, not a revenue choice. The bill does not raise new revenue. It earmarks revenue that is already being scored, already being spent, and already being projected in CBO's ten-year baseline. The question the bill does not answer — and that its fiscal architecture is designed to prevent readers from asking — is: if $15 billion per year is redirected to the industrial bank, what spending currently funded by that revenue is cut, or what deficit increase is accepted?

That question is the bill's structural defect, and the reason the "tariff revenue" framing is a wonk-laundering operation.

On a PAYGO basis — the procedural rule requiring that new mandatory spending be offset or waived — the industrial bank is a $150 billion ten-year mandatory-spending program that either needs a $150 billion offset or needs to be scored against a baseline that already accounts for the revenue diversion. A PAYGO waiver is of course possible; Congress waives the rule for many bills. But a waiver requires a majority to vote to accept the deficit increase — which is the same substantive fiscal decision the tariff-revenue framing is designed to obscure. If scored as new spending, CBO will require a pay-for. If scored as reallocation of existing baseline revenue, the offset question does not disappear; it simply moves from CBO's cost estimate to the appropriations committee that has to decide what loses $15 billion per year. Either way, the fiscal machinery applies. The bill does not exempt itself from the machinery. It just frames the machinery out of the public conversation.

Three points follow.

First, the historical analogies are wrong. [Hamilton's First Bank of the United States managed federal debt and established credit](/articles/2026-07-10-micron-s-250-billion-plan-sets-stage-for-korean-chip-production-calls/); it did not make equity investments in small manufacturers. FDR's War Production Board directed private industry under emergency wartime authority; it did not create a permanent loan fund. The Marshall Plan transferred federal dollars to rebuild Europe; it did not redirect tariff revenue into domestic industrial subsidies. Every one of these analogies names an institution whose purpose, legal authority, and fiscal structure are categorically different from what the bill proposes. Invoking them does the rhetorical work of attaching the bill to the institutional prestige of Hamilton, FDR, and Marshall while detaching it from the fiscal specifics that determine whether the bill's architecture actually functions. This is the wonk-laundering move in its purest form: historical authority substituted for fiscal substance.

Second, the structural diagnosis is wrong. Manufacturing employment fell from 19.6 million in 1979 to roughly 12.6 million today, with 75,000 jobs lost since January 2025. The decline has continued through successive administrations, successive industrial-policy interventions, and successive rounds of tariff increases. The pattern suggests the binding constraint is not capital access — it is cost structure. The empirical record on the CHIPS Act and IRA manufacturing incentives — reviewed in CRS reports on semiconductor offshoring, the Tax Foundation's counterfactual analyses of post-enactment investment behavior, and the Center for Growth and Opportunity's political-economy study of the CHIPS and Science Act — indicates that while targeted subsidies shifted some production decisions at the margin, they did not alter the labor-cost, regulatory-compliance, and supply-chain arithmetic that drives offshoring decisions for most firms. An additional $15 billion per year in federal subsidies, distributed across thousands of small and medium manufacturers, does not change the equation that determines whether a $1 million capital investment in Johnstown, Pennsylvania, pencils out better than a $1 million capital investment in Shenzhen. The bill identifies the symptom — capital does not flow to small manufacturers in the industrial Midwest — and proposes to treat it with a fiscal mechanism that does not address the underlying diagnosis.

Third, the funding stream is structurally unsuited to permanent infrastructure. Tariff revenue is a function of import volume and tariff rates, and the bill's sponsors are betting on a revenue source whose behavior they do not control. It is true that since 2018, successive rounds of rate increases — from Trump's initial Section 301 actions through Biden's maintenance and Trump's second-term escalations — have driven tariff revenue sharply upward, with effective rates on Chinese goods now reaching 34 percent or higher on many categories and some product lines facing combined rates above 75 percent. Rate increases have outpaced volume declines so far. But this underscores the bill's vulnerability, not its stability. Tariff rates are set by executive action and can be raised, lowered, or eliminated by the next administration. A permanent industrial bank funded by a revenue stream that swings with each president's trade policy will either feast or famine depending on who occupies the White House — which is the opposite of the predictable, long-term capital planning the bill's beneficiaries would need. The fiscal architecture does not create a contradiction so much as a dependency: the program's viability is hostage to the very tariff politics it claims to transcend.

There is a further irony the bill's sponsors may not have intended. The same congressional coalition promoting this bill as "industrialization" has spent years arguing that tariffs are a regressive consumption tax that raises prices on working families. The empirical consensus supports them: Federal Reserve research finds that the pass-through rate on tariffs is generally high, often near 100 percent, meaning the burden falls on domestic consumers and firms rather than foreign exporters. The bill proposes to fund an industrial-subsidy bank with a regressive consumption tax and calls it a jobs program. The distributional analysis, if JCT or TPC runs it, will show the incidence falling on the same working-class consumers the bill claims to serve.

The score is the score. The methodology footnote — what happens to $150 billion of baseline revenue over ten years when it is redirected to a new mandatory-spending program — is the indictment. The bill's sponsors do not get to grade it.
