The household, the neighborhood, the playground are sacred trusts, and the governing authority exists to keep them so. For years the city declined to govern. The open-air drug market is not a spontaneous growth; it is the fruit of an order that refused its work. Frey has now turned, and the turning is just. The Lileks column names, with appropriate scorn, the council members who would prefer that the needle remain in the playground rather than the criminal record attach to the needle-strewer. They speak of criminalizing homelessness, of following the undocumented into deportation, of the cruelty of enforcing the law. They do not speak of the four children.

Four children, in the Little Earth housing project, stepped on discarded needles in the month before the mayor's announcement. They required HIV post-exposure prophylaxis. The treatment is a twenty-eight-day course of antiretroviral medication, begun within hours of exposure, taken twice daily, with side effects that include nausea, diarrhea, fatigue, and headache. The children were tested for HIV at the time of the stick, and again at six weeks, at three months, and at six months. They were tested for hepatitis B and hepatitis C. The anxiety of those intervals is not abstract; it is the particular anxiety of a child who has been told that a discarded syringe has entered his foot, and that the drug now in his body is the only thing standing between him and a disease that, untreated, becomes a life sentence. The needles were discarded in the open-air drug market that the city, until Frey's announcement, declined to disperse. The market has done its work: the syringe lies where the child steps, the child steps where the child plays, the playground is a needle field. The children were playing.
