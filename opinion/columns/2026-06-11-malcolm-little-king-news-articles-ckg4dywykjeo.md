*They contest a pageant while the water rises and the ceiling hangs half-torn.*

Nichia Rowson knows what the collapse actually smells like. For nearly two years, the acrid stench of an illegal waste dump towering in Bickershaw has drifted a quarter of a mile into her neighborhood. The rats grew so thick she tore down her kitchen ceiling to fight them, and the ceiling is still gone. In nearby Platt Bridge, Dawn Royds woke last New Year’s Day to blue police lights and her children’s toys floating in floodwater — the second time in a decade the water had come for her house, the first in 2015 dismissed as an act of God, and a minister came and £329,000 was allocated for flood defences, but the water will come back. These are the actual conditions of the Makerfield constituency: rats in the walls, rising water in the living room, the waste pile festering since 2024, a summer fire that did not remove it, and a generation of young men told that all they are worthy of is a job in a warehouse.

And into this precise, physical reality step the machines of national politics, hungry for a proxy war.

On June 18, the people of Makerfield go to the polls in what political scientists correctly call a proxy prime ministerial election. Andy Burnham, the Greater Manchester Mayor, is standing for Parliament here, and [he has said outright that if he wins he will enter any Labour leadership contest](/articles/2026-06-04-burnham-says-he-would-seek-to-enter-any-labour-leadership-contest/) and challenge Keir Starmer. His rival, Robert Kenyon of Reform UK, a local plumber running as the “normal local lad,” feeds on the despair the dump represents. On the fringes, Restore Britain, buoyed by Elon Musk’s direct attention on X over the killing of teenager Henry Nowak, offers a death penalty referendum instead of pest control.

The cui bono trace of this by-election is blindingly short and heavily tilted away from the constituency’s actual residents. The concentrated beneficiaries are the national political brands. Burnham’s leadership campaign operation gains a parliamentary platform; Reform UK’s donor network gains a trophy seat. Burnham’s apparatus needs to beat the insurgent right to prove to Labour MPs that he alone can hold the coalition together. Reform UK needs a by‑election victory to demonstrate that its momentum is irreversible and that the “broken Britain” story has fully captured the abandoned post‑industrial north.

The diffuse cost-bearers are the people of Ashton-in-Makerfield, whose lived reality is reduced to a rhetorical prop in a national branding exercise. Burnham knocks on every door, and he does it with sincerity, but the strategic center of gravity of his campaign is not in Makerfield’s potholes; it is in Downing Street. Reform UK’s Kenyon pitches opposition to new housing on green‑belt land — a safe, localized motte — while hiding his party’s bailey: a national project of political realignment that would not unclog the Bickershaw dump or permanently secure the Platt Bridge floodwalls.

This deployment of national ambition over local necessity is a textbook execution of the motte‑and‑bailey technique [Catalog ID: motte_and_bailey]. Burnham advances the controversial claim — the bailey of his stated intent to use Makerfield as a stepping‑stone to replace the Prime Minister — and retreats to the defensible motte of cheaper bus fares and constituent services whenever the ambition is challenged as opportunistic. The residents on the doorstep hear the motte; the Labour parliamentary party watches the bailey.

Dominating both campaigns is the exhausted slogan that “Britain is broken.” Think tanks report it as the most common descriptor of the country. Burnham, who says the nation has been on the wrong path for forty years, borrows it; Reform UK deploys it as its foundational premise. But this is a deliberate frame‑engineered relabeling [Catalog ID: frame_engineered_relabeling]. The phrase substitutes a vague, existential malaise for the specific structural mechanisms of abandonment. The listener feels the malaise without ever being invited to demand the mechanism. The frame persists because naming the mechanism — the financialized abandonment of municipal infrastructure — would implicate both parties’ funders.

The mechanism is the economic architecture of post‑industrial extraction. The Lancashire coal pits that George Orwell documented in *The Road to Wigan Pier* are long closed. They have been replaced by wind‑swept logistics hubs and warehousing. As Mark Webster of the Ashton Bears rugby league club notes, a young man in Makerfield today is considered worthy only of a job in a warehouse. The shift from unionized, community‑anchored industry to precarious, transient logistics is not an accident of “brokenness.” It is a deliberate design choice. A thing‑oriented society, as Martin Luther King Jr. diagnosed at Riverside in 1967, prioritizes the velocity of goods over the stability of the people who move them.

King argued that when a polity values its machines and its logistical throughput above the human beings who tend them, you do not have a housing problem and a pest problem and a flood problem; you have a single, three‑headed pathology, and you cannot kill one head while feeding the other two. The acrid smell of the Bickershaw dump is not a failure of local pest control; it is a failure of the civic state to prioritize the human environment over the logistical indifference. The £2.65 billion the government boasts of spending on flood defences nationally is a balm applied to the symptom while the root cause — the financialized abandonment of the municipal infrastructure that supports working‑class communities — remains fully operational.

This is also a moment of profound exposure for the establishment. In the *Star Wars* universe, Cassian Andor tells the converted prison guard Kino Loy, “Power doesn’t panic.” The fact that national attention has been laser‑focused on a constituency that makes up 0.1 percent of the electorate, treating it as the hinge of the nation’s political survival, is the political equivalent of panic. The Labour‑Reform duopoly is escalating its rhetoric and deploying its heaviest hitters into a small north‑western town precisely because it knows it is exposed. The structural rot is visible in the removed kitchen ceilings and the floating toys.

Burnham’s case is that he can deliver reindustrialisation — advanced manufacturing, military‑tech, computer‑science jobs on the same ground where the pits closed. It is not a stupid case. The motorcycle manufacturer Langen was founded here by a local engineer who wanted to prove you could do it in Ashton. The town council spent £6.6 million on regeneration. There is some evidence, local and specific, that the place is not dead. But Burnham is making the opposite bet: that he can occupy the mender’s ground while standing far enough from the government’s failures to avoid the smell. He will win or lose in Makerfield on the margin of voters who believe that a man who has been in public life for twenty-five years, who has held senior office, whose ambition is undisguised, will somehow govern differently from every other Labour politician who has stood where he is standing. It is a thin margin. It is the margin that decided the last two general elections. The suffering is real; the attention it is now receiving is purely transactional.

The tragedy of Makerfield is not that it is broken. The tragedy is that it is being used as a mirror for national vanity. Burnham and Reform UK are playing a zero‑sum game over the optics of recovery while the actual recovery requires the patient, unglamorous work of municipal investment, the re‑industrialization that brings computer‑science jobs and advanced manufacturing back to the north, and the restoration of local democratic agency that treats residents as citizens rather than as ballot‑box ballast.

The arc of the moral universe is long, but it does not bend toward justice while its inhabitants are conscripted as an audience for a national pageant they did not commission. It bends when the people who live with the rats in the walls and the water in the floorboards are treated not as the victims of a broken Britain, but as the architects of a rebuilt one. King’s eschatological horizon demands that we stop asking which national figure will descend upon a forgotten town to save it. It demands that we demand the structural repair of the town itself, recognizing that the people who survived the closing of the pits and the opening of the warehouses are the ones who must build the next iteration, with tools that are finally put in their own hands. The broken ceiling must be refixed by the state, and the state must be reminded that the people who inhabit it are not props, but the purpose.
