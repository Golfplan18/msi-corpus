Trump and Rubio are selling arms through NATO allies while offering to broker peace.

Rubio stood in Manila this week and told reporters there's "no quick path" to ending the war Russia started by invading Ukraine. He called past U.S.-mediated talks "unsuccessful or at least unfruitful." Then he said the president remains committed "if conditions and factors have changed to make that possible." That's a sentence that says nothing. And the nothing is the point.

Because while Rubio talks about finding "new suggestions and new ideas," the weapons keep moving. NATO allies are buying them from the same defense contractors who supply the Pentagon. Lockheed Martin builds the HIMARS rockets that Ukraine fires. RTX builds the Patriot interceptors. General Dynamics builds the artillery rounds. Their plants in Fort Worth and Tucson and Groton run day and night. The workers who build those weapons don't get to choose where they end up. But their paychecks depend on the next contract, and the next contract depends on the next war. The administration is buying these weapons through NATO allies, and the same secretary of state is offering to broker peace. The gap between those two things isn't a gap. It's the business model.

Lavrov, for his part, told Rubio that further arming Ukraine is "unacceptable." The Russian position hasn't changed since the invasion began. The demand is simple: stop helping Ukraine defend itself, and Russia will accept a "political and diplomatic resolution" on terms that leave its conquests intact. Lavrov invoked commitments from a Putin-Trump summit in Alaska last year, according to the Russian Foreign Ministry, but the substance of those commitments remains undisclosed. Nobody in Manila will say what Russia must give back.

And here's the part that should bother anyone who's ever built something with their hands and watched someone else take the credit. The same secretary of state who is offering to broker peace in Ukraine is threatening to destroy Iranian infrastructure. "The price will continue to get higher every single night," Rubio said of Iran. "The president's policy is a head for an eye." The man is mediating one war and escalating another in the same afternoon. The common thread isn't diplomacy. It's performance.

Eisenhower, in his farewell address of January 17, 1961, warned in paragraph 24 that "the potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist" when the military-industrial complex acquires unwarranted influence. Sixty-five years later, the administration is buying weapons through NATO allies while offering to broker peace. The mechanism is the same: the arms flow continues, the diplomacy performs, and the gap between what is said and what is done grows wider. The defense contractors keep getting paid. The workers who build the weapons keep clocking in. The men who fight keep dying. And the administration says it's looking for "new ideas."

What would real diplomacy require? It would name what Russia must give back. It would condition any negotiation on withdrawal from occupied territory. It would tie the peace process to the same accountability mechanisms that apply to every other aggressor state in the post-1945 order. The United States must publicly and unequivocally state that any talks are contingent on Russia's withdrawal to the February 2022 lines. That's not a radical position. It's the minimum the U.S. has said it believes in since 1945.

The men who will pay the cost of this diplomatic void are not in Manila. They are in the trenches in eastern Ukraine, where every day the U.S. avoids naming what Russia must concede, and those men pay for Rubio's performance with their lives.
