*The wars are permanent, and the demand is endless.*

GM is retooling from car parts to weapons parts because the wars in Ukraine and Iran fired more missiles than American factories can replace. In a small shop in Georgia, a man might recognize the move: when the work changes, you learn the new work. The difference is the direction. An automaker is contracting for a defense company, not the other way around, and the reason is that the prior inventory is depleted and the production lines are not fast enough to replenish it.

General Motors’ vehicle sales have been declining, and factory space is sitting idle. GM re-entered the defense business a decade ago with GM Defense, a subsidiary that started with infantry vehicles. Now, as the Wall Street Journal’s Sharon Terlep reported, GM has been in talks with Lockheed Martin since early this year about manufacturing commonly used components for the defense contractor’s weapons. GM CEO Mary Barra has met with Trump administration officials to discuss a larger military role for the automaker. No deal is final. The contours could change. But the direction is clear enough: idle plants, new revenue, and an administration requesting the largest defense budget in modern history to build back what two wars consumed.

Lockheed Martin builds the F-35, the THAAD interceptor, and the Black Hawk. The company has agreed to increase production of air-defense interceptors, and it is hitting supply-chain bottlenecks across its supplier base. The wars in Ukraine and the Middle East depleted stocks of missiles faster than the existing production architecture could replace them. The Pentagon now says it may need companies outside the traditional defense supply chain to backstop the contractors that cannot keep up. That is why an automaker is being asked whether it can build missile parts.

Here is the problem the story’s framing conceals. High-end munitions production is not automobile manufacturing. The overlap between what an auto-parts supplier makes and what a missile interceptor requires is limited. This is not a question of retooling a stamping press. It is a question of whether the United States defense industrial base, now consolidated into five prime contractors and a strained tier-one supplier network, has enough capacity to sustain the operational tempo the country is currently running. The Trump administration has invoked Cold War-era production authorities to accelerate what the industrial base cannot currently deliver. Now GM is being courted to fill the gap.

The political economy here is something Eisenhower would have recognized. In his January 17, 1961 farewell address, he warned of the military-industrial complex’s “unwarranted influence” and called for “an alert and knowledgeable citizenry” to hold it accountable. He also described a structural arrangement in which “the total influence — economic, political, even spiritual — is felt in every city, every State house, every office of the Federal government.” Sixty-five years later, the system has produced its own inverse leverage. It is not that the defense industry controls the civilian economy. It is that the civilian economy is being asked to re-enter the defense economy because the defense economy fired more weapons than it built.

The administration’s requested budget is $1.5 trillion, which would be the Defense Department’s largest in modern history. It includes tens of billions of dollars for new munitions and drone manufacturing. The Patriot production line has tripled output and still will not hit its production target until 2030. The administration is pursuing cheaper, faster missiles to address slow replacement rates. Other automakers are exploring the same territory: European manufacturers have discussed defense production for Israeli and European systems, and Ford’s CEO has confirmed discussions with the U.S. government about military-related projects. The civilian industrial base is migrating toward the defense industrial base not because the economics are favorable but because the weapons ran out.

Andrew Bacevich, the retired Army colonel and West Point graduate whose son was killed in Iraq, has spent two decades extending Eisenhower’s warning into the contemporary period. In *Washington Rules*, he laid out the bipartisan consensus that sustains permanent American military engagement across administrations of both parties. “The rules that define the Washington consensus on national security,” he wrote, “are, first, maintain global military preeminence; second, maintain a global military presence; and third, intervene whenever and wherever necessary to uphold the first two.” What has changed is the scale of the apparatus required to sustain them. When the permanent-war posture outruns the defense industry’s production capacity, the answer is not fewer wars. The answer is more factories.

The Journal notes that high-end munitions production is more complex than commercial auto manufacturing, and there is limited overlap between items produced by the automotive-parts supply chain and the weapons components in shortest supply. That is the objection that will be raised in every boardroom and every hearing: GM does not know how to build missile guidance systems. But the Pentagon is not asking GM to build guidance systems. It is asking for commonly used parts — the commoditized, high-volume components the auto industry’s supply chain produces by the million. The complexity gap is real, and it actually proves the thesis. Even the simple parts are in short supply because demand has so far outrun the dedicated defense industrial base that the Pentagon is now enlisting non-traditional manufacturers, a task that has already sent officials looking beyond the usual contractors. When the defense contractors are at capacity and the wars are not stopping, the auto industry’s idle plants become the answer by default. The arrangement is not a perfect fit. It is an imperfect fit that happens to be the only one available. That is the permanent-war economy’s logic.

And the factories are available. The auto industry is selling fewer cars than it did a generation ago. Factory floors sit idle. Ford is in talks about military-related projects. Volkswagen considers making Iron Dome components. Mercedes-Benz floats entering defense production. This is not a coincidence of patriotism. This is the permanent-war economy absorbing civilian industry sector by sector, because the wars are permanent and the demand is endless.

I want to be clear about what this means for the people who work in those plants. The men and women on the assembly lines in Michigan and Ohio and Tennessee did not choose to enter the arms industry. They chose to build cars. They are skilled at building cars. When their factory is retooled to build missile components, they will do the work because they need the paycheck, and the paycheck will be steady because the contracts will be steady, and the contracts will be steady because the wars are not stopping. That is not a choice. That is an offer you cannot refuse.

The Fortunate Son calculus is instructive here. The executives who negotiate these contracts and the shareholders who profit from them will not be the ones whose sons and daughters are deployed to fire the missiles or fly the aircraft that the missiles are fired at. The class divide between who designs the wars and who fights them has been documented across every American conflict since Vietnam, and the post-9/11 volunteer force has only deepened it. GM’s executives and Lockheed’s executives and the Pentagon’s senior officials and the members of Congress who appropriate the $1.5 trillion budget come disproportionately from a class that does not send its children to the recruiter’s office. The men and women on the factory floor — they do.

From where I sat in the gunner’s seat in Iraq, the equipment we used came from defense contractors whose names everybody knows. The tanks, the ammunition, the radios, the body armor — each one manufactured by a company whose quarterly earnings depended on how many wars the United States was prosecuting. When the wars expand, the orders expand. When the orders expand, the factories need to expand. When the defense contractors run out of capacity, the automakers move in. The structure does not require anyone to be evil. It only requires that everyone follow the incentives.

Let me count what that choice has cost. More than seven thousand American service members killed in the post-9/11 wars, a toll the Costs of War Project has catalogued alongside the hundreds of thousands more wounded, many with injuries they will carry for the rest of their lives. The veteran suicide rate, which the VA’s own reports document year after year, is a ledger entry the Pentagon does not include in its budget justification. The moral injury of men and women who served in wars whose strategic rationale shifted beneath them while the defense budgets that sustained the wars never wavered. The civilian dead in Iraq and Afghanistan and now Iran — counted by the Costs of War Project and Iraq Body Count — numbers so large they lose meaning but do not lose weight.

Sun Tzu warned that the object of war is victory, not lengthy campaigns. The United States has been running lengthy campaigns across two theaters, depleting its weapons stocks below what its defense industrial base can replenish without enlisting civilian manufacturers, and requesting the largest budget in department history to bail itself out. This is not a business story about General Motors finding new revenue lines. This is a story about a country that exhausted its war-making inventory and is now cannibalizing its civilian industrial capacity to replace what it consumed.The auto parts recall easier work. These parts are going somewhere else. The arrangement is not a conspiracy. It is a structure. And it will not change until the citizenry Eisenhower called to be alert and knowledgeable — the same passage he used to warn of the military-industrial complex — demands that Congress reclaim the war-making authority the Constitution assigned to it and that the permanent-war economy has absorbed. Nothing less will stop GM from entering the weapons business, or Ford from following, or the next idle industry from being absorbed in its turn.
