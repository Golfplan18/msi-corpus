*He traded human decency for proximity and called it a huge mistake.*

They'll fly Bill Gates into Washington and sit him in a windowless room. Staffers from the House oversight committee — Comer's people — will take their turns asking about Jeffrey Epstein.

The billionaire will say he was stupid. Foolish. A huge mistake.

Here's the thing about being stupid, about making a huge mistake, about being foolish: when you're the second-richest man on earth and you fly on private jets with a convicted sex trafficker for three years after he's already done time for soliciting a minor — that stupidity leaves bodies.

Not Gates's body. Not his children's bodies. Not the bodies of anyone he cares to name. The bodies left in the wake of men like Epstein are always somebody else's daughters, somebody else's sisters, the ones nobody with a private jet ever had to think about until the DOJ files drop.

Bill Gates traded human decency for proximity to Jeffrey Epstein. The Justice Department's release of the Epstein files makes the timeline impossible to ignore. You met with the convicted sex offender repeatedly between 2011 and 2014, three years after he had pleaded guilty to soliciting a minor for prostitution. You flew on his jets. You sat in his rooms. You knew what he had done to young girls, and you decided that his wealth was worth the risk.

The Guardian reports Gates called it an "18-month thing" that restricted Epstein's travel — like it was a bad Yelp review, not a federal sentence for child prostitution. He was aware. He proceeded anyway.

You and your foundation staff told yourselves it was about global health. The foundation admitted that employees interacted with Epstein on the belief that he "could mobilize significant philanthropic resources." It was a transaction. You wanted his money to fund your clinics, and he wanted your legitimacy to wash away the stench of his crimes.

Gates's defense — "I did nothing illicit. I saw nothing illicit" — is the logic of the man who can afford not to see. The committee will ask about the photographs with women whose faces are redacted. Gates will say Epstein asked him to pose with his assistants. The billionaire philanthropist who can mobilize millions for global health could not say no to a photo op with an assistant whose face later gets redacted in a federal file.

This is what Catholic Social Teaching calls the instrumentalization of human dignity — treating persons as means rather than ends. The worst violation isn't always direct instrumentalization; it's dignitary exclusion. The billionaire who can fly to Paris to meet with Epstein and return to Seattle for a foundation town hall has excluded himself from the consequences that land on victims he'll never have to meet.

In a town hall with your own employees, you called your actions "stupid" and a "huge mistake." You apologized to the people who work for you. But an apology to your staff is not a reckoning with the victims. "Mistake" is a word we use for miscalculations, for the wrong turn on the highway. What you did was not a calculation error. It was a failure of nerve, a failure to turn your own stomach when you should have.

The town hall where you "spoke candidly" happened in a building your money built. The foundation staff asking questions work under a roof you paid for. Accountability, conducted on the terms of the accountable, is not a reckoning — it's reputational maintenance with a Q&A session.

The moral trap you walked into is the exact inversion the prophet Isaiah warned against, the ancient ledger where men call evil good to buy proximity. You sold your standing for his checkbook, believing the ends justified the proximity, until you hit the terrible arithmetic of the Gospel, which asks what it profits a man to gain the whole world yet forfeit his soul. You cannot buy back a forfeited soul with global health grants, and you cannot hide behind the closed doors of congressional depositions from the final judgment, where the question asked of nations and leaders alike is simply this: I was a stranger and you did not welcome me. The least of these were the girls Epstein trapped, and they were not welcomed; they were walked over, so the work could move forward.
