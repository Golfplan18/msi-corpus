ICE planned to warehouse thousands of people in buildings without enough drinking water. On Wednesday, the agency withdrew its appeal of Pennsylvania’s denial of water permits, effectively killing the plan. The state had determined that local water and wastewater services in rural Berks and Schuylkill counties were inadequate for the thousands of detainees the agency intended to hold there. The plan, for now, is dead.

For more than a year, the administration has sought to convert empty warehouses across the country into mass detention facilities — a strategy that MSI has covered from its earliest stages, when [communities from Georgia to Pennsylvania began pushing back](/articles/2026-04-08-us-communities-push-back-on-plans-to-convert-warehouses-into-detention-centers/). In Social Circle, Georgia, [local resistance forced the agency to reverse course](/articles/2026-06-19-social-circle-s-revolt-appears-to-derail-ice-detention-center-plan/). Now a state’s refusal to issue permits — grounded in the most basic necessity of human life — has done the same in Pennsylvania. The federal government that claims the authority to seize people, confine them, and deport them could not even guarantee them a glass of water.

You planned this, I say to the men and women of ICE, and you must reckon with what you planned. You intended to take human beings — mothers, fathers, children — and lock them behind warehouse walls in facilities not built for habitation. These are structures with no windows designed for occupancy, no ventilation fit for human lungs, no plumbing configured for the basic needs of hundreds or thousands of people. You intended to do it in rural towns whose water systems could not support the population you would have crammed inside. You knew this. The state told you. And you appealed anyway, until you could no longer sustain the pretense that what you were doing was anything other than what it was: cruel.

The cruelty is not accidental. When the government treats immigrants as a problem to be solved rather than as persons to be encountered, it produces plans like this one. The logic of warehousing — storing people as inventory, moving them through processing centers like freight — follows directly from the logic that says some lives are disposable. This is what the Hebrew prophets called *trampling the poor*. It is what Pope Francis named at Lampedusa: the globalization of indifference, the refusal to weep.

The Catholic tradition I write from holds that the migrant is Christ in distressing disguise. “I was a stranger and you welcomed me,” Jesus says in Matthew 25, and the judgment on those who did not welcome the stranger is severe. But the tradition does not only speak to the welcoming of the stranger; it speaks to the dignity of the stranger when she is already here. The U.S. bishops, together with their Mexican counterparts, wrote in *Strangers No Longer* that “the human rights and human dignity of undocumented migrants should be respected.” The principle of solidarity teaches that we are responsible for one another, and the principle of the universal destination of goods teaches that the earth's resources are meant for all people, not just those with power. There is no asterisk next to that sentence that says *unless you can call them a security threat and put them in a warehouse.*

I know the objection: that those who cross the border without authorization have broken the law and must face consequences. I do not dispute the sovereignty of nations to control their borders. But the punishment for a civil violation cannot be indefinite detention in inhumane conditions. That is not justice; it is the machinery of cruelty dressed up as enforcement. And the community members who organized, the state officials who denied the permits, and the local water managers who said *this cannot be done* did what the federal government refused to do: they saw the human beings behind the policy and said no. They showed up at public hearings, filed objections with the DEP, and refused to let their town become a warehouse for human beings.

To be clear, the problem is not only this administration’s. The impulse to warehouse the vulnerable is older than any party. The Democratic administration before this one expanded family detention and used Title 42 to expel asylum seekers without due process. The detention machine has deep roots, and it has been fed by both coalitions. The confession I make as a Catholic is that my own church has often been silent when it should have cried out, and that silence is complicity.

Yet the plan died. A state agency said, in effect, *you cannot do this here because the water runs out.* A group of federal attorneys filed a withdrawal notice. And a community that refused to be silent can take this as evidence that resistance works. The door of return, for those who would plan such things, remains open: you can stop. You can see the faces of the people you would have detained and decide that they are not inventory. The God of the stranger, the God who heard the cry of the Israelites in Egypt, hears the cry of the migrant in Pennsylvania. So does the neighbor who refuses to look away.

We are not powerless. The water that would not flow through those warehouses still runs through the hearts of the people who said no. That is grace, and it is ours to extend. The warehouse doors may have closed before they opened. The welcome table is still being set.
