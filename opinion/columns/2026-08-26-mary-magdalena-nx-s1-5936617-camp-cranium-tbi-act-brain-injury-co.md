Camp Cranium has run in a Pennsylvania field since 2008, teaching children who survived brain injuries to shoot arrows, climb rock walls, and trust their bodies again. Some of the campers are in wheelchairs. Some use crutches. Some had half their brain removed at age five to stop the seizures.

An estimated 2.8 million traumatic brain injuries occur in the United States every year, including about 475,000 in children, according to the Brain Injury Association of America. The CDC's own household survey found those numbers are undercounts; brain injuries are "hidden" because the damage is inside the skull, invisible to the emergency-room triage sheet.

The federal infrastructure for tracking and preventing these injuries is the Traumatic Brain Injury Act, passed by Congress in 1996 and reauthorized four times. The 2020 reauthorization provided $23 million a year through 2024, split between CDC concussion research and state grants. The act lapsed in 2024 and has not been reauthorized. Congress did appropriate $8.25 million for TBI program activities through the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026 — roughly a third of the prior floor, routed around the act.

In early 2025, the Trump administration fired hundreds of employees at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, including the entire team that tracks traumatic brain injuries. The team was three months from launching a nationwide concussion surveillance system — the system meant to count the injuries that do not show up on a triage sheet. The agency will not say how many were terminated, reinstated, or replaced.

In a written statement, an HHS spokesperson, Emily Hilliard, said the administration "remains committed" to TBI prevention and surveillance, and that the work is now handled by "other staff members" at the National Center for Injury Prevention and Control. She said the CDC is "deciding how to establish a cost-effective national concussion surveillance system within the bounds of current funding" and that the agency in 2026 "dedicated funds" to an educational campaign, an injury prevention program, and concussion surveillance — without naming a number, a timeline, or a person assigned to do any of it.

Markwayne Mullin, now the secretary of homeland security, supported reauthorizing TBI funding when he was a senator. Congress is "still considering" whether to extend the act through 2030.
