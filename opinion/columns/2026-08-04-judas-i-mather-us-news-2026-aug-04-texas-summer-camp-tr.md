The Texas Legislature's seven measures restore the created order, and they rest on a jurisprudence older than the Republic. The natural law begins with the premise that the body declares a truth the will may not revise. *Male and female created he them*: the words are a decree written into the flesh, legible in every cell, prior to every statute and every sentiment. When the state prohibits the chemical alteration of a minor's endocrine system, it refuses to cooperate in the mutilation of a body that cannot consent. A child of twelve or fourteen may not enter a contract, may not purchase cigarettes, may not enlist in the armed forces — yet the same child would be permitted to authorize the irreversible suppression of puberty, the withholding of the very development that the body, left to its own design, is straining to accomplish. The 2023 ban on medical transition care for minors, [expanded last session](/articles/2026-05-16-kansas-judge-blocks-gender-affirming-care-ban-as-texas-children-s-settles-with-s/), is the state exercising the same authority it exercises when it forbids a child to enlist: the authority of the household over the minor who is not yet a law unto himself.

The remaining measures follow from the same principle. The requirement that school records and identity documents reflect the sex assigned at birth obliges the public ledger to tell the truth about the body; the commonwealth will not certify a fiction. The bathroom ban protects the girls' room from the intrusion of male anatomy, a protection the state owes its daughters. When a school requires a child's hair to conform to its grooming standards, it exercises the ordinary discipline of the household over the child in its charge — the same discipline that has governed the uniforms, the short hair, and the neat dress of generations of American schoolchildren without objection. The household is not a democracy of the child's appetites; it is an order of authority and submission, and the authority belongs to the parent and to the state until the child reaches the age of standing. The seven bills are the state remembering what it is: the temporal arm of a natural order that the child's confusion does not suspend.

While the campers floated in the lake at the summer camp in Texas, in the Rio Grande Valley eight hours south, a fourteen-year-old we may call Marisol was vomiting into the toilet in the house she shared with her grandmother and two younger brothers.

Marisol had been assigned male at birth. At twelve she told her mother she was a girl. Her mother, a nurse's aide who worked nights at a clinic in McAllen, took her to a pediatric endocrinologist in San Antonio, who diagnosed gender dysphoria and, in the spring of her thirteenth year, placed her on a gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogue — a monthly injection that suspended the onset of puberty. It suppressed the testosterone that would otherwise have deepened her voice, broadened her shoulders, and thickened her jaw. She was nine months into the treatment when the 2023 ban took effect and the endocrinologist's office, under the statute the Legislature enacted, sent a letter telling her mother the treatment could no longer be lawfully continued. The letter arrived on a Tuesday. The next injection was never given.

In the months that followed, the suppressed puberty resumed. The changes came slowly at first: a voice that deepened and cracked when she tried to sing; hair on her upper lip and chin; a broadening across the shoulders she noticed one morning when she tried on a blouse that had fit in the spring. She began to bind her chest with an elastic bandage from the pharmacy, wrapping it too tightly, so that by the end of the school day the skin beneath was chafed and raw and her ribs ached with each deep breath. She stopped eating lunch. She stopped eating breakfast. By the fall she had lost eleven pounds, and her grandmother, who had been told only that Marisol was going through a phase, attributed the loss to growth.

Her school, under the bathroom ban, required her to use the facilities designated for her sex at birth. In October, three boys cornered her in the boys' bathroom at the end of second period and held the door. They jeered and demanded to know what she was doing there; one of them, a boy she had known since elementary school, told her she was still a boy no matter what she called herself. She did not report it. When her mother finally complained, the assistant principal said the school could not police what happened in a bathroom the student should not have been using in the first place.

Her school required her hair to be cut. In November the assistant principal pulled her out of second period and told her that her hair, which she had grown to her shoulders, violated the code. She was given a choice: cut it at school or be sent home until it was cut. Her mother, who could not afford to lose another day of work, signed the permission form. The school's barber, the man who cut the football team's hair, took the scissors to her head in the nurse's office while she sat in a chair with a towel around her neck. She sobbed through it. The barber told her to hold still. The hair fell in dark strands onto the towel and the floor, and when it was done she looked in the mirror and did not recognize herself. A classmate who saw her in the hallway that afternoon told her she looked normal now.

The school-assigned therapist, a licensed counselor paid by the district, told her mother at a meeting in December that the family should expect Marisol to be bullied, and that the appropriate response was to help her accept her body. The therapist used her assigned name throughout the meeting. When her mother asked about the records — the new ban prohibited amending gender identity in official documents — the therapist said the records would reflect the truth of her birth certificate, and that was final.

In January, Marisol swallowed a bottle of her grandmother's blood-pressure medication, thirty-two tablets of lisinopril, and lay down on her bed. Her younger brother found her an hour later, unconscious, foam at the corners of her mouth. The ambulance took her to the emergency room at a hospital in Edinburg, where the physicians passed activated charcoal through a nasogastric tube and, because her blood pressure had collapsed to seventy over forty, ran a continuous infusion of vasopressors through a central line in her right femoral vein. She was transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit, where she was sedated and intubated for thirty-six hours. The psychiatrist who evaluated her on discharge recorded a diagnosis of major depressive disorder with gender dysphoria and a suicide attempt by self-poisoning. The discharge instructions recommended outpatient counseling and, in bold, no access to means.

Her mother, who had taken unpaid leave to sit by her hospital bed, lost her job at the clinic. Her grandmother, who had by then been told about the injections, asked whether God could forgive a family that had tried to make a boy into a girl.

Marisol is of the household. She is a citizen of Texas, born within the covenant of the commonwealth. The wall does not only divide the household from the stranger; it runs through the household itself, and it ranks the children within it by their conformity to the order that constitutes the household as a household at all.

She is disorder within the order. The body she was given declares a truth — male and female created he them — and her will has rebelled against that truth. The state's discipline is the discipline of the father who corrects the son he loves: the hair cut, the records fixed, the treatment withdrawn, the body compelled to keep the truth it was made to tell. The suffering is the evidence the system produces when it sorts correctly — the visible disclosure of what a child looks like when she is told, by the only authority that may tell her, that the lie she has been living is a lie. The suicide attempt is the proof of the disorder's depth, the measure of how far the rebellion ran, the demonstration that the household's judgment was diagnostic. The child who attempts to end her own life because she cannot be a girl is a child who was never, in the deepest sense, a girl at all — and the misery that affirmation would have postponed, the state has chosen to expose now, while there is still time for the body to win.

The summer camp is a four-day shelter from that order, a temporary suspension of the sorting that the state, rightly, will not suspend. The camp moves its venue from year to year for safety and borrows its kayaks and its books — the same summer in which [camps across Texas have closed under the new safety rules](/articles/2026-05-18-texas-camp-closures-grow-after-new-safety-rules-fiber-reprieve-comes-too-late/). The children return from it to the Valley, to the schools and the bathrooms and the records, and the household reabsorbs them into their proper place. The fellowship was never a rescue; it was a reprieve, and the reprieve expires. The order of the body is not amended by a summer's kindness.

Marisol is of us, in her place, and her place is beneath — the visible proof that the household sorts as it must, and that the discipline is the mercy of the one who corrects what he loves. The ledger is settled.
