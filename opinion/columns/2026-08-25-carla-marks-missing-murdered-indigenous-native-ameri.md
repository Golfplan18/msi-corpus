The FBI just put a price on its own failure — and then announced it as progress. A $25,000 minimum reward for tips in more than fifty missing and murdered Indigenous person cases in Indian Country is not generosity. It is the floor below which the agency was willing to let cases rot, now unveiled with a press conference and a number attached.

The number is the news.

Lisa Carlow, whose father Patrick Carlow Sr. was found dead at Pine Ridge in 2023, said it from Oglala: “Everybody’s scared in Oglala. So to me it’s like, okay, what do you guys know that you’re not saying?” That is not the voice of a daughter who believes a check will repair the government’s credibility. It is the voice of someone who has watched federal authority move at its own glacial pace and reasonably wonders whether it is protecting itself before it protects her.

The Federal Bureau of Investigation investigates federal crimes on nearly two hundred Native American reservations alongside the Bureau of Indian Affairs. At the end of 2025, the FBI’s National Crime Information Center logged just under 1,500 active cases involving missing Native Americans. The fifty-plus cases now assigned a $25,000 minimum reward are a sliver of that workload, and a sliver of the violence.

A floor is being set on a small patch of an enormous floor.

The FBI does note that not every missing-person case becomes a federal investigation. Fine. That makes the agency’s announcement narrower, not more impressive. The question remains: after decades of federal jurisdiction, how many families receive sustained investigative attention, and how many receive a reward bump when the agency needs to demonstrate movement?

Two cases the FBI used to dress up the rollout tell the story more clearly than the press release does. Maleeka Boone was eight years old when investigators said she was likely struck by a vehicle on the Navajo Nation. Emily Pike was a San Carlos Apache teenager whose body was recovered after she disappeared from an Arizona group home. These are the children the agency put on the podium.

The Bureau is not embarrassed to place young lives next to a $25,000 price tag. It is proud of itself. It held a press conference.

Most of the affected cases are less than a decade old. About a dozen have been unsolved for more than two decades. Two decades. A generation has come of age inside the FBI’s filing cabinets, and the best the agency can offer is an increase from whatever reward existed before to $25,000.

The announcement is not a policy. It is a press release with a number attached.

The skepticism from families and tribal advocates is not a fringe opinion or an attitude problem. It is the only honest reading of the record. Tips do not flow from money alone, especially when the money comes from the same government communities have learned, through long experience, not to trust. Distrust of federal authority in tribal communities is a survival adaptation. It was earned. It is reinforced every time a case goes cold and a name disappears into a federal database.

Communities [across the country already marked Missing and Murdered Indigenous Peoples Awareness Day](/articles/2026-05-05-indigenous-communities-nationwide-mark-missing-and-murdered-indigenous-peoples-a/) earlier this year. [Events marking the same day](/articles/2026-05-04-events-mark-missing-and-murdered-indigenous-peoples-awareness-day/) took place in cities and on reservations alike. Families, tribal nations, and advocates have spent years putting names, dates, and jurisdictions onto cases federal agencies allowed to vanish into generic file drawers.

The federal answer arrived months later, in the form of a reward bump.

Twenty-five thousand dollars. That is the floor.

None of this means the rewards should be mocked or refused. Families should use them. The money may produce a tip. A tip may produce evidence. Evidence may produce an arrest. A useful instrument is still a useful instrument, even when the institution handing it over has confused it with a solution.

But the announcement has unintentionally created something more valuable than the bounty itself: a public ledger.

Every case attached to a reward is now a case the FBI has formally identified, formally prioritized, and formally committed to paying for. That ledger can be audited. It can be expanded. It can be carried into every hearing and meeting where a family is told to wait, every congressional office asking what the federal commitment actually is, and every tribal government demanding to know why one case receives a public promise while another remains in the drawer.

The agency has accepted one principle in public: the answer cannot be zero.

Good. Let’s use that admission.

The next demand is not simply more money per tip. It is the structure the tip money papers over: a permanent, fully staffed FBI unit dedicated to missing and murdered Indigenous person cases; concurrent tribal jurisdiction recognized in every state; and sustained federal funding for tribal cold-case units that does not depend on whether Washington finds a case photogenic this quarter.

Pine Ridge, the Navajo Nation, and San Carlos are not mysterious jurisdictions where federal investigators cannot operate. They are places where federal investigators have chosen, for generations, to operate selectively. The reward announcement does not change that choice. It changes only the public price tag attached to it.

That is worth something. It is not worth nearly enough.

The MMIP movement has already built the muscle the FBI now wants to claim it started: awareness days, walking events, public registries, tribal data-collection efforts, and families who refused to let a missing person become an unsearchable line in a federal database. The agency is joining a movement, not launching one. The $25,000 floor is the price of admission.

So keep the ledger. Name every case. Track every promised payment. Publish every delay. Ask what investigative step followed each tip and what happened when it did not. Make every $25,000 case one the FBI must show progress on publicly, or explain why it has not.

The agency put a price on its failure. Now families and advocates have a number to organize around, a commitment to enforce, and a fresh lever paid for by the institution that should have built it decades ago.
