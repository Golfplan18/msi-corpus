On August 5, two Phoenix police officers stopped a man and a woman on a Phoenix road. By the time the encounter ended, both of them had been physically assaulted and at least one had been hit with a Taser. The officers did not write a report. They did not activate their body-worn cameras. The stop left no record in any system the Phoenix Police Department maintains, because the only person who could have made a record was the person who chose not to.

Phoenix Police Chief Matt Giordano announced Saturday that Officers Antonio Felix and Luis Vasquez had been fired and arrested on charges including aggravated assault and kidnapping. Detectives established probable cause for those arrests despite the absence of any video evidence from the encounter, Giordano said. There was no indication the officers knew the victims beforehand.

That last detail is the part the comfortable read skips past. We are not looking at officers who targeted someone they had a grudge against. We are looking at officers who, on what was supposed to be a routine traffic stop, did whatever they wanted to two people, and the only reason any of us know about it is that the people they did it to told someone. The case exists because the victims survived and spoke. The cameras did not help. The reporting system did not help. The architecture of police accountability in Phoenix is built so that this is what undiscovered misconduct looks like, and undiscovered misconduct is the dominant share of what the misconduct actually is.

This is not a story about two bad officers. This is a story about a system that requires victims to surface their own abuse before the system admits the abuse happened. Felix and Vasquez are named. They will face the legal process they earned. But the cameras they wore on their chests and the reporting forms they were obligated to file are also named — by what they failed to do. They failed because they were built to fail.

A body-worn camera is not an accountability device when the officer wearing it decides whether it records. It is a tool the officer can use selectively. Officers who intend to assault civilians and disappear the stop do not activate the camera. The officers who were going to do everything by the book were never the problem the cameras were meant to solve. The cameras were meant to solve the officers who were not going to do everything by the book, and those officers — the actual problem — are precisely the ones who can choose not to turn the device on. Felix and Vasquez chose. The cameras did what Felix and Vasquez told them to do.

The reporting requirement failed the same way and for the same reason. A traffic stop that produces no report is, for accountability purposes, a stop that did not happen. The vehicle was stopped. The civilians were detained. The assault occurred. The Taser was used. None of it entered the record because the officer who could have recorded it was the officer committing the misconduct. The architecture does not catch this. The architecture produces this.

Giordano deserves credit for moving on the case once it reached his office. He fired the officers. He announced the charges. He told the public what detectives had established: probable cause, Giordano said, despite no video. The chief's office did what the chief's office could do once the case came to it. That is not the same as a system that prevents the case from existing in the first place. The case existed because a victim came forward. The system discovered it after the discovery. The system did not produce the discovery.

The structural question is not whether Felix and Vasquez will be convicted. The structural question is how many other August 5 traffic stops in Phoenix produced no report and no body camera footage, and how many of those stops involved conduct the department will never know about, because the architecture is built so that the only people who can record a stop are the officers conducting it. Felix and Vasquez are the ones we know about. The pattern that produced them is the pattern that will produce the next pair. The pattern is built into what the department issued them and what the department told them to do with it.

The officers were carrying the devices. The officers were carrying the reporting forms. The officers decided. The decision is where the misconduct lives, and the decision is where the system has chosen to leave it. Body cameras that officers activate are not surveillance of the police; they are surveillance the police do to themselves, when they choose to. An unreported stop is a stop the department has no record of, because the department designed the recordkeeping around the officer's voluntary disclosure. Voluntary disclosure is not accountability. Voluntary disclosure is the absence of accountability dressed up as a procedure.

The fix is structural and it is not complicated. Body cameras that record continuously and store footage to a server the officer cannot edit or delete. Activation is not a decision the officer makes; recording is the default state of the device. A traffic stop that does not enter the dispatch log cannot occur, because dispatch is the entry point for the stop, and the dispatcher creates the record.
