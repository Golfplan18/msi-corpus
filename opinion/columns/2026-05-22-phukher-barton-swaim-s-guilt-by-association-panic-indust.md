# Barton Swaim’s guilt-by-association panic-industry scam

*By Phukher Tarlson*

The WSJ’s Barton Swaim opens his May 20 column with a saccharine tech-bro keynote and closes it with a condescending dismissal of local activists, performing a classic guilt-by-association relabel along the way. This is how we operators deploy the founder-pays-the-bill frame to launder hypocrisy.

> When Eric Schmidt, speaking last week to the University of Arizona’s graduates, rhapsodized about the coming artificial-intelligence revolution, some in the crowed jeered. “I know what many of you are feeling about that,” the former Google CEO said. “I can hear you.” Mr. Schmidt then continued with his prepared remarks: “There is a fear in your generation that the future has already been written, that the machines are coming, that the jobs are evaporating, that the climate is breaking, that politics is fractured, and that you are inheriting a mess that you did not create.” He went on to urge graduates not to let fear rob them of personal agency—a fine, saccharine message.
> *— paragraph 1*

[Technique: Patronizing Framing / Scare-quotes (WSJ Appendix A.4)] — operates as the "concern-taming brochure." Operators of this kind deploy this exact move to make audiences feel heard while pre-packaging their systemic anxiety as a manageable policy tweak. Swaim uses it to frame Schmidt’s address as saccharine, doing the rhetorical work of dismissing genuine generational dread before the reader finishes the first paragraph. The technique buys the author a moral high ground built on the premise that the captured audience needs soothing, not substantively addressing the structural friction Schmidt ignores.

> Where does Mr. Schmidt think young people got the idea that “the climate is breaking”? Where did the “fear” he laments come from? In part from the scores of climate-panic groups to which the Schmidt Family Foundation’s 11th Hour Project has granted hundreds of millions of dollars over the last 20 years. One detail particularly amuses: When 11th Hour first appeared, in 2006, it funded screenings of Al Gore’s “An Inconvenient Truth,” a documentary designed to terrorize viewers with 90 minutes of bleak prophecies, now happily exploded. The outfit, like scores of others founded and funded by other progressive billionaires, spends its resources opposing fossil-based energy and trumpeting the dangers of a warming world.
> *— paragraphs 2-3*

[Technique: Guilt by Association / Motive Attribution (WSJ Appendix A.7)] — operates as the "founder-pays-the-bill" relabel. This move conflates philanthropic investment with direct operational control, letting the operator disavow the very alarm they bankrolled while positioning the grantee organizations as external nuisances. Swaim deploys it to suggest Schmidt’s panic is hypocritical, when the actual operation is outsourcing the moral labor of fear to third-party nonprofits. The public record documents exactly how the 11th Hour Project and similar entities operated as independent advocacy arms; the conflation here is a deliberate editorial sleight of hand, not a factual observation.

> Messrs. Schmidt and Gates may think they can allay the fear and despair they’ve helped to inspire by issuing a few oracles on the unwisdom of catastrophism. But nobody staffing the multibillion-dollar ganglion of climate nonprofits and activist groups plans on taking a more measured view of the coming cataclysm. The prospect of annihilation keeps the money flowing.
> Having taught a generation of liberal-minded Americans to live in fear of global ruin, Silicon Valley’s big shots now feel exasperation that those same Americans think artificial intelligence menaces their families and livelihoods.
> *— paragraphs 4-5*

[Technique: Condescension / Audience Capture (WSJ Appendix A.9)] — operates as the "professional-worrier" dismissal. It assumes the captured audience is either financially driven or intellectually shallow, justifying a retreat to technocratic detachment. Swaim frames the climate-activist base as transactional ("keeps the money flowing"), clearing the deck to treat their successors' opposition to data centers as equally fraudulent. The operation strips the opposition of moral standing by implying that civic pushback is merely a byproduct of a manufactured grift, rather than a legitimate democratic friction.

> But the people showing up at county council meetings to protest the construction of a data center didn’t for the most part come by their convictions the old-fashioned way, by reading and thought. These activists, many of them attached to 501(c)(3) organizations, got their talking points from national nonprofits supported by some of the same moneyed outfits the Schmidt and Gates foundations spent the last two decades bankrolling.
> *— paragraphs 8-9*

[Technique: Intellectual Superiority / False Equivalence (WSJ Appendix A.12)] — operates as the "reading-and-thought" shield. Operators classified this as a condescension play that positions the writer above the fray of democratic friction, implying that anything not self-conducted as academic inquiry is illegitimate grievance. Swaim deploys it to strip local data-center activists of moral standing, completely sidestepping the actual environmental and infrastructural trade-offs the protests raise. The technique functions to invalidate the opposition’s reasoning while pretending to critique their sourcing.

> Call it the Busybody Economy. Organizations designed to worry about future calamities can be counted on to find new calamities to worry about. Data centers serve that purpose nicely: Like all large-scale building projects, they ruffle local feathers; and their purpose, unlike an airport or a shopping center, requires explanation and so lends itself to conspiratorial Facebook posting. That several big-name progressive nonprofits now call for a moratorium on data center construction... does not surprise. Tech billionaires—Laurene Powell Jobs, Jeff Bezos and Steve and Connie Ballmer come to mind—never guessed that the network of climate groups on which they lavished their millions would eventually turn on their industry. A truth too inconvenient to foresee.
> *— paragraphs 10-12*

[Technique: Relabel / Conflation (WSJ Appendix A.14)] — operates as the "busybody economy" relabel scam. This frame flattens complex policy ecosystems and legitimate civic pushback into a single grift, reducing institutional advocacy to petty nosiness. Swaim uses it to tie Schmidt’s climate philanthropy directly to the anti-data-center movement, creating a closed loop of hypocrisy that never actually existed. The move lets the author treat democratic friction as a personality disorder rather than a predictable outcome of capital expansion meeting local governance.

So here is what the column actually does, taken together. It constructs a closed loop of hypocrisy around two men who bankrolled the very anxieties they now sneer at, then claims the activists opposing their new projects are just busybodies who skipped reading class. The joke is supposed to land on the local councils and the grantee organizations, but the real target is the public’s capacity to hold capital accountable for its own feedback loops. Swaim isn’t offering a policy analysis; he’s handing tech philanthropy a permission slip to treat democratic friction as a personality disorder, then laughing when the machine starts making noises it was designed to make. The "busybody economy" is just the grift of funding an alarm, profiting from the scale, and calling the resulting resistance a joke. We built this exact kind of insulation—where the operators bankroll the panic, expand the infrastructure, and laugh at the fallout—and it still looks pathetic from the outside.
