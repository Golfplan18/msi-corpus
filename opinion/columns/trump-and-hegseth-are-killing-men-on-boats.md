Trump and Hegseth are killing men on boats.

The strike pattern began on September 2, 2025. On that first strike, by the *Washington Post*'s detailed account of November 28, 2025 — sourced to seven individuals with direct knowledge — Secretary Hegseth issued a verbal directive to the operation: kill everybody. The missile hit. Two men survived and were seen clinging to the smoldering wreckage. The Joint Special Operations commander, Rear Adm. Frank Bradley, monitoring the operation from Fort Bragg, ordered a second strike to comply with the directive. The second missile killed the survivors. The pattern has continued for eight months. By the running Just Security timeline, the cumulative documented total as of May 8, 2026 stands at fifty-eight strikes, one hundred ninety-three killed, twelve missing and presumed dead, and four known survivors.

These are the facts of the operation. Each one is on the public record. The pattern has run from late summer 2025 to the present without interruption.

The men are dead. Some of them were probably drug runners. Some of them probably were not. None of them was given the chance to be the one or the other in a court, before a magistrate, with an attorney to read the evidence and a judge to weigh the proof. They were killed at sea by missiles fired from American military aircraft and surface combatants on orders that traveled down a chain of command from the White House through the Secretary of Defense to the operational officer who pulled the trigger. The legal authority claimed for the killing is an opinion of the Department of Justice Office of Legal Counsel that the administration has refused to release to the public, including to the senators who have asked for it. The opinion reportedly relies on a theory that the United States is in a "non-international armed conflict" with the designated cartels — a category from the laws of war that requires an actual organized armed group on the other side, with a command structure that wears the responsibilities of a belligerent. The Tren de Aragua name does not satisfy that category. Drug cartels are criminal enterprises, and criminal enterprises are matters of law enforcement.

The United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, said in October 2025 that the strikes "violate international human rights law" and that the operations "find no justification in international law." The Human Rights Watch country team and the Just Security analysis from the three working international-law scholars — Bridgeman, Schmitt, and Goodman — converged on the same conclusion: the strikes are arbitrary deprivations of life, which is the term of art international human rights law uses for what the plainer English calls extrajudicial killing. The reason is the same in every account: lethal force is permitted as a last resort against an imminent threat to life. None of these boats was an imminent threat to anybody. They were small craft on open water, and the standard interdiction protocol — close the distance, board the vessel, arrest the occupants, seize the cargo — was available and is the protocol used in every prior counter-narcotics deployment in the same waters that any of us who served in the early 2000s can recall.

What the administration calls a non-international armed conflict against the cartels is a procedural fiction designed to relocate the killing of unarmed men in small boats from the rule-of-law column to the law-of-war column. The doctrine cannot bear that weight. Andrew Bacevich, in *Washington Rules*, named the substrate: the operating logic of the permanent-war apparatus pushes the executive branch toward the maximalist Article II self-defense theory in every administration that holds the apparatus, because the apparatus exists and must be fed with operational tempo, with new authorities, with categories of person against whom force may be applied without the procedural friction of the criminal-justice and constitutional-republican systems. The pattern crossed from the Bush administration into the Obama administration through the drone-strike program against named terrorist suspects in the tribal areas of Pakistan, from there into the next Trump administration's targeted-killing operations, from there into the Biden administration's continuation of the same authorities, and from there into the current pattern. The coalition that wrote AUMF 2001 into permanent open-ended authority is bipartisan and runs across four presidential administrations of both parties. What is happening in the Caribbean and the eastern Pacific now is what the apparatus produces when the operating-tempo logic outruns the political-class oversight that was supposed to bind it.

The political-class oversight has been asked to bind the apparatus and has refused. The Senate voted twice on War Powers resolutions in the fall of 2025 — once on October 8, once on November 6 — to terminate the operations under the 1973 Resolution's authorization-and-termination requirements. Both failed for want of Republican votes. A January 14, 2026 resolution to remove forces from hostilities within or against Venezuela tied fifty to fifty and was broken by the Vice President in favor of continued operations. The Senate Armed Services Committee Chairman, Roger Wicker of Mississippi, and the Ranking Member, Jack Reed of Rhode Island, both sent letters to the administration asking for information on the strikes. The letters went unanswered. The administration's classified Office of Legal Counsel opinion was reportedly delivered to a small group of lawmakers under classification protocols that prevent them from discussing its contents in public. The senior lawyer at SOUTHCOM raised concerns over the legality of the strikes and, by Senator Kaine's office, was overruled. The pattern is not opaque. The political-class oversight has the procedural tools that the 1973 Resolution gave it. The political-class oversight, when those tools were used, did not produce a majority that would use them.

Michael Walzer, in *Just and Unjust Wars*, argued that the discrimination requirement — the requirement to distinguish combatants from non-combatants — is the load-bearing axis of the moral substance of war. He argued it from the cases the philosophers and the international lawyers had worked through for centuries by the time the book was published in 1977. The discrimination requirement is what makes the difference between a soldier and a civilian morally significant. It is what makes the killing of a civilian morally distinct from the killing of a soldier. It is what the rules of engagement the Pentagon teaches at the War College are supposed to operationalize. The rules of engagement have language about what an operator does when an enemy combatant is no longer a combatant — when the soldier is wounded and cannot fight, when the soldier surrenders, when the soldier is in the water unarmed and clinging to the wreckage of the platform from which the soldier might once have fought. The language is not difficult. It is the language of what you do not do. The September 2 second strike on the two unarmed men clinging to the wreckage of the first strike's boat is the situation the language was written to govern. The second strike is the answer the rules of engagement teach in every accredited military-education program in the United States to be the wrong answer.

Barbara Tuchman, in *The March of Folly*, set out a four-part checklist for the kind of failure of state she called folly: the policy must be contrary to the self-interest of the state pursuing it; an alternative must have been available and named at the time; the policy must be the choice of a group rather than an individual leader; and the policy must persist past the point at which contrary evidence is available. The Caribbean strike pattern meets all four. The strategic effect on the cartels' command-and-control architecture is minimal because the architecture is on land in jurisdictions where the strikes are not taking place. The strategic effect on the relationships with the regional governments — Venezuela, Colombia, the Caribbean states whose maritime approaches are being treated as the operational area — is uniformly negative. The interdiction-and-arrest alternative was named in the SOUTHCOM senior lawyer's overruled objection. The decision is the choice of the senior administration officials in concert with the OLC and the operational command. The evidence has been on the public record since the first strike and the strikes have continued. Tuchman's checklist did not anticipate the Caribbean drug-boat pattern. The Caribbean drug-boat pattern fits her checklist exactly.

The path forward is the path the constitutional-republican apparatus was designed to operate. Congress holds the war power under Article I; the OLC memo authorizing the strikes is published and read by every senator and every representative; the Senate exercises the War Powers Resolution authorities the 1973 statute gave it and votes the operations terminated; the Department of Defense's Inspector General investigates the September 2 second-strike incident and the cumulative pattern under Article 118 of the Uniform Code of Military Justice; the operational command transitions back to the interdiction-and-arrest protocol the prior deployments used; the men picked up at sea are brought back to a courtroom and tried for the offenses for which there is evidence. None of this requires a constitutional amendment, a new statute, or a change in the political composition of the government. It requires that the institutions hold the line that the institutions were built to hold.

That this seems implausible is the diagnosis, not the situation. The implausibility is what Dwight D. Eisenhower warned of in the twenty-fourth paragraph of his farewell address of January 17, 1961: the conjunction of an immense military establishment and a large arms industry whose total influence — economic, political, even spiritual — is felt in every city, every statehouse, every office of the federal government, and against which the citizen-soldier and the citizen-legislator must guard. Sixty-five years later, the conjunction has produced an executive branch that orders missiles fired at unarmed men in small boats, a Department of Justice that writes the legal cover for the orders and keeps the cover classified, and a legislative branch that, when offered the opportunity to use the authorities the statute gives it, declines.

Rear Adm. Bradley, on September 2, 2025, was at his station at Fort Bragg, watching the operation on the screen. The first missile had hit. Two men were in the water, clinging to the wreckage. The directive from the Secretary of Defense to leave no survivors was on the line. The second missile was Bradley's order. The men in the water did not have the chance to be one thing or the other before a court. The orders had come from the senior officials in the administration and the legal cover had come from the Department of Justice and the political-class oversight had not stopped any of it. Two missiles. Eleven dead. The first of fifty-eight strikes the pattern has now produced.

That is what is being done in our name. That is what the constitutional-republican apparatus was built to prevent. The apparatus has not prevented it. The apparatus is being asked, here, by this column, in the plainest English available, to do what it was built to do.
