Alaska is stealing an 80-year-old bush pilot's airplane over a six-pack of beer.

The state's criminal forfeiture laws, as applied by the Alaska Supreme Court in *Jouppi v. Alaska*, reach the $95,000 1969 Cessna that Kenneth Jouppi flies to supply remote villages above the Arctic Circle. The offense: a passenger packed a six-pack of Budweiser in a shopping bag for a flight to Beaver, Alaska, a village where alcohol is banned. Jouppi was convicted of an alcohol-related misdemeanor. The state then moved to take his plane.

The U.S. Supreme Court agreed Monday to hear Jouppi's challenge. The question is whether the forfeiture violates the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause, which prohibits the government from imposing penalties that are "grossly disproportional" to the offense. *Timbs v. Indiana*, 586 U.S. 146, 150 (2019).

The disproportionality speaks for itself. The plane is worth $95,000. The underlying offense is a misdemeanor involving a six-pack of beer. Jouppi did not own the beer. He did not drink it. He was preparing to fly a passenger who had packed it with groceries. The state's theory is that the plane itself is forfeitable because it was used to facilitate the transport of alcohol into a dry village.

The Alaska Supreme Court accepted that theory. Under the state's criminal forfeiture statutes, the court held, the plane is subject to seizure because it was "used or intended for use" in the commission of the crime. The court did not conduct a proportionality analysis under the Eighth Amendment, concluding that the Excessive Fines Clause does not apply to Alaska's forfeiture laws in the same way.

That conclusion is in direct tension with *Timbs*, in which the Court held that the Excessive Fines Clause applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Timbs* involved Indiana's seizure of a $42,000 Land Rover after a drug offense. The Court ruled that the forfeiture could violate the Eighth Amendment if the penalty was grossly disproportional to the gravity of the offense.

The facts in *Jouppi* are more stark. The plane is Jouppi's livelihood. He flies it to supply remote villages — Beaver, Fort Yukon, and Venetie among them — where air travel is the only connection to the state's road network. Without the plane, the livelihood disappears. The passenger brought a six-pack. The state's response is to take the man's career.

The Court's decision to grant certiorari in *Jouppi* is itself notable. The Court has been willing to hear Excessive Fines Clause cases in the past — *Timbs* was a unanimous decision — but has also been reluctant to extend the clause's reach in ways that would limit state forfeiture regimes. The grant signals that at least four justices believe the Alaska Supreme Court's reading of *Timbs* is wrong, or at least worth correcting.

The case is also a reminder of how forfeiture works in practice. The government seizes property, often worth vastly more than the underlying offense, and then puts the burden on the owner to get it back. The disproportionality is the feature, not the bug. The state gets the asset; the owner gets a lawsuit that lasts a decade.

Jouppi, now in his 80s, has been fighting the forfeiture for fourteen years. "I see it as my duty to ensure that the Bill of Rights actually means something," he said. The Court's decision to hear the case suggests that at least four justices agree the question is serious enough to warrant review.

The state will invoke the real harms of alcohol in dry villages. That is a genuine problem. It does not justify stealing a man's airplane over his passenger's grocery bag. The state's argument: the forfeiture laws deter pilots from transporting alcohol into dry villages, and the plane was the instrumentality of the crime.

But the instrumentality argument has limits. The plane was carrying groceries. The passenger packed beer with other food. Jouppi denies knowing the beer was in his passenger's bag. The forfeiture is not about deterring the pilot; it is about taking the asset.

The Eighth Amendment is supposed to check that kind of government overreach. The Court's decision in *Timbs* said so. The question in *Jouppi* is whether the Court means it.

This is a case where the Court has the opportunity to enforce the Bill of Rights against a forfeiture regime that has lost all sense of proportion. The Court has [already ruled](/articles/2026-06-18-supreme-court-rules-government-cannot-automatically-bar-all-drug-users-from-owni/) this term that the government cannot automatically bar all drug users from owning guns — a case about the limits of government power to impose sweeping penalties. *Jouppi* is the same principle: the government cannot take a man's livelihood because a passenger packed a six-pack.

The outcome will tell us whether *Timbs* was a real constraint or just a polite suggestion.
