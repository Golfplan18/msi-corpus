Trump and Senate Republicans are closing rural maternity wards to pay for billionaire tax cuts. The bill writes the trade into the same legislation, the same week, the same vote. The math is the math.

Here is the chain, in order, with the documentation named at each link. The UNC Sheps Center has tracked rural-hospital closures since 2005. Their count is one hundred and ninety-five — one hundred and eight full shutter-the-doors closures and eighty-seven conversions to facilities that no longer carry acute inpatient care. The Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform tracks the at-risk list ahead of the closure list. Their current count is seven hundred and sixty-eight rural hospitals at risk and three hundred and fifteen at immediate risk within three years. The Sheps Center, the CHQPR, the March of Dimes, and the state hospital associations all carry the same closure sequence: the labor-and-delivery ward goes first, then the inpatient pediatric service, then the ICU, then the front door. L&D goes first because L&D is the lowest-margin service line a hospital runs, because Medicaid pays close to half of cost on births, and because Medicaid covers a large share of rural births. Cut the reimbursement and the math at the L&D ward collapses before the math at any other wing does.

This is what the bill the Senate Republican leadership shepherded, the House Republican majority wrote, and the Trump administration signed on July 4, 2025 does. Public Law 119-21 — the One Big Beautiful Bill Act — cuts Medicaid through four mechanisms at once. It imposes work-reporting requirements on Medicaid expansion adults, effective January 1, 2027, that the Congressional Budget Office projects will drop 5.3 million people from coverage by 2034. It tightens the redetermination cadence on expansion adults, dropping another 700,000. It freezes existing state provider taxes and caps new ones, cutting federal Medicaid spending by $225.7 billion over ten years and dropping another 1.2 million. The aggregate is 10 million additional uninsured by 2034 — 7.5 million from Medicaid alone — on CBO's own numbers. The Joint Committee on Taxation's and the Tax Policy Center's distributional tables show what the cuts pay for: the top one percent of households gets an average tax benefit of about seventy thousand dollars, an income gain of more than four percent, with the top decile capturing roughly eighty percent of the bill's net value. In dollar terms in 2026 alone, the richest one percent gets $121 billion in net tax cuts; the entire middle quintile, twenty times as many households, gets $56 billion. The bottom two quintiles get about five percent of the total. That is the trade. The hospital wards in counties like Adams County are closing to fund a tax cut for households that will never set foot in those wards.

Wendell Berry, in *The Unsettling of America*, made the analytical move that does the work here. The book's argument is that the rules a rural economy runs by get written by and for the consolidated operator, and the small operator — the family farm, the one-shop village, the small rural hospital — does not lose because the small operator is less wanted by the people who depend on it. The small operator loses because the rules of the game are written to make the small operator unable to clear the bar the rules set. The 2025 Medicaid provisions are this exact operation transposed from agriculture to medicine. Rural hospitals are not closing because rural counties have decided they would rather drive ninety minutes to deliver a baby. They are closing because the federal reimbursement formula that keeps an L&D ward solvent in a county of twenty thousand people has been narrowed by the people who write the formula, and the people who write the formula are the same coalition whose rhetoric celebrates the small-town America the formula is hollowing out. *We Too* Chapter 16 names this the Community Collapse contradiction — the coalition that says "small-town values" is the coalition that closes the small town's hospital.

The bill's defenders will point to the Rural Health Transformation Program — Section 71401, fifty billion dollars over five years, half distributed equally to approved states and half by formula. Kaiser's analysis of the fund is the analysis that controls. Fifty billion over five years cannot offset a trillion in Medicaid cuts over ten. The fund is general-purpose, not labor-and-delivery-targeted, and it runs through state-administered application programs unlikely to land in the bank accounts of Critical Access Hospitals on the schedule the closure cascade is already running on. Wisconsin's projected loss in federal Medicaid funds alone is around $1.7 billion over the ten-year window per Kaiser's allocation across the states — larger than any plausible Wisconsin share of the fund. Joel Salatin has been saying for fifteen years that the rural infrastructure consolidated industries depend on is the rural infrastructure consolidated industries refuse to maintain; the fund is the maintenance gesture without the maintenance budget.

The symmetric application is the honest one. Rural-hospital closures have run through both partisan eras. In the ten states that refused the Affordable Care Act's Medicaid expansion for political reasons, rural hospitals closed faster than in the expansion states, and the Democratic Party has not produced what the moment requires either — federal investment in rural hospital infrastructure as critical infrastructure, on the model the Rural Electrification Administration applied to rural electricity in the 1930s, with the structural-funding floor that lets an L&D ward exist whether the next county's population is twenty thousand or two thousand. That floor has never been built. The 2025 cuts are the acceleration. The bipartisan failure to treat rural medicine as infrastructure is the substrate. Naming the substrate sharpens the acceleration rather than softening it. The people who wrote the 2025 bill knew the substrate and wrote the acceleration into the same legislation as the tax cut.

Wisconsin has somewhere around thirty Critical Access Hospitals — the federal designation for the small rural facility with twenty-five or fewer inpatient beds and the cost-based-reimbursement structure designed to keep those facilities open because they are the only hospital within a thirty-five-mile drive of the populations they serve. Three Wisconsin hospitals are publicly named in the at-risk reporting: Holy Family Memorial in Manitowoc, Mayo Clinic Health System Oakridge in Osseo, Aspirus Stanley. Western Wisconsin Health's labor-and-delivery patient population runs about a quarter Medicaid. Wisconsin Public Radio's coverage indicates tens of thousands of Wisconsinites projected to lose coverage under the bill. Wisconsin's congressional delegation includes Republican members and senators whose districts contain the rural counties most exposed to the closure cascade. Those members voted for the bill that accelerates the closures. The constituents in those counties will pay the price the donors who wrote the bill will not.

The bench notebook for May records what is visible on the section-line drive out to County G: the regional-chain veterinary clinic sign has finally come down off the building that used to be the small-animal practice, and the lights are out behind the windows of the storefront that used to be the bank branch Sara's mother worked at for thirty years. The lights at the hospital are still on. The lights at the L&D ward will go out on the schedule the bill's authors wrote into the statute. That schedule is the policy. The policy is the bill. The bill is the trade.

The affirmative is the one Berry's frame demands and the one the membership of a county like Adams County would write if a county were the unit of policy accounting. Federal investment in rural hospital infrastructure as critical infrastructure on the REA template. Medicaid reimbursement stabilized as the load-bearing floor it has functioned as for forty years. The L&D ward at every Critical Access Hospital funded as the public good it is. The 2025 reconciliation bill does the opposite of this on every dimension. The choice was made by named people who will face their constituents again. The constituents in counties like Adams County deserve the truth said plain. That is what the trade was. That is what the vote was.
