*Public safety begins where armed power stops entering every door.*

Donald Trump's agents shot and killed two American citizens.

An ICE officer killed Renee Nicole Good in Minneapolis in January. Customs and Border Patrol agents killed Alex Pretti in the same city later that month. Four days after Pretti's killing, and twenty-one days after Good's, Massachusetts introduced the bill that became the PROTECT Act. A state looked at the crackdown and wrote a law in answer.

On Wednesday, Governor Maura Healey signed the law at La Colaborativa in Chelsea, a Latina-led nonprofit economic-development agency in the heart of the immigrant community. She stood among immigrants, advocates, and law-enforcement officers. She stood among the people the law protects.

The law takes effect immediately. It bans warrantless immigration arrests at courthouses, schools, hospitals, health-care facilities, and child-care centers. It prohibits local police from entering formal partnerships with ICE. It bars state and municipal agencies from participating in the federal machinery of immigration enforcement while preserving their authority to investigate and prosecute actual crimes. It gives people a cause of action against government officials who violate their constitutional rights. It strengthens protections for people held in county jails. Healey called it the strongest state-level protection against ICE in the country.

Read plainly, it says this: In Massachusetts, a school is a school. A hospital is a hospital. A courthouse is a place of justice, not a net. The local officer is the neighbor's protector, not a recruiter for the deportation machine.

These are not random places. They are where a life is lived. They are where a child learns, where a patient seeks care, where a witness asks for justice, where a family brings its children because it believes the state has not yet become an enemy.

Washington has an answer, and it is always the same sentence: sanctuary politicians are putting politics above public safety.

Let me take that claim seriously. The people who make it deserve an answer, not dismissal. Yes, if local police will not hold people for the federal machine, some people who have broken the law may remain in their communities. That is the true half.

Here is the suppressed half: a community that cannot trust its own police will not call them. The victim stays silent. The witness looks away. The crime goes unsolved. Public safety was never built by making every patrol officer an agent of deportation. It was built on the willingness of the harmed to ask for help.

Massachusetts' public-safety secretary said it plainly: strong public safety requires the trust of the communities the police serve. The state law preserves local power to investigate and prosecute crimes. It refuses only to convert local government into an arm of a federal deportation operation.

The administration has signed $70 billion into the enforcement machine. It has threatened New York, and New York refused to bend. Its infrastructure is expanding. Its agents went to Minneapolis. Two American citizens are dead.

The obligation is not optional. It is not a suggestion for the particularly devout. It is a binding command issued to the nation as a whole, rooted in the nation's memory of having been the stranger.

Every American family has a land of Egypt in its past. The work of the commandment is to remember.

What Washington calls obstruction, the tradition calls sanctuary: the place where the hunted person can reach safety before power lays hands on him.

The word *sanctuary* comes from the Latin *sanctuarium*: a holy place, a place of refuge. It is one of the oldest words in the moral vocabulary of the West.
