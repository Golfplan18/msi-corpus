Texas passed seven anti-trans bills last year. This is what it built: a four-day summer camp running on donated kayaks and Frito-Lays boxes, where an eleven-year-old named Velvet heard their name spoken in public for the first time. The Guardian's dispatch from [Color Splash Out](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/04/texas-summer-camp-trans-kids) — reported by Nicky Yeager, photographed by Salgu Wissmath — is a portrait of that camp: pay-what-you-can, which means most kids pay nothing; run by a non-binary minister named Yadi Martínez-Reyna; held at a venue that changes every year, for safety reasons and because the budget is a shoestring.

Give the camp its due before I take a knife to the space it's been wedged into. This is the best story of the American summer, and not because it's small. The kids are not the problem. Velvet, eleven, not out to most of their heavily Catholic family, drifting in that lake; Nix Pasivan, thirteen, whose public school made them cut their hair and who sobbed through it, saying "I'm so excited to be here, I just keep saying that"; Alastair Mendoza, seventeen, who swam in public without thinking twice and plans to come back as a fellow so nobody else has to come out alone. That is what safety looks like. It's four days long, it's an eight-hour drive from home, and next summer it'll be somewhere else.

Now count what it runs on. Supplies recycled from schools. Banned books donated by school librarians who had to comply with the state's policies to keep their jobs. Kayaks lent by a returning camper's mom, who also volunteers as the camp nurse. Group projects built out of Frito-Lays boxes. Funding scraped from three progressive reverends and the director's friends, after the federal cuts to nonprofits. That is the cost structure of a safe place in a state that spent last year making it unsafe on purpose.

The Rio Grande Valley, where most of these kids come from, is one of the poorest regions in the country — and it has no queer youth organizations at all. Zero. Not one. That is not an accident of geography. It is the product of a session that banned these kids from the bathrooms in government buildings, prohibited them from amending their own records, and expanded a 2023 ban on the medical care their doctors recommended — all wrapped in the fearmongering that sells the package. Every one of those laws tells a kid the same thing: this place is not for you.

Here's the old American story underneath it. The barn burns down and the neighbors show up with lumber. The funeral happens and the casserole brigade appears. A child needs four days of being called by the right name, and a minister's friends cover what the reverends don't. None of it gets invoiced. Nobody calls the casserole a political program. That is real, it is good, and it is not enough. Casseroles are what communities make when the public arrangements fail — a complement to a decent common life, never a replacement for it. And when the only thing between an eleven-year-old and a whole state's message is a casserole, the casserole isn't the triumph. It's the receipt.

The state didn't make these children disappear — it doesn't know how. It made their safety into a charity, and the charity into a yard sale. The camp didn't fail; the legislators did, at their one actual job, which is keeping their constituents' kids safe. A state that wanted these families to be fine would fund a youth center in the Valley. It would stand behind the librarians who donated those books instead of forcing them to comply under threat. It would spend its session on the thing the camp has to beg for.

And here's the part nobody at the capitol sees, because it lives at the level of the lake, not the dome. The camp is not the fragile thing in this story. A legislative session is a very expensive way to build a very small amount of cruelty. The camp, in the same year, built houses named the Glitter Gorgons and the Rainbow Legends out of recycled cardboard and a borrowed kayak, and invited every camper who turned eighteen to come back as a fellow and pay it forward. That's the amortization schedule of the one model of community that actually compounds — in kids who know their own names.

Texas passed seven laws to make sure these children knew they weren't wanted. The Valley answered with a lake, a kayak, and a house called the Rainbow Legends. I know which side of that ledger gets the better return.
