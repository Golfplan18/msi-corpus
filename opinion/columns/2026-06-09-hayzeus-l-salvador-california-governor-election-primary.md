Steve Hilton and Xavier Becerra trade your poverty for campaign advantage. The primary is decided: Hilton, the British-born former strategist turned U.S. citizen since 2021, will face Becerra in November. One pitches a pragmatic MAGA makeover. The other defends the machinery that produced the highest poverty rate in the nation. They stand before the voters, and neither man asks what it actually costs to be poor in a state that has spent thirty-six years treating the hungry as a problem to be managed rather than neighbors to be fed.

You know the cost. You know it when the rent comes due and the paycheck does not stretch, when a trip to the grocery store turns into arithmetic you cannot solve. The politicians know the cost too, but they see it differently. They translate your hunger into polling data. They package your fear into television advertisements. The U.S. and Mexican bishops warned us in 2003, in *Strangers No Longer*, never to reduce human beings to policy instruments—yet your survival becomes the raw material of their ambitions the moment a camera turns on. I concede that neither man single-handedly built the poverty they now campaign on. But I also know that we who have cheered from the sidelines have treated the lives of the struggling as the currency of our political victories, sharing a viral clip that mocks the homeless or convincing ourselves that a moderate checkmark washes our hands of the mess. The complicity is ours, and it is not washed away by choosing the lesser candidate.

The accusation holds symmetrically across the ballot. Hilton is a foreign-born operator wearing a MAGA endorsement and pointing at career politicians; Becerra is a career politician pointing at the hollow promises of the machine that has controlled Sacramento since a Republican last won statewide in 2006. One promises to cut your costs. The other promises his experience will shield you from them. It does not matter which side holds the microphone, because the mechanism is the same. When you treat the hungry as an audience to be managed, you are no longer doing politics; you are performing a rite whose meaning has long since drained away, a closed clergy administering what the laity have stopped believing in.

And that performance rests on a deliberate starvation. For decades, California has concentrated authority in the capital and gutted the intermediate bodies—the families, the parishes, the neighborhood associations, the local school boards—that the principle of subsidiarity says should bear the weight of the common life. The result is not a mystery. Registered Democrats outnumber Republicans nearly two to one; the Democratic machine has captured roughly sixty percent of the vote in three consecutive gubernatorial contests. After thirty‑six years of that lock on power, California leads the nation in poverty, in unemployment, in the sheer cost of staying alive. That is not a coincidence. It is the predictable fruit of a political order that has treated the poor as a statistical externality rather than as bearers of inviolable dignity.

Even Hilton’s stump-speech instincts—clean up the corruption, cut your costs, help your business, fix our schools—are little more than a garbled echo of subsidiarity from the business side. Cut your costs: stop making the state’s regulatory apparatus a tax on family life. Help your business: let the local economic actor, not the Sacramento planner, direct resources where the need is actually felt. Fix our schools: return genuine authority to parents and to communities, instead of treating education as a delivery pipeline for ideological compliance. None of this is a fully‑formed social doctrine; it is a collection of instincts, entangled with a president whose moral gravity is an open question. But for a state that has squandered a generation starving the mediating institutions to death, even garbled instincts are a confession of how far the center has drifted from the common good.

Meanwhile, Becerra has already made the status quo his platform. He promises no change to the policies that delivered the nation’s highest poverty rate, and the Democratic coalition rewards him for it. That is not governance. That is an incumbent’s defense of a machine whose only remaining function is its own perpetuation.

But the real temptation for any observer of conscience is not to choose between the two. It is to stay out of the fight because neither man passes a personal‑virtue screen. That is a mistake. The Church insists on personal conversion *and* structural justice, and when a system has become so deformed that it can produce forty million people living inside a coast‑and‑desert experiment with the nation’s worst poverty, structural justice is long overdue. The Torah commands the community to leave the corners of the field for the gleaning poor, recognizing that survival is a right in a land that produces enough for everyone. Amos did not tell the exploiters to moderate their greed; he told them they were trampling the needy and bringing ruin upon the poor. The same prophetic word lands on the British strategist and the career Democrat with equal weight.

A just political vision would not treat poverty as a moral failure to be punished or a statistical anomaly to be debated on cable news. It would treat a living wage, affordable housing, and reliable public schools as the floor of a common life, not the ceiling of political ambition. That vision does not arrive by choosing the candidate with the better‑sounding slogan. It arrives when the neighbors who are being traded like commodities decide to stop being traded.

The door is still open. Both men can put down the slogans and begin the work of repair. But you do not need a British strategist to tell you that your life is a heavy ledger, and you do not need a career politician to define the shape of your suffering. Romero did not tell his congregation to pick the safer candidate in a rigged system. He told the soldier pointing the weapon that God’s law is higher than the general’s command and that conscience is the only thing worth keeping. Join a tenant union. Start a neighborhood food share. Sit in at the zoning meeting when the eviction vote happens. The work ahead does not wait for an election to end. Lay down the anger. Pick up the tools. Fix what is broken.
