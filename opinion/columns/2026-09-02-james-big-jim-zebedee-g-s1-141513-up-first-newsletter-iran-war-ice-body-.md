The dead are people, not talking points. Iranian mothers are burying their children this week because Washington's second wave of strikes dismantled Iranian air defenses, radar, and mine-laying capability along the Strait of Hormuz. Kuwaiti refinery workers are clocking in wondering if today is the day their plant lights up. Bahraini dockworkers are moving containers off ships because the freight has to move even when the drones are overhead. American families in uniform are checking their phones for orders that haven't come yet and praying they don't.

Bombing another country doesn't come free. Somebody's kid flies the plane. Somebody's neighbor back home is going to hand over four dollars a gallon next Tuesday because the Strait just got more dangerous. The cost lands on people who work with their hands and pay their taxes and have about as much say in what gets blown up tonight as the Iranian mothers burying their children.

Washington is framing this as response. The second wave came after the missile attack on the Jordan base, and the brass is calling it retaliation. Maybe so. But the first wave — the one that took out the radar and the mine-laying capability that protect Iranian shipping — that was Washington's. Dismantling a country's defenses and then accusing it of aggression is not defense. That is preparation for something bigger. I've seen that move before. So have most men and women who ever wore the uniform. You do not bomb a country and expect silence. The return strike was always coming. Washington knew it.

The travel warnings now hitting Americans across the Middle East are not an Iranian weapon. They are the bill for decisions made in Washington. The people paying it are the American families the State Department just told to leave, and the Gulf port and refinery workers in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Iraq who live there, and the families on both sides of the water who will bury somebody this week.

The pattern is older than this week. Twelve consecutive nights of strikes ran on the same logic. I've seen this script more times than I want to count — Iran does something, Washington escalates, Iran answers, Washington escalates again, and the language always puts Washington in the response seat no matter who moved first. The only thing that changes is the wave number.

Smedley Butler spent thirty-three years in the Marine Corps and put it plain in the end — war is a racket, run for the benefit of the very few at the expense of the very many. He was talking about 1935. He could have been writing about this week. The few make the call. The rest of us get the bill.

What is new is the widening of the theater. The travel warnings aren't a footnote — they're the quiet admission that this is no longer a contained fight between militaries. The refinery workers in Bahrain and the families the State Department just told to shelter in place are living through that admission right now. So are the rest of us, who will pay four dollars a gallon next Tuesday and not think about it long, because that is what war costs when nobody at the top is paying attention to the bill.

The third wave is already in preparation. The question that has not been answered is whether anyone in Washington is actually planning for what comes after, or whether the next round of strikes is itself the plan. And the question that has not been asked, that nobody in a suit will ask out loud, is what the people on the ground in Kuwait and Bahrain, the Iranian mothers, and the American families paying at the pump think about it — because they are the ones paying for every minute of it.
