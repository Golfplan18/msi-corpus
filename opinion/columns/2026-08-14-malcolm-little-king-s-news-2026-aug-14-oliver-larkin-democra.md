Jared Moskowitz accepted a district the governor built for him, and the Democratic Party's operating class has named the transaction "electability." He is the two-term congressman who calls himself a "DeSantis Democrat," the governor's appointee to run the state's emergency-management division, the first congressional Democrat to enlist in the "department of government efficiency" project that exists to dismantle the federal apparatus he was sent to Washington to staff, and the House vote who cast for the Laken Riley Act at the precise moment the governor's marquee immigration detention camp was being shuttered under a documented accumulation of civil-rights complaints. He has agreed to no debates. He has held no public events with voters. He walked into the new 25th because his own district was redrawn into a hostile Republican shape, and the governor drew him a softer one in its place. The party's institutional reasoning tells us this is the choice of a competent professional; it is, in fact, the choice of a man who needed a district in order to keep his seat.

That is the entire Democratic establishment argument in a single transaction, and it ought to be named.

The price of "electability" was the 25th congressional district: a coastal strip from Delray Beach to Miami Beach that exists only because a recent U.S. Supreme Court ruling stripped the operative teeth out of the Voting Rights Act and Governor Ron DeSantis, working at the speed of a man who had been waiting for exactly that ruling, immediately redrew the Florida map to deliver as many as four additional Republican seats — a redraw that [forced sitting Florida House members like Pia Dandiya to relocate their campaigns](/articles/2026-08-06-pia-dandiya-moves-florida-house-campaign-after-map-redraw/). Sixty-five percent of the new 25th's electorate is white; twenty percent is Hispanic; five percent is Black. More than sixty percent of the voters are over fifty. The district that Debbie Wasserman Schultz had held for years was deleted to make room for a seat shaped to the specifications of a man who had just lost the geography he used to own. Moskowitz opted into that gift rather than fight a Republican-leaning seat elsewhere. The party calls it pragmatism. It is acceptance. So this is how your representation dies: not with a coup, but with a map drawn by the man who chose your congressman.

What Moskowitz did with the seat is the rest of the record. He became the first House Democrat to join Trump's DOGE initiative — the president's cost-cutting apparatus aimed at dismantling federal agencies and programs. He voted for the Laken Riley Act, which expanded ICE detention authority — the same apparatus civil rights groups reported committing severe human rights violations at the Florida detention camp where his opponent announced his candidacy. He maintained unwavering support for Israel and voted against a resolution to limit US military involvement in the conflict with Iran. He has refused to defend any of this in a public debate. This is the record that "DeSantis Democrat" describes: not bipartisan cooperation, but the gentle disappearance of the opposition.

His allies cast this as "pragmatic" survival. The argument is straightforward: Florida's government is dominated by the Republican apparatus, so a Democrat who cooperates with that apparatus is more electable than one who confronts it. The political scientist Sean D Foreman at Barry University frames the question bluntly: "The anti-socialism card has been played for so long that it's ingrained in our politics here."

The argument has a true half. Florida's government is dominated by one party. A Democrat in this environment faces genuine strategic constraints. Conceding that is not optional — it is the honest starting point.

But the "pragmatism" frame suppresses the variable that inverts its conclusion. The question is: what is the difference between accommodation and collaboration? Accommodation means working with the other party on shared goals while maintaining independent judgment. Collaboration means joining the other party's initiatives, voting for their signature legislation, accepting their patronage, and refusing to face your own voters in public debate — then calling the result bipartisanship.

Moskowitz did not merely survive one-party dominance. He was selected by the governor for a state role, joined the president's cost-cutting initiative, and voted for the governing party's immigration enforcement bill. At each step, the collaboration was not a reluctant compromise but an active choice — and at each step, the beneficiary was the same political apparatus that gerrymandered the district to produce exactly this kind of Democrat.

The cui-bono trace is clean. Who benefits from a "DeSantis Democrat"? The apparatus that redrew the district benefits from a Democrat who cooperates with the redistricting, joins their initiatives, votes for their priorities, and never forces them to defend their record in a competitive race. The cost-bearer is the electorate — voters redrawn into a less competitive seat, represented by a man who will not look them in the eye at a town hall.

Against Moskowitz, Oliver Larkin is running a campaign that is, by any honest accounting, doing more with less. He is thirty-four. He grew up in Fort Lauderdale, the son of a retired army officer. He was working a minimum-wage line-cook job after college when Bernie Sanders' 2016 campaign swallowed him whole; he quit, drove to New Hampshire, and went from unpaid volunteer to running the campaign's Portsmouth field office. He organized in Nevada, Kansas, Utah, New York, Rhode Island. He spent the following years in Washington unionizing his own workplace before moving back to south Florida in 2022. He announced his congressional run at a protest outside "Alligator Alcatraz," the now-shuttered state immigration detention camp. His platform is not a mood board: government-funded universal healthcare, free tuition at public universities, the construction of millions of new affordable housing units, the protection and expansion of temporary protected status, and the abolition of ICE. He is a self-described "cat dad" and drag-show enthusiast who says he wants south Florida to be a safe and welcoming place for everyone. He is, in the polite Beltway phrase, an extremist; in the actual language of canvassing, he is the candidate who shows up.

Five weeks before the primary, in ninety-five-degree heat, Larkin walked a Fort Lauderdale block in a dark suit. A resident who asked why he was wearing the suit got the line: "I'm dressed for the job I want." A longtime resident named George told him he had his vote after a five-minute conversation about lowering property-insurance rates in a neighborhood that had flooded three years prior. A Haitian immigrant who had lived in Fort Lauderdale for thirty-nine years told him he did not see what politicians had done for the city and, after another five minutes of policy, not ideology, said: "I'll vote for you." Larkin never used the word socialism at the door. He described what socialism actually does. That is the entire argument the Democratic establishment cannot bring itself to make, because making it concedes that the label has been lying about the contents.

That is what a campaign looks like when it shows up at the door. What Moskowitz's campaign looks like is harder to describe, because Moskowitz has largely avoided public events with voters and has not agreed to a single debate. Larkin's campaign has made the contrast explicit. "Congressman Moskowitz doesn't want to be accountable to voters in this district," Larkin said. Moskowitz declined to comment.

The organizing engine behind the campaign is real. About seventy-five volunteers — some of whom drove in from counties on the other side of the state — knocked on thousands of doors across the district in a single weekend. As of days before the primary, the campaign had knocked on more than thirty-five thousand doors and made more than one hundred seventy thousand calls to voters. Larkin has raised just over a million dollars from nearly twenty thousand unique donors, a portrait of small-dollar dependence that the party's consulting class treats as a liability. He has leaned into the wider [DSA wave that has now swept Democratic primaries this year](/articles/2026-06-24-leftist-wave-hits-democratic-primaries-dsa-backed-candidates-win-big/), appearing alongside Hasan Piker at a virtual fundraiser that pulled in roughly sixty thousand dollars in a single night and citing a fundraising surge of nearly a hundred and twenty thousand dollars in the week after the New York City primary wins. Moskowitz, by contrast, has raised nearly three million dollars from the usual donor pool, declined to debate, declined to comment on the absence of a field operation, and is now watched over by operatives who treat any disagreement with their judgment as a kind of factional indiscipline.

The polls tell the story the establishment does not want told. A Beacon Insights survey in June had Moskowitz leading Larkin fifty-one percent to nineteen percent. A poll last week from the Center for Strategic Politics had the race in a statistical tie. Larkin's own internal polling showed a wide head-to-head deficit that narrowed sharply once voters were given information about both candidates' records — including Moskowitz's affiliation with the federal efficiency project and Larkin's status as a first-time candidate and democratic socialist. Information moves voters. The party does not want to give voters information. That is the entire game.

The case against Larkin is the case the establishment always makes: he cannot win a general election. Steven Schale, a longtime Florida Democratic strategist and friend of Moskowitz's, put it on the record: "If Oliver Larkin wins that primary, he will lose the general election, and he will probably lose it pretty badly." Foreman, the Barry University political scientist, notes that the anti-socialism card has been played in Florida for so long that it is now part of the terrain — and adds that he is not sure the conditions for a democratic-socialist breakthrough exist in the state yet. The argument runs: the district is too wealthy, the electorate is too old, the Jewish Democratic population in the 25th is too large, the party has been punished for ideological overreach too many times, and the safer play is the man who can survive a general.

That argument has a name: it is the argument that cost the Democratic Party seven million voters between 2020 and 2024. It is the argument that produced the long string of special-election collapses and under-performance in non-Trump cycles. It is the argument that has, in state after state, delivered legislatures to Republican supermajorities by nominating the safest possible candidates and watching those candidates fail to turn out the base. The party has now spent a decade losing the same way and has decided to lose the same way harder, with the additional flourish of telling the voters who want something different that they are the problem.

Schale, to his credit, added something more honest in the same conversation — that the DSA "provides real opportunities for young people" to lead campaigns, and that part of the movement's appeal is a "legitimate frustration with incumbents who should have gone home 15 years ago." Even the establishment strategist acknowledges the structure his party's "pragmatism" has produced. The DSA's national co-chair argues the organization's growth in rural and Republican-leaning areas shows the model can travel beyond deep-blue cities. Foreman offers a different read: "The democratic socialists have already won this round in Florida, even if none of their candidates win." Both assessments miss the structural point. The question is not whether democratic socialism can win in Florida. The question is whether a political system that gerrymanders districts to produce compliant Democrats and then calls the result pragmatism can survive being named for what it is.

The establishment has noticed the contradiction. In June, after the wave of socialist primary victories, [centrist Democrats began pushing for a formal organizational break](/articles/2026-06-29-centrist-democrats-push-for-formal-break-after-socialist-primary-wins/) — a separate party, a separate caucus, a separate vehicle for the consultant-and-DLC instinct that has now been losing elections for a generation. The argument for the break is that the Democratic Party has been captured by the loudest left flank and cannot speak to the median voter. The argument against the break — the argument the centrist case cannot bring itself to make — is that the median voter has been telling the party's pollsters, in survey after survey, that they want the things Larkin is selling: a lower cost of living, an end to insurance-driven foreclosures, an end to the immigration detention state, a healthcare system that does not bankrupt them when they get sick. The "median voter" the establishment keeps losing to is the median voter the establishment refuses to nominate for.

Florida is the test case the centrist case cannot survive. The state's record on progressive ballot measures, when progressive measures are actually put to the voters, is not the record of a closed reactionary mind. In 2018, nearly sixty-five percent of Florida voters approved restoring voting rights to most Floridians with prior felony convictions. In 2020, sixty-one percent of the state's voters approved a fifteen-dollar minimum wage amendment in the very same election Donald Trump carried the state. The voters who passed both of those measures did not disappear between 2020 and 2026. They are inside the 25th. They are the residents whose streets flooded while their insurance rates climbed and whose cost of living outpaced their wages for the entire decade the establishment was telling them the path forward was restraint. They are the people Larkin is canvassing. They are the ones saying "I'll vote for you" before he has said "democratic socialist."

The "DeSantis Democrat" frame is supposed to inoculate Moskowitz against the charge that he is, in policy terms, a slightly more cautious version of the governor's preferred outcome. The inoculation has not held. Moskowitz took the emergency-management appointment from DeSantis. He was the first House Democrat to join the federal efficiency initiative. He voted for the Laken Riley Act — to expand detention authority — during the season his own state's marquee detention facility was being closed under civil-rights pressure. He voted against a resolution to limit U.S. military involvement in Iran. His allies argue that none of this matters because the general election will be decided by affordability and not by ideology. They are right that affordability will decide the election; they are wrong that none of this matters. The voters in the 25th who are losing their homes to insurance non-renewal, who are paying more every year for less healthcare coverage, who watched a governor spend the last four years building a detention state in their backyard — those voters are deciding affordability. They are deciding it in exactly the terms Larkin is laying down at the door.

On Tuesday the primary will end one way or another, and the party's consultants will read the result through whichever lens the morning after demands. If Moskowitz wins narrowly, they will tell you the verdict is electability. If Larkin wins, they will tell you the verdict is the failure of electability as a strategy and the dawn of a new conversation about what the party is for. Either way, the conversation Larkin is having on Fort Lauderdale doorsteps — about the property-insurance rate and the flooded street and the public hospital and the immigrant neighbor and the cost of staying alive — is the conversation the Democratic Party's professional class has spent a decade refusing to have. He is doing it in a dark suit, on the hottest day of the summer, in a district the governor drew for his opponent. That is not a seed being planted for a future movement. That is a door being opened in the present tense. The party that does not want him is the party that has been telling its own voters, for a generation now, that the price of electability is the policy of the man they are losing to.

That is the transaction. The price has been paid. The question is whether the voters in the new 25th recognize what they have been sold.
