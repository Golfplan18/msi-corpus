Texas legislators made trans children pay for their political panic.

The evidence is not hidden in a committee transcript. It is a teenager in a kayak, moving through reeds and minnows without the fear that usually accompanies swimming in public. The camp is Color Splash Out. It is a 13-year-old returning from a four-day gathering to a town in the Rio Grande Valley where, by their account, safe queer community is nearly impossible to find. It is an 11-year-old hearing their chosen name spoken aloud in public for the first time.

Color Splash Out is a temporary, pay-what-you-can gathering for queer and trans youth. Its location changes each year because safety concerns and a shoestring budget make permanence difficult. Its supplies come from recycled school materials, donated books, borrowed kayaks and a network of adults who refuse to let the children they love be left alone with the consequences of somebody else's legislative theater.

This is what the state has produced: not safety, but scarcity. Not protection, but a private rescue operation assembled from religious leaders, friends, former campers, volunteer nurses and whatever materials can be found.

The source article reports that Texas passed seven anti-trans bills in 2025, including a government-building bathroom ban, restrictions on changing gender identity in certain records and an expansion of the state's prohibition on medical transition care for minors. The public language surrounding such measures invokes safety, children and parental authority. The distributional result is simpler. The state makes ordinary life more dangerous and then leaves community organizations to repair the damage with donated books and Frito-Lay boxes.

That is not an unintended side effect. It is the policy.

The honest half of the argument is that children deserve protection. Public institutions do have duties around safety, privacy and care. Schools should address bullying. Parents should not be erased from their children's lives. Any serious account begins there.

But the Texas apparatus does not stop there. It takes the legitimate concern for children's welfare and routes it through a political hierarchy in which trans children become the designated cost-bearers. Their names are questioned, their records narrowed, their access to care restricted and their schools made less reliable as places of belonging. The claimed beneficiary is “safety.” The actual beneficiary is the officeholder who can perform protection by making a vulnerable minority carry the fear.

That is the cui-bono question the official language is built to avoid: who gains when a child is turned into a public threat?

Frame-engineered relabeling is the deliberate substitution of terms with different meanings in order to shift how the underlying issue is understood. Here the public referent is a child trying to live, learn and participate without humiliation. The political label is danger. The result is that the child must prove their innocence before receiving the ordinary protections other children are presumed to have.

The apparatus then compounds the first injury with institutional abandonment. The camp's director, Adora Ysaguirre, described being doxxed, losing a teaching job and facing harassment and physical assaults because she is trans. A former camper who continued volunteering after aging out of a youth group described falling into an abusive relationship after losing that community. The point is not that every hardship has one cause. The point is that the state keeps removing protective infrastructure while demanding that individuals survive the resulting exposure.

That is how a wicked problem is manufactured. Texas does not merely pass a law and produce one consequence. It creates a web: public stigma, reduced access to care, fear at school, weak local support, nonprofit scarcity and the constant threat that the remaining refuge may be forced to close. The problem has multiple actors and no single legislative switch will repair it. But the absence of a single switch does not mean the actors are invisible.

The elected officials who wrote and voted for these restrictions are visible. So are the institutions that preserve them. So are the adults who have decided that making trans children less secure is an acceptable price for demonstrating ideological loyalty.

The children are not the crisis. The crisis is a government that has learned to call exclusion protection.

The camp's mutual-aid structure exposes the lie more clearly than another official statement could. Yadi Martínez-Reyna, the non-binary minister who helped create Color Splash Out, began from an inclusive religious practice and built a secular space where children could play without being inspected for ideological compliance. The camp's houses borrow from ballroom culture and drag-ball traditions. Campers choose names and pronouns. Former campers return as fellows and mentors. Adults do not treat the young people as abstractions in a legislative argument; they treat them as people with skills, fear, humor, memory and a future.

That is not indulgence. It is public work performed where public institutions have failed.

The late Martin Luther King Jr. argued in *Where Do We Go From Here: Chaos or Community?* (1967) that charity could not substitute for restructuring the conditions that continually produced deprivation. Color Splash Out is the charity the state has made necessary—the reminder that the machinery has not been restructured. It is the warm hand placed over a wound while the machinery that made the wound keeps running.

And the machinery is not confined to Texas. Main Street Independent has already traced how [camp closures grow when “safety” rules become tools for exclusion](/articles/2026-05-18-texas-camp-closures-grow-after-new-safety-rules-fiber-reprieve-comes-too-late/). The same political logic appears whenever officials announce protection while narrowing the spaces in which vulnerable people can live freely. The rule is always presented as a shield. The cost is always paid by someone with less power to refuse it.

So the answer cannot be gratitude alone for the people keeping Color Splash Out alive, though gratitude is owed. The answer is to stop treating mutual aid as a substitute for rights. Restore the public institutions. Protect affirming spaces. Fund youth organizations in regions where isolation is the policy's predictable result. Remove officials from the machinery of exclusion through the democratic means available: organizing, voting, litigation, public pressure and sustained witness.

The children should not have to become brave enough to endure what adults have chosen to impose. They should not have to wait for a four-day camp to discover that their names can be spoken without apology, that water can be entered without fear, that community can be ordinary.

The long horizon is not a promise that the arc bends by itself. It bends when specific people, in a specific moment, put their bodies, money, time and authority against the machinery that holds it straight. Color Splash Out is doing that work with borrowed kayaks and donated books.

The rest of us can do it with the power we have.

By any means necessary that remain human, democratic and accountable: name the law, name the office, name the harm, protect the child, and refuse to let the state call abandonment safety.
