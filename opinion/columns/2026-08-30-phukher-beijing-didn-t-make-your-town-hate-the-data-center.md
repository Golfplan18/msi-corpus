The latest whisper campaign blaming Chinese bots for the wave of local opposition to data centers is the most expensive case of deflection American politics has produced in years. Not because the foreign-influence question isn't real — it is, and it should be investigated. But because the people reaching for that explanation first are using it to launder past a much simpler truth: their neighbors have real reasons to be unhappy, and those reasons aren't going away when the bot accounts get suspended.

Start with what a data center actually does. It is, at bottom, an industrial facility that is large, loud, requires substantial electricity and water, and produces heat, noise, and a constant thrum of diesel generators during testing. It drinks water by the million gallons. It pulls power that a small town cannot replace without rate hikes. It puts a single enormous industrial facility on land that the local fire department was never sized to handle. It rarely hires locally in proportion to the tax revenue it claims to bring. The "free lunch" line — large tax revenues with modest demands for services — is a brochure, not a balance sheet. Property-tax abatement deals routinely shift infrastructure costs back onto the ratepayers who were promised the abatement would never be needed.

When those ratepayers show up at a planning meeting, they are told the same thing in the same tone: you are backwards, you are NIMBY, you are standing in the way of progress, and — increasingly — you are probably a Chinese bot. That second move is the new one. It is also the dishonest one. The first move was bad enough; the second compounds it. The technique is what the [Bad-Faith Catalog](/propaganda/docs/bad-faith-techniques-catalogue) calls **pre-emptive legitimacy-withdrawal** — the opposition's standing to object is declared already forfeited, on grounds of who they are supposed to be, before any specific evidence of foreign agency has been produced — running in combination with **poisoning the well** in its ad-hominem form, where the people who will speak are preemptively discredited so nothing they say can be heard fairly. Both moves do the same work. They don't rebut the argument. They refuse to let the argument arrive.

The people who flipped the public conversation from "should we approve this site" to "are you a foreign agent" did not do it because they had evidence of a bot farm. They did it because they had run out of arguments on the merits and decided that the most efficient way to win a zoning fight was to delegitimize the opposition. I built versions of this. Not this exact version — I never labeled a planning commission a foreign op — but the playbook is the one I worked from. Find the demographic whose concerns are actually moving the room, relabel them as something the room is already primed to distrust, and let the label do the work the merits could not. Calling your neighbor a foreign operative because he doesn't want a 500-megawatt facility next to his son's elementary school is a tactic. It is not an analysis. We would be no better than Hillary Clinton declaring that all opposition to her 2016 campaign was Russian disinformation.

The evidence for the alleged Chinese bot campaign is thin in exactly the way that evidence is always thin when the conclusion has been pre-written. A handful of accounts. Some coordinated posting patterns. A venture capitalist — Bill Gurley, no friend of Beijing — asking an AI tool called Grok for a summary. An Axios reporter, Josephine Walker, writing up what looked like coordinated Chinese-language posts flagged by a SpaceX-affiliated account called Global Government Affairs. None of this proves that the mother who spoke against the new server farm for three hours at the board of supervisors meeting is a Beijing operative. None of it proves that the county commissioner who demanded an impact study is taking instructions from the Politburo. The people who insist otherwise are not reasoning. They are branding.

And they are choosing, at this moment, to make foreign influence the headline rather than the local impacts that are actually moving voters. That is a choice with consequences. It means the policy debate is happening about bot detection and content moderation instead of about water rights, transmission-line siting, and who pays when the backup generator fails. It means the people whose land is being condemned are being told their concerns are not their concerns — they are a foreign op. It means the next round of facilities will be approved faster, on worse terms, because the loudest objection is now a national-security story instead of a county-budget story.

Meanwhile, the serious reporting on what is actually happening — Lindsay Ellis and Amrith Ramkumar in the Wall Street Journal on the politics, Ko Lyn Cheang in the San Francisco Chronicle on the workforce dimension — keeps producing material that does not fit the foreign-agent frame. The Richmond Times-Dispatch editorialized on August 27 under the headline "The hidden risk of AI adoption: Fewer future experts," and the warning is real: the easier AI makes things look, the fewer young people may want to learn the underlying craft. Meanwhile, the immigration lawyers report a system that has become nearly incomprehensible, with wait times measured in decades for talented engineers from India and China who have job offers in hand and skills in acute shortage. The vetting challenge is real. The espionage threat is real. But the policy of essentially refusing to engage with this potential brain gain is hard to defend on either economic or national-security grounds.

There is a particular cruelty in the routine. The same Congress that delivers somber speeches about American AI competitiveness tightens the immigration screws on the very foreign engineers — many of them Chinese, many of them Indian — who could come here and build the things. The CCP, if it is running a bot campaign, has picked a smart target. Americans arguing with each other about whether to build something are not Americans building it. Every week of delay is a week in which the technology leadership of the free world becomes slightly more uncertain.

In the counties where the facilities actually go, the workers who build them are not the ones complaining. The electricians, the HVAC specialists, the concrete pourers, the fiber-optic splicers — these are the people whose careers are being transformed by the AI buildout. Rural counties in Texas, Virginia, Iowa and Arizona compete to host this infrastructure like college towns competing for a Chipotle. School budgets get funded. Property taxes get cut. Fire departments get new trucks. That is the part of the picture the deflection routine would rather you did not see. The people who stand to gain from a fast, well-done build are real. The people who stand to lose from a careless build are real. Neither side is a psyop.

If Beijing is running a bot farm, prosecute it. Suspend the accounts. Name the operators. Hand the file to the FBI and let them work. That part is easy and obvious. What is not easy and not obvious is letting "China did it" become the all-purpose answer for any local fight any developer wants to lose quietly. That is how you get a country that cannot build a substation without a senator, cannot site a transmission line without an FBI agent, and cannot run a planning meeting without an X post about foreign agents.

The local opposition to data centers is real. It is also, in many cases, right. The fastest way to make sure it never gets a fair hearing is to keep answering it with Beijing.
