*She was used, and then she was discarded when the moment was over.*

The Texas Republican Party used a captive endangered African elephant as a campaign prop. She is four tons of sentient creature, her species listed as endangered by the International Union for Conservation of Nature, and her name is Paige. She was brought into the George R. Brown Convention Center in Houston on Friday after Governor Greg Abbott finished his keynote, draped in a campaign banner, a “larger-than-life surprise.” She stopped mid-procession and urinated on the floor.

The video went wide. Someone shouted “Oh no.” Others laughed. The Texas Democratic Party posted the footage and called it a perfect metaphor. The Texas Republican Party posted their own video, calling Paige “a treat for our delegates and guests — one they won’t ever forget.”

The metaphor is the chain — literal and figurative — the ones the Animal Rights Coalition describes, and the invisible one that lets us treat a living creature as scenery. The metaphor is a sentient, four-ton creature whose parents were killed for ivory, rented out for $125-a-ticket “up close and personal” encounters at a facility in Cut and Shoot, Texas, and then trucked to a political convention to be a mascot for a party whose leaders have made it easier to shoot her kind.

Paige came from the East Texas Elephant Experience. The company says she and two other elephants were brought to the United States after their parents were poached for ivory in the late 1990s. The Animal Rights Coalition has a page titled “Send Jeanie, Krissy, and Paige to Sanctuary!” — they accuse the business of forcing the elephants to endure “long hours of confinement, often chained and transported in cramped trailers across the country.”

Former President Trump loosened restrictions on importing elephant trophies from African countries, a practice that, by the assessment of major conservation organizations, has significantly contributed to the species’ sharp decline. His sons, Donald Jr. and Eric, are prolific big-game hunters, photographed on safari in 2011 posing with animals they had killed, including an elephant. The party that wrapped a living elephant in a campaign banner is the same party that, in measurable policy terms, is making the world less safe for elephants. Paige was brought in to symbolize the Republican Party, and she did: she was used, and then she was discarded when the moment was over.

“The righteous care for the needs of their animals, but the kindest acts of the wicked are cruel.” That is Proverbs 12:10, and it was written for a moment exactly like this one. A party that claims the Christian tradition put a four-ton endangered animal — orphaned by poachers, shipped across the country in chains — on a convention floor so delegates could feel the thrill of their own symbol in the flesh.

Pope Francis wrote in *Laudato Si’* that “every act of cruelty towards any creature is contrary to human dignity” (LS 92). The Catechism of the Catholic Church says the same: “It is contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly” (CCC 2418). St. Francis called the animals brothers and sisters, not props. Across the Hebrew and Christian traditions, the consistent teaching is that animals are not ours to use however we please. They are not decoration for our political rallies. They are not props for a movement that spent last week celebrating a women’s summit organized around the proposition that faith authorizes a narrow vision of female submission — a movement that speaks the language of God’s order while treating God’s creatures as expendable scenery for campaign season.

The case of Happy, the Bronx Zoo elephant who was deemed “self-aware” by a court and euthanized last month at age 55, [made the same point from a different angle](/articles/2026-05-27-happy-self-aware-bronx-zoo-elephant-euthanized-at-age-55/): institutions that claim to care for elephants keep them in conditions that break them down, and we call it conservation until the animal dies.

The confession this column owes: I am not writing from innocence. I went to circuses as a child. I have lived in a culture that treats animals as entertainment, as spectacle, as things to be rented and displayed and forgotten. The difference between my childhood circus visit and what happened in Houston is not kind, only scale: I did not wrap the elephant in a banner bearing my party’s name. But the chain on Paige’s leg is the same chain we put on anything we decide is ours to use. The system that treats sentient creatures as products is not one party’s system. It is ours.

But there is something specific about parading an endangered animal through a political convention while your own policies endanger her kind. There is something specific about wrapping a creature in your banner when your own sons posed with the corpse of one like her. You draped her in your banner. Your own president made it easier to kill her kind for sport. And when she did the one thing in that building that was entirely her own — the one act that was not rented, not choreographed, not draped in someone else’s message — you called it a surprise.

Paige relieved herself on the convention floor because she is an elephant and that is what elephants do when they are stressed, confined, and far from home. The one thing she did in that building that belonged only to her was the thing you did not want. The laughter in the hall was nervous laughter: people knew they were watching something indecent. The video spread because somewhere in every person watching it was the recognition that this was wrong.

The door is open. You can stop. Send Paige to a sanctuary, where she can live out her life free from exploitation, as the Animal Rights Coalition has been begging for years. Admit that using living things as props is wrong. Look at the photographs of the president’s sons with the elephant they killed, and ask yourself what kind of party you are building. Choose to treat the creatures of God as creatures of God, not as party decorations.

Let the tradition you claim ask the question you have not answered: what did you think she was? The chain is still on the floor.
