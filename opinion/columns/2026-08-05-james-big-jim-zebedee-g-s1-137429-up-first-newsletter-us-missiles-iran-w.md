The Pentagon is burning through its missile stockpile in the war with Iran and leaving workers and communities to pay the price.

That is the plain meaning of the stockpile report. The Army has used most of its long-range attack missiles in the conflict. Precision weapons such as ATACMS and PrSMs are nearly depleted. Patriot interceptors, the principal American defense against drones and missiles in the Middle East, have fallen by two-thirds since the war began, according to a Center for Strategic and International Studies report.

That is not a procurement inconvenience. It is the bill for treating war as an operating tempo instead of a political decision.

Now, I'm just a simple man, but a parts counter teaches the difference between an expensive part and an available part. A nation can possess the world's finest weapons and still be strategically poor if it cannot replace what it fires. A shop owner who burns through replacement parts faster than he can order them does not call the shortage strength. He asks what job required the parts, whether the job was necessary, and who approved the work.

The same questions belong in the war room. But they also belong in the factory where the missiles are built, the town where the factory sits, and the budget hearing where the money is allocated.

A Patriot interceptor costs about $4 million. A long-range missile costs about the same. That money comes from the same federal budget that funds highways, schools, and local services. Every dollar spent on a missile is a dollar not spent on a town where the main employer is a defense plant. Those missiles are built by machinists and welders and assembly-line workers in places like Huntsville and Tucson and Newport News. They work overtime when the demand spikes. They get laid off when the war slows down. The war economy is a rollercoaster. It depends on the next conflict, and the next conflict depends on the next depletion. The community near the base does not get a vote on whether the base stays open, whether the workers get a raise, or whether the tax dollars go to roads instead of rockets.

A contractor can book revenue from a replacement contract. A soldier cannot book a replacement missile when an incoming weapon is already in the air. A worker in a defense plant does not get to decide whether the next missile is built for the front line or for the shelf. The boss who makes the decision already knows which way the money flows.

The same logic that burns through missiles also burns through people. The defense industry consolidates around a few large firms that control production. When the demand spikes, those firms get the contracts and the workers get the overtime. When the demand drops, the workers get the pink slips and the firms keep the backlog. The workers who build the missiles are not executives. They are machinists and welders and assembly-line workers who live in the same communities as the people who come into the shop for a brake job and find out the parts are on back order. They depend on the contract. The contract depends on the war. And the war depends on a stockpile that is almost empty.

The same administrative culture that has proposed nondisclosure agreements for current and future federal employees now asks the public to accept depletion as a technical detail. But the missile count is not a classified abstraction when the shortage changes what the country can defend, whom it can protect, and which future conflict it can afford to enter. Congressional oversight is more urgent, not less. The country needs to know how its weapons were spent, what they accomplished, and what risks their absence creates. But the people who live near the defense plants, who depend on the contracts, who send their kids to the schools that are losing funding to the war — they need to know too. They need to know whether their town is being sacrificed to a war that has no defined objective, no defined exit, and no defined source of replenishment.

In his farewell address of January 17, 1961, Eisenhower warned the country to guard against "the acquisition of unwarranted influence" by the military-industrial complex and the "disastrous rise of misplaced power." He was not warning only about crude corruption. He was warning about a permanent military establishment and arms industry acquiring its own momentum inside public life. Sixty-five years later, a missile shortage is one receipt for that warning. The defense worker in Huntsville who builds the Patriot interceptors, the family near the base who depends on the factory for a paycheck, the town where the roads are crumbling because the tax dollars are going to rockets instead of schools — they are the ones who pay for that warning's neglect.

The shortage should be treated as evidence, not merely as an acquisition problem. The United States needs a durable industrial base for genuine defense. It does not need to confuse an industrial base with an obligation to keep every war supplied indefinitely. A factory can be necessary without every campaign being wise. A weapon can protect a soldier in one operation while helping create a strategic vulnerability in the next.

The constitutional posture is plain. Congress should authorize this war with a defined objective, a defined duration, a defined cost, a defined exit condition, and a defined source of replenishment. If Congress will not do that, it should end the war. The executive branch does not receive a permanent credit line because the Pentagon can describe the next salvo as an immediate need. And the people who live near the defense plants, who depend on the contracts, who send their kids to the schools that are losing funding — they do not get to keep their jobs while the war burns through the stockpile.

The Army is not running out of missiles in the abstract. It is spending them in a war. The future service members and allies who may need them will inherit the shortage. The worker who builds the missile is the one who pays the price.
