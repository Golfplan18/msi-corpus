Donald Trump and the 65-member "Sharia-Free America" caucus are bearing false witness against their Muslim neighbors, and the Westwood Islamic Community Center is paying the price.

In the early hours of August 3, someone cut the wire fence around the Westwood Islamic Community Center in Cincinnati, broke a window, entered the office, stole surveillance cameras and television monitors, and wrote "die" along with other profanities on a whiteboard. Two posters bearing Quranic verses in Arabic were smashed through. Glass covered the floor. The center is a mosque and a food pantry in an underserved, majority-Black neighborhood in west Cincinnati. It has been broken into three times in seven years. The previous incident left racist graffiti on the walls.

No suspect has been apprehended. Board member Nashid Shakir, who has belonged to the center since the 1960s, put the matter in plain English: "The police came out and they checked the boxes for making a report. But we haven't had any follow-through."

The attack came during the same week as President Trump's Oval Office remark to reporters on August 10: "We have jihadists being elected all over the place." The target was Abdul El-Sayed, the Michigan epidemiologist and Democratic Senate candidate who had just won his primary election on August 4. The Center for the Study of Organized Hate documented a twentyfold increase in attacks on El-Sayed since the election. CSOH executive director Raqib Naik described "a very organized and coordinated campaign to generate fear and hysteria about Muslims for electoral gain."

The Oval Office speech and the wire cutters in Cincinnati are not the same act. They are the same operation at different scales.

The Sharia-Free America caucus was formed last December by Texas Representatives Keith Self and Chip Roy. It claims 65 members from 17 states. Its stated premise is that Islamic religious practice in America presents a civilizational threat requiring congressional organization against it. That question is not paranoia. It is a judgment formed by the record. In December 2005, two pipe bombs exploded outside Cincinnati's Clifton mosque, the city's largest, two hours after worshippers gathered to pray. The FBI investigated. No one has ever been charged. Twenty years on, that is the federal record of protection for a bombed house of worship in this city.
