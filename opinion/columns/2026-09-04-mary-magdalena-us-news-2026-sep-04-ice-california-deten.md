The California City immigration detention center sits in a desert region north of Los Angeles. It opened in August 2025, run by the private prison firm CoreCivic under contract with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. It holds roughly 1,600 people — the largest ICE facility in California, and one of the largest in the country. Since the day it opened, every person inside has been denied contact visits. No exceptions for birthdays. No exceptions for a pregnant wife. No exceptions for a five-year-old daughter.

Of the ten largest ICE detention centers in the country, four prohibit contact visits entirely: California City, the Stewart detention center in Georgia, the South Texas processing center in Pearsall, and the Montgomery processing center in Conroe, Texas.

Families travel up to three hundred miles to the remote site, only to find their relatives seated on one side of a thick pane of glass, speaking through telephones. One person can speak through the phone at a time. The other side waits. The cost of seeing your own family is twenty-one cents a minute. Six dollars and thirty cents buys a half-hour video call.

The California Department of Justice audited the facility this spring and photographed the visitation room. It contains picnic tables — the same tables in use when the facility was a state prison, when families sat at them, when fathers held their children on their laps. Those tables are now roped off. ICE detainees are not allowed to enter that part of the room. A row of windows at the edge is what they get.

The state audit found that the prohibition appeared to violate CoreCivic's own corporate policy — the policy the company itself provided to the state. That policy stipulated that physical contact during visits should only be restricted for individuals classified as "security risks." No such classification appears to apply to the general population of California City.

Michelle Brané, who served as immigration detention ombudsman for the Department of Homeland Security, said contact visits were standard practice in ICE facilities prior to 2016. They were normal. They were humane. Beginning in Trump's first term, more detention centers shifted to glass-only visits, Brané said, and the change has persisted since.

CoreCivic spokesperson Ryan Gustin defended the policy in writing: "Facility leadership, in conjunction with our government partners, has determined that non-contact visitation is the most appropriate approach given security considerations." He cited the prevention of contraband as the primary concern.

Warden Christopher Chestnut told Rev. Deborah Lee of the Interfaith Movement for Human Integrity, when she visited in July and asked why no one inside could have a regular visit, that facilities with contact visits had higher overdose rates. He told a minister this. He told her without a document, without a study, without a number. He said it to a woman who had driven to your facility to ask you to let mothers hold their children. As if the cure for addiction were to deny a five-year-old a hug with her father. As if a pregnant wife should be separated from her husband because somewhere, in another facility, somebody overdosed.

In April, Gustavo Guevara, who had been detained inside your facility since it opened, sat down with the men in his dorm and helped write a demand for contact visits. They were afraid, too — afraid of being deported without ever getting to say goodbye in person. Guevara worked with the California Collaborative for Immigrant Justice and a coalition of advocates to deliver those letters to your office — and to launch the "Hugs Beyond Bars" campaign.
