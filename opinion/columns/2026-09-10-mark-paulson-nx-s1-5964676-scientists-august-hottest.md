The fossil fuel industry is baking the planet to death, and August was the receipt.

Last month the planet averaged 62.53 degrees Fahrenheit — 16.96 Celsius — which Copernicus, the European climate service whose record goes back to 1940, called a tie with July 2023 for the hottest month they have on file. The margin between the two is a hundredth of a degree. That is also the margin of uncertainty. That is how you arrive at "tied." Eight of the eight hottest months on record have come since July 2023. August ran 1.65 degrees Celsius above the pre-industrial baseline — well past the 1.5 line the Paris Agreement drew in 2015 as the ceiling for civilization to function as designed. The 1.5 line we agreed to in Paris is not a goal we are missing. It is a line we have already crossed, on average, every month for nearly three years. The oceans tied their all-time monthly record in August, matching the March 2024 mark [Copernicus reported earlier in the month](/articles/2026-08-24-copernicus-reports-oceans-hit-hottest-temperatures-on-record/). Within that same month, the 22nd set the daily record — the hottest day ever measured in the world's seas. We are not setting records and breaking them. We are setting records and tying them and then breaking them again the same month.

NOAA announced last week that the Lower 48 just lived through its hottest summer on a record that goes back to 1895. Not by a tenth. Not by a hundredth. By almost four-tenths of a degree Fahrenheit over the summer of 1936 — the Dust Bowl peak, the one our grandparents lived through, the one we still measure summers against. The summer average ran 74.37 degrees across June, July, and August. August alone ran 75.58, with daytime highs averaging a blistering 88.56. That was the summer of the dust. This was the summer of the heat. Both go on the same shelf.

This is the moment the abstract becomes operational. The El Niño spike behind this August adds a tenth or two tenths of a degree — real, but not the story. The story is the relentless fossil-burning underneath, which is now compounded by an El Niño already forecast to break strength records in the months ahead. Zeke Hausfather at Berkeley Earth put the next line plain: 2027 will likely break 1.8 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial, on top of an El Niño that has not finished. Some days in the year ahead will cross 2. The 1.5 line is not a target anymore. It has been a footnote for fourteen months running.

The Adams County Times-Reporter ran a piece in the second week of August about the well on County Trunk G dropping two feet in three weeks. The co-op sent a note in the August bill reminding customers that demand hit the highest August peak in their history. The dairy operations on the south side of the county have been running their barn fans all night. That doesn't happen here in August. That happens in July. August was supposed to be the cool month. The cool month had fans running all night on the dairy farms and a well dropping two feet and the maple trees on the south side of my property starting to turn color three weeks before they are supposed to.

I keep a notebook. I have kept it for twelve years running — the ice-out on Lake Petenwell, the date the first mosquitoes show up, the week the maples run, the first night the barn door stays open because even at ten the air is warm. August is supposed to be the month the notebook goes quiet. This August the notebook did not slow down. The first maples on the property showed color in the middle of August. I have never recorded that before. I wrote it down.

Andrew Dessler, climate scientist at Texas A&M, said it plain in the email the wire services ran. "It is now time to stop acting surprised that our warming planet keeps getting warmer. This is occurring exactly as forecast by scientists literally decades ago. The interesting question is not whether we will keep breaking heat records (we will), but whether we're going to do anything about it or just stand there while the climate train runs us over."

The climate train has been running for decades. August just made the schedule public. What's not tied is the trajectory. What's not in dispute is which way the capital is going to flow because of it. Three shifts are already in motion.

Insurance and real estate are repricing, fast. When daytime highs average 88.56 in August and roads start to buckle, the actuarial tables get rewritten. Properties across the Sun Belt are seeing insurance markets either withdraw or reprice into territory that makes ownership uneconomic. The capital is voting, and it is voting out of places the climate is no longer underwriting.

Cooling is now infrastructure, not a luxury. The cities that figure out district cooling, grid-scale storage to handle air-conditioning load, and building codes that don't cook their occupants are about to win a multi-decade competitive advantage. Gulf-state neighbors are bidding against each other for the same engineering talent. The next decade's winners are being chosen now, in procurement offices nobody is watching.

Agricultural geography is redrawing in real time. Wheat belts move. Citrus belts move. The places that can still grow food at scale are becoming strategically valuable in a way that has not been true since the original green revolution. Land prices in the new viable zones are already moving; the agribusiness consolidation that follows is already underway.

The temperature record isn't a story about the atmosphere. It is a story about capital flows, supply chains, and which institutions are fast enough to rewrite their models before the next hundredth of a degree lands.

The fossil fuel industry has known what they are sitting on for forty years. ExxonMobil's own scientists were modeling the warming inside the company in the late seventies and early eighties. Their executives were going to spend the next three decades denying what their own people had written down. That playbook — hire the doubt, fund the think tanks, build the architecture of not-knowing so that nobody has to act on what is known — ran first for the cigarette companies, and then for the climate. Same architects. Same outcome.

The administration in power pulled us out of the Paris agreement in January, for the second time in a decade. The previous administration approved the Willow Project and ran LNG export at record pace. Neither administration is going to be remembered for what they did about August.

The essayist I keep going back to is Wendell Berry, who has spent fifty years writing about what happens when the consolidated operators come into a place and don't know what they are sitting on. Berry's frame is that a community's economy is its membership, and that the operators who strip the membership out for the extractable value have stolen from every generation that comes after. The corollary he has been writing toward for fifty years is the harder one: you can know what you are losing and still not love it enough to act.

Simon Stiell, the UN climate chief, said the only thing there is left to say plainly. "Until humanity stops burning colossal amount of coal, oil and gas, the pollution this causes will keep baking our planet, and extreme heat will keep smashing all-time records, killing millions and costing trillions, hitting transport and health systems, and pushing up prices for household basics like food."

Killing. Not warming. Killing.

The line from 1936 to 2026 is straight enough to draw with a square. When the market said rural electricity wasn't worth the wire, the federal government and the cooperatives ran the wire anyway, and the wire is still there. Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative is in this county because someone decided the market was wrong about what rural places were worth electrifying in 1936. The argument against running the next wire — the one that would put a heat pump in every farmhouse on the central sands and a community battery on every co-op substation — is the same argument the IOUs made about rural electrification ninety years ago. We have done this before. We can do it again. We have not yet decided to.

Aldo Leopold, who hunted and wrote in the same sand country I hunt and write in, said conservation is a state of harmony between men and the land. He was writing before the rural electric cooperatives existed. He would have known what the cooperatives were doing. The notebook I keep is the notebook of a man watching the harm come in. The shop is where I write the column. The column is the way I try to be useful. None of it is enough.

I have watched the ice go out later for twelve winters. I have watched the mosquitoes come earlier for twelve summers. I have watched the maples start to turn in the middle of August and written it down in the notebook and thought: that is new. That is new because the planet is not doing something different. It is new because we have been doing the same thing to the planet for forty years, and the planet is finally not pretending.

The county may still have a county in twenty years. We will see.
