James Freeman turns Michigan policy disagreement into bigotry.

That is the operation in his August 10 “Best of the Web” column in the *Wall Street Journal* opinion pages. I built versions of this frame-loading trick in the cable years. Freeman targets Abdel El-Sayed’s Michigan gubernatorial candidacy through five connected moves: label the candidate an extremist, label his possible defender a coward, reduce a prior political appeal to a scolding, convert policy disagreement into moral contamination, then attach the candidate to a hated outside figure. The surveillance item and the experts item in the roundup — including Ethan Baron’s San Jose Mercury News report and Rachael Robertson’s MedPage Today question about what we would do without experts — are the daily housekeeping. They make a one-target attack look like innocent news collection.

The operation lives here.

> In a perfect world, the elder statesmen of the Democratic Party would help discourage voters from embracing the reckless extremism of the hard left. But the former community organizer who is the party’s most influential politician seems to have decided for now to signal support—but not too much support.
>
> *— Opening paragraph*

“Reckless extremism of the hard left” — frame-engineered relabeling, WSJ §A.1 — opens the piece by assigning the verdict before the reader encounters a policy. The operation is simple: substitute a term carrying a different emotional charge, then let the new term do the thinking. A Michigan gubernatorial candidate becomes “the hard left” before he becomes a person with a health-care plan, an infrastructure proposal or a record that can be examined.

Operators call this loading the dock. You set the frame before the cargo arrives.

The phrase “elder statesmen” does complementary work. It places the Democratic Party’s reasonable center on the page as the natural home of seriousness, with El-Sayed already outside the fence. Freeman is running a con in the first sentence. The reader absorbs “extremism” and never goes back to ask whether the candidate earned it.

This is not a disagreement about a program. It is the relabel scam.

> For now it seems Mr. Obama is executing the same self-serving straddle he did with New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani—offering encouragement but not an explicit endorsement, so that Mr. Obama still has the option to avoid responsibility in the event of political catastrophe.

“Self-serving straddle” — frame-engineered relabeling again, with attribution of motive smuggled inside the label. Freeman gives the reader no evidence that Barack Obama’s position is self-serving, no account of what the calculation might involve, and no distinction between caution, coalition management and cowardice. The word “straddle” supplies the conclusion. It makes judgment look like fear.

This is the coward’s ledger Freeman writes for Obama: encouragement on one side, responsibility on the other, with every act of political care entered as evidence of personal calculation. The phrase is designed to survive verification because it does not quite assert a fact. It supplies a character verdict and calls the verdict analysis.

The documented scene, as described in the draft of the attack, is less convenient. Obama opened by acknowledging the grievance of Black men who had been taken for granted by a party that surfaced every two years and disappeared afterward. He argued from the record of what both White House tenures had delivered for Black communities before making the plea. The line about Black men not getting past the idea of a woman as president was one moment inside that appeal. It was not its “gist.”

That distinction matters. A full appeal can contain a bad line, a clumsy line or a line that lands badly. A strawman turns the line into the whole appeal so the author can condemn the speaker without engaging the appeal that actually occurred.

“Calling for police defunding” and “bloody policy” — strawman of progressive positions, WSJ §A.6 — followed by pre-emptive legitimacy withdrawal. Freeman characterizes El-Sayed’s position in its most alarming available form, then treats any later clarification as proof of deceit. The candidate is not permitted to have changed, clarified or been misunderstood. Every exit is sealed in advance.

The true half must be conceded. Michigan did experience a violent-crime spike in 2020 and 2021. Public safety is a legitimate concern. Covid restrictions imposed real costs on personal liberty, and voters had reasons to assess them. That is the documentary starting point.

The operation begins when a call to defund the police and reinvest in communities is compressed into “bloody policy,” and when public-health restrictions imposed during a deadly pandemic are converted into a “grudge against liberty.” The chosen labels do not describe the dispute. They decide which moral universe the dispute will inhabit.

The “some sort of bigotry” line performs a second job. Freeman tells readers in advance that any defense of El-Sayed will be an accusation against them. Voters who disagree receive confirmation. Voters who might defend him are told that their defense is the same shaming that failed in Pennsylvania.

This is the all-exits-sealed operation. The candidate is extreme if he stands by his position, dishonest if he modifies it, and morally dangerous if anyone explains it. That is not scrutiny. It is a shell game with the policy removed.
