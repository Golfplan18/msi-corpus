Trump and Vance robbed twenty million children to fund a billionaire tax cut and called it pro-family.

The architecture is on the page. Public Law 119-21, signed on the Fourth of July 2025, raises the Child Tax Credit's headline number to $2,200 per child and indexes it for inflation. The refundable portion stays capped at $1,700. That gap is where the robbery happens. A married couple earning $400,000 — the new phaseout ceiling — gets the full bump: an extra $1,000 for two kids. A single mother of two earning $24,000 gets nothing. Her income tax liability is already zero. The non-refundable portion is a credit against a tax she does not owe. The refundable portion, capped at $1,700 per child, is what families like hers actually receive, and that cap was not raised. The headline number went up. Her credit did not. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities counted twenty million children whose parents do not earn enough to receive the full increase. Twenty million children for whom the bill that calls itself pro-family is, in operation, no bill at all.

The Congressional Budget Office published the distributional analysis in August 2025. The top decile of households takes an average of $13,600 per year. The bottom decile loses an average of $1,200 per year, a 3.1 percent reduction in household resources. Those numbers are the bill. That is the actual policy. Everything else — the press conference at the South Lawn, the Vance speeches about ordinary families, the floor speeches by Mike Crapo and John Thune on the Senate side and Jason Smith and Mike Johnson on the House side — is the marketing.

The SSN architecture is where the robbery acquires a specific demographic shape. The qualifying child must have a Social Security number. That was already the rule under the 2017 tax law and was due to sunset; this bill makes it permanent. The new piece is the requirement that the parent claiming the credit — or, on a joint return, at least one spouse — must also have an SSN. The Tax Policy Center estimates 500,000 children, overwhelmingly U.S. citizens, knocked out of the credit directly because no parent on the return holds the right document. The Center for Migration Studies of New York estimates a further 4.5 million children at risk in mixed-status households where one or both parents file with an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number rather than an SSN — about 910,000 of those children in California, 247,000 in Florida, 226,000 in New York. They are citizens. They are children. The bill, in operation, will look at their families' tax returns, find an ITIN where the new statute demands an SSN, and exclude them from the so-called pro-family credit. The SSN-extended-to-parents architecture is not a fraud-prevention measure. There is no fraud being prevented; the ITIN exists because the IRS requires people without SSNs who owe federal taxes to file federal taxes, and those people have been filing and paying for thirty years. The architecture is a deportation tax. It is a way of using the family-policy code to make the families of the wrong immigrants poorer than the families of the right ones.

There is a counterfactual on the record, and the counterfactual is recent. In 2021, the American Rescue Plan expanded the Child Tax Credit to $3,600 per child under six and $3,000 per child six to seventeen, made it fully refundable so that the lowest-income families received the full credit, and paid it out in monthly installments rather than as a lump-sum tax-time benefit. According to the Columbia University Center on Poverty and Social Policy, the child poverty rate fell to a historic low of 5.2 percent that year. The benefit kept 3.7 million children out of poverty by December 2021. Congress allowed it to expire on schedule at the end of 2021 — a decision that involved Senator Joe Manchin and a Democratic Senate that did not have the votes, and a Republican caucus uninterested in the extension on any terms. By 2022, the child poverty rate had more than doubled to 12.4 percent. Columbia's counterfactual analysis projects that, had the expansion stayed in force, the 2022 rate would have been 8.1 percent — roughly five million additional children kept out of poverty. The bill that Trump and Vance signed in July 2025, branded "One Big Beautiful Bill" and marketed as the most important pro-family legislation in decades, replaces a credit architecture that demonstrably halved child poverty with a credit architecture that excludes twenty million low-income children, five hundred thousand citizen children of immigrant parents, and 4.5 million children in mixed-status households from the help the bill purports to deliver. The architecture is the choice. The choice is to redirect the money to the top decile and call it Christmas.

The Romney Family Security Act 2.0 is also on the record. A 2022 proposal by Senators Mitt Romney, Richard Burr, and Steve Daines that would have provided $350 a month per child under six and $250 a month per child six to seventeen, with simplification of the EITC and consolidation of overlapping family-tax benefits. The Niskanen Center analysis projected a 12.6 percent reduction in child poverty. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities raised real concerns about the EITC offsets — somewhere around ten million children would have been worse off because of how the bill paid for itself — but the architecture demonstrated that a Republican-authored child allowance was structurally possible. The proposal went nowhere. Senate Republican leadership did not move it. The current bill's authors — Crapo, Thune, Daines (who switched sides) — are the people who did not move it. There was a version of pro-family Republican policy on the table three years ago. The version that became law in 2025 chose distributional regressivity instead.

Anne Helen Petersen documented, in *Can't Even*, the millennial generation's full conceptualization of itself as "walking college resumes" — burnout as the structural condition produced by labor-market deregulation, the financialization of the household, and the collapse of the good job. The mothers of children under twelve are reading the 2025 bill from inside that condition. The bill assumes its targets are already inside the human-capital frame: that the response to a credit you don't qualify for will be to earn more, that the response to a cap that was not raised will be to climb the income distribution into the bracket where the credit applies, that the response to an SSN requirement that excludes your spouse will be to acquire a different spouse. The framing is not new. Petersen's diagnostic is the substrate against which the actual numbers in the actual statute have to be read. The bill is not solving a problem millennial mothers experience. The bill is converting the problem into more of the problem.

Annie Lowrey, in *Give People Money* in 2018, wrote that poverty in the United States is a choice — that the policy instruments to end it exist and have been used elsewhere — and that the cash transfer is the cleanest, least-paternalistic instrument available. The United States, Lowrey notes, is one of only two countries on earth without guaranteed paid parental leave; the other is Papua New Guinea. U.S. public childcare spending sits at roughly one-quarter of the OECD average. The 2021 ARP expansion was the closest the United States has come, in recent decades, to the cash-transfer instrument Lowrey describes. The 2025 bill is the reversal of that instrument and the reassertion of the choice.

Leo XIII wrote, in *Rerum Novarum* in 1891, that "wages ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner" — the family wage as a doctrinal floor, the worker's pay required to support not only himself but his household. The encyclical is 134 years old. It is the foundational document of modern Catholic Social Teaching. Its definition of pro-family policy is the inverse of the architecture of P.L. 119-21. A bill that takes resources from the bottom decile and transfers them to the top decile, and that excludes the lowest-income twenty million children from a credit it claims to expand, is not a pro-family bill under any moral tradition the named perpetrators claim to be operating inside.

Francis, in *Fratelli Tutti* in 2020, wrote that "some people are born into economically stable families, receive a fine education, grow up well nourished, or naturally possess great talent. They will certainly not need a proactive state; they need only claim their freedom. Yet the same rule clearly does not apply to a disabled person, to someone born in dire poverty, to those lacking a good education and with little access to adequate health care. If a society is governed primarily by the criteria of market freedom and efficiency, there is no place for such persons, and fraternity will remain just another vague ideal." Francis wrote that paragraph against the architecture of P.L. 119-21 five years before P.L. 119-21 existed. The architecture's authors profess Catholicism. The architecture's beneficiaries are not the persons in Francis's second sentence. The architecture's victims are.

What our parents could afford on a USPS supervisor's wage and a part-time Catholic-school nurse's wage — three kids through Catholic school, a paid-off rowhouse, a Wildwood beach week every August — is not what we mothers of kids under twelve can afford on two professional incomes. That gap is documentary. The childcare bill that runs $2,400 against a combined post-tax income of $8,800; the mortgage at 7 percent that has not refinanced; the student-loan balances from public Pennsylvania universities our parents could not have imagined attending without our debt — these are the conditions inside which the kitchen-table math happens. The class position our parents handed us was the white-middle-class one, with grandparents' estates that contributed to down payments and college-educated parents who navigated financial-aid paperwork on our behalf; that anchor matters because the gap we name is the gap between an inheritance our class produced and an income our class can no longer secure. The mothers below us — single, immigrant, working-class, Black, Latina, mixed-status — face the same structural conditions multiplied by exclusions our class does not. The Child Tax Credit architecture in P.L. 119-21 is the bill written against them more than against us. The class position obliges naming.

The bill the authors should have written exists in three places on the public record. It exists in the 2021 American Rescue Plan, which halved child poverty before Congress allowed it to lapse. It exists in the Romney-Burr-Daines proposal of 2022, which the current bill's authors declined to advance. It exists in the créche, école maternelle, paid parental leave, and subsidized childcare that Pamela Druckerman documented as the operating infrastructure of pro-family states elsewhere. The bill Trump and Vance signed on July 4, 2025, was a choice against each of those alternatives. The bill chose the top decile. The bill chose deportation-tax architecture. The bill chose the cap that excluded the lowest-income twenty million children. The bill chose the headline number that does not reach the mothers who needed it.

A single mother of two in Philadelphia, earning $24,000, opens the IRS instructions for tax year 2025 and finds that the new $2,200 per-child credit, the one whose passage was a Rose Garden ceremony, is, in her case, zero additional dollars. That is the bill. That is the pro-family policy. That is what was robbed and what was kept.
