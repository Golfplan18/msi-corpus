Secretary Rubio is detaining a professor, her husband, and their teenage son in Texas for what a woman said on television in 1979 — before the son was born.

Maryam Tahmasebi is a professor of psychology and statistics in Los Angeles. Her husband, Seyed Eissa Hashemi, works alongside her in American academia. Their teenage son is an American teenager. They entered the United States legally in 2014, became lawful permanent residents in 2016, and have spent the twelve years since building the life the green card was supposed to represent — a life defined, in Tahmasebi's words, by "my own actions, not anyone else's."

That life is in pieces. Since then, the family has been held in immigration detention: Tahmasebi and her son at the South Texas Family Residential Center in Dilley, Hashemi at the South Texas Detention Facility in Pearsall, in conditions his wife describes as "horrible." A federal judge has temporarily blocked their deportation. Their lawyers have asked the government to release them, or to charge them, or to let them leave voluntarily. The government has refused all three. When the family tried to self-deport, DHS declined their request for voluntary departure. A government attorney offered instead a stipulated removal — waive your hearing, leave the country, lose your rights. The family said they wanted to leave on their own terms. The government wants them detained without end.

The State Department will not say what crime the family has committed. It will not say how a 1979 television appearance by Masoumeh Ebtekar — a college-age spokesperson for the students who took over the American embassy in Tehran, and whose son was not yet born — threatens the national security of the United States in 2026. The agency, in the words of spokesman Tommy Pigott, "does not divulge protected, sensitive, or classified information pertaining to the termination of legal status of foreign nationals." The family is being punished for what their mother-in-law once said on television. The State Department will not say why.

The pattern is not limited to them. The State Department has also revoked the legal status of two Iranian women initially identified as the niece and grandniece of Qasem Soleimani — a relationship his own family has denied, and subsequent reporting has cast doubt upon. It has revoked the status of the daughter of former Secretary of the Supreme National Security Council of Iran Ali Larijani. The targeting appears to begin with the surname.

Tahmasebi wrote her account from inside the detention facility in Dilley, Texas. She called it what it is: bloodline punishment.

"We have been put through hell for the past four months," she wrote, "and our physical and mental health have deteriorated in ways that might not be reversible for years."

The State Department hides behind classified information to justify revoking the legal status of a professor and a teenager. The Department of Homeland Security tells them to download an app.
