ICE Arrested a Father at His Green Card Interview and Took Him From His Children.

Connor Read came to America when he was nine years old. He attended American schools. He married an American woman. He has three-year-old twin boys, Ryder and Ozzie, who know no country other than this one. On June 3, he arrived at the federal immigration building in Tampa for his green card interview — the legal process by which a person who has built a life here asks the country to recognize that life — and four ICE agents were waiting inside the interview room. They arrested him. Immigration officials told his wife he would not be coming home.

He has been in detention for nearly two months, held in the same ICE system where [families have described conditions they call inhumane](/articles/2026-06-06-families-describe-distress-over-inhumane-conditions-at-new-jersey-ice-jail/) and [nearly forty detained women joined a hunger strike](/articles/2026-06-12-nearly-40-women-join-hunger-strike-at-new-jersey-ice-detention-facility/) to protest their treatment. He spent his first eight days in a shared cell in a Pinellas County jail before being flown to a Louisiana ICE facility — hundreds of miles from his wife and twins, their one point of contact sliding further away. He is appealing his final removal order and has been told the decision may take six months.

The government calls Connor Read a "criminal illegal alien." His criminal record consists of possession of narcotic equipment, possession of liquor, and resisting an officer — the kind of charges that fill county court dockets every Friday night in every American city. Millions of citizens with identical records go to work on Monday morning. They tuck their children in at night. No one calls them aliens.

That label has a history. Mae Ngai, in her study of how American law manufactured the category, documented a figure the system constructs as someone who cannot fully be and a problem that cannot be solved. Connor Read was nine when his parents brought him here. He did not choose to cross a border. He did not choose to overstay a visa. He was a child, and the system that now threatens to deport him to a country he has visited once in nineteen years was designed to produce exactly this outcome: a human being reduced to a line item, a father transformed into a case number, a family treated as an externality.

The Torah commands, thirty-six times by the rabbinic count, love of the stranger. "You shall not oppress a stranger, for you know the heart of a stranger, having yourselves been strangers in the land of Egypt" (Exodus 23:9). The commandment does not add "unless the stranger's parents overstayed a student visa." It does not add "unless the stranger was convicted of possessing drug paraphernalia at twenty-two." It says you know the heart. The heart of a man who has built a life among you and now faces expulsion from everything that life contains.

Pope Francis at Lampedusa named what happens when a society loses the capacity to see that heart. "We have fallen into a globalization of indifference," he said. "We have become used to the suffering of others; it doesn't concern us, it doesn't interest us, it's none of our business!" He was speaking about bodies pulled from the Mediterranean. He could have been speaking about a father in a Louisiana detention cell whose three-year-old sons do not understand why he did not come home.

The ICE spokesperson said the administration is "targeting the worst of the worst." This is the language of war applied to a man whose greatest offense was possessing drug equipment. The phrase does work that has nothing to do with Connor Read and everything to do with manufacturing the consent of Americans who want to believe the deportation machinery is pointed only at dangerous men. It is not. It never has been. Adam Goodman documented this: the deportation machine did not begin with this administration. It was built across decades, fed by both parties, and its mechanism has always been the same — coercive administrative procedures backed by calculated fear. Nine of ten people deported are Mexican, but the machine does not discriminate by nationality when it has a body in its grip. It will take a British father of twin American boys just as readily.

The Supreme Court in *Plyler v. Doe* drew "a moral distinction between the unauthorized parents who chose to enter the United States and their unauthorized children who bear a status over which they can have little control." That moral distinction is what stands between every child brought to this country and the full weight of a system that would hold them accountable for a decision they never made. Connor Read was nine. The decision was not his. He was granted DACA protection as a teenager — the system itself acknowledging this was a person who should not bear the punishment for his parents' paperwork. Then he committed the ordinary failings of a young man — a paraphernalia charge, a liquor violation, a resisting arrest — and the same system that had extended its hand withdrew it. The door that had been open became a wall.

This is what the machine does. It extends one hand and hides the other behind its back. It invites you to the interview and stations agents inside the room. It calls you a "criminal alien" for offenses it would call youthful mistakes if committed by anyone it had already decided to see as human. It tells your wife you are not coming home. It holds you for months while your children learn, day by day, what it means to have a father who was present and then was not, and no one can explain to a three-year-old why.

Jesus said, in the passage Matthew records at the close of all things: "I was a stranger and you did not welcome me" (Matthew 25:43). He did not qualify it. He did not say "unless the stranger had a drug charge at twenty-two." He said the failure to welcome the stranger is the failure to welcome him. The judgment falls not on the stranger's record but on the host's heart.

The climate Trump is exploiting is one our own communities helped to build. Every administration since the Hart-Celler Act of 1965 has contributed to a system that produces millions of people living in twilight status — their presence simultaneously required by the economy and refused by the law. We who have lived comfortably within that system, who have eaten the food harvested by its labor and slept under roofs framed by its hands, have tolerated the machinery because it operated out of sight. Now it operates in plain view, in the interview room, and we are being asked to look.

Connor Read is appealing his final removal order. His sons will be nearly four by the time a decision comes — old enough to have learned, in whatever way a toddler learns, that their father left and did not come back, and no one could tell them why.

Óscar Romero stood before the soldiers of El Salvador and said: "In the name of God, in the name of this suffering people whose laments cry out to heaven, I implore you, I beg you, I command you: stop the repression." He was speaking to men with rifles. I am speaking to men with paperwork. The weapon is different. The wound is the same. A father is taken from his children, and the system calls it the rule of law, and the children do not understand why the rule of law requires their father to disappear.

The door of return is open. It is always open. Connor Read can come home. The question is whether we — who have inherited both the Constitution and the Sermon on the Mount and have not yet figured out how to hold both — will let him.
