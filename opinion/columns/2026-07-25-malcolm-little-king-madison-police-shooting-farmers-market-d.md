The farmers' market on the Square is not the baseline. The body is the baseline.

This is the fact Madison's white majority will not name. The wire copy from Madison this week opens with the disruption. The city's summertime events — the farmers' market, the concerts, the festivals that make Madison Madison in July — have been canceled. The killing of Corey Ruiz disrupted them. The disruption is the news.

The facts in the standard account are not wrong. Ruiz died July 22 after a scuffle with a Madison police officer near a food co-op. He is identified in Wisconsin court records as Black or Latino. The shooting is the third time since 2012 that Madison police have killed someone in the same neighborhood — the same neighborhood the coverage calls "the heart of the city's most liberal neighborhood." Madison is 70% white. The killing has forced the city, the standard account reports, to once again reckon with calls from the Black community to address racial justice issues. The family of Corey Ruiz [has stated he did not deserve the shooting](/articles/2026-07-24-family-of-corey-ruiz-says-he-did-not-deserve-madison-police-shooting/). The department says he had a knife. There is no body-camera footage, as the chief [has acknowledged](/articles/2026-07-24-madison-police-shooting-lacks-body-camera-footage-chief-says/).

All of that is accurate. None of it names what the framing itself is doing.

The disruption frame is not neutral. It is a specific analytical choice: the killing is the disruption, and the events are the normal. The farmers' market is the baseline, and the body is what interrupted it. This is the move a liberal white city makes every time police violence lands in a neighborhood whose self-image depends on not being the kind of place where police kill Black and Latino men. The body does not belong to the neighborhood. The body is the thing that happened *to* the neighborhood from outside — a disruption, an interruption, an unfortunate incident that forced the city to once again reckon with calls from the Black community.

The cui-bono trace here is not about who benefits from Ruiz's death — no one benefits. The trace is about who benefits from the coverage frame. The disruption frame lets Madison's white majority experience the killing as something that happened *to them* — something that interrupted their farmers' market, their concerts, their sense of their city as the kind of place that doesn't do this. The frame is the city's emotional insurance policy: the death is an intrusion, not a product. The neighborhood is the victim. The body is the inconvenience.

This is what the otherwise competent wire copy obscures. Madison is 70% white. The neighborhood where Ruiz was killed is, per the coverage, "the heart of the city's most liberal neighborhood." The same neighborhood has now produced three police killings of Black or Latino men since 2012 — Paul Heenan in 2012, Tony Robinson in 2015, Corey Ruiz in 2026. A liberal white neighborhood that produces three police killings in the same neighborhood in fourteen years is not experiencing disruptions. It is producing them. The farmers' market and the body are not separate categories. They are products of the same apparatus: a city whose white majority has built a pleasant summer calendar on top of a policing structure that kills Black and Latino men in the same neighborhood.

The wire's discipline — who, what, when, where — cannot, by construction, name the structural pattern. The coverage can report that the shooting is the third in the same neighborhood since 2012. It cannot report that a liberal white city whose police kill Black and Latino men three times in the same neighborhood in fourteen years is not a city experiencing disruptions. It is a city whose policing apparatus produces the bodies, and whose summertime events calendar produces the sense that the bodies are what interrupted the normal.

The arc does not bend toward justice on its own. It bends only when specific people, in a specific moment, push it. The push here begins with refusing the disruption frame. The farmers' market is not the baseline. The body is the baseline. The city that produces the body is the story. The cancellation of the summertime events is not the news — it is the city's first honest acknowledgment that the body was there before the events, that the body belongs to the neighborhood, and that the neighborhood's pleasant self-image has been sustained by refusing to name what its own policing apparatus keeps doing. The Black community is not calling for reform from outside the normal. The Black community is the normal calling the white majority to account for the apparatus it has built and is still building, the apparatus whose third body in fourteen years landed this week at the intersection by the food co-op, yards from the farmers' market that Madison's white majority would rather not see as what it is: the pleasant surface of a structure whose foundation is the body count.
