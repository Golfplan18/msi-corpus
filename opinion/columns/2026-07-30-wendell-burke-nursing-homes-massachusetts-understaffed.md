The call light came on at 9:40 at night and stayed on past 11:20, past 11:35, past midnight, past 12:10. Lynette Couture was 55, recovering at Plymouth Harborside Healthcare from a car crash that had shattered her left leg — doctors had drilled a metal device into the bone to put it back together. She asked for pain medicine three times between 9:40 and 10:40 that September night. When the call button in her room went unanswered, she called the nursing station twice from her own cell phone. No one picked up. A non-medical staffer working a double shift finally chased down a nurse at 12:15 a.m. — more than two and a half hours after the first ask. "The angrier I got, the more I cried," she told The New Bedford Light. "I made it known to everyone that walked past my room: it was excruciating."

Massachusetts's own data say the quiet part aloud: [half of the state's nursing homes are now short the staff their residents are paying for](https://apnews.com/article/nursing-homes-massachusetts-understaffed-care-9f12003fe034e3cf4027948d5f6cc013), according to reporting this week from the Associated Press. Not a few struggling facilities, not outlier neglect — half. The institution America built to hold the weight families cannot carry alone is telling us, in its own reports, that it can no longer carry that weight either.

There is a real defense of these facilities, and it must be named honestly. Medicaid reimbursement rates have not kept pace with the cost of actual care. A home where the state's per-diem falls short of what round-the-clock nursing genuinely costs has limited room to staff at safe levels — and many operators, especially smaller and independent ones, are making real choices inside a broken reimbursement structure, and the crisis has their names on it even when the cause does not. To ignore that is to punish the wrong people.

But that defense, however real for some, cannot explain the entire architecture. The same policy apparatus that keeps reimbursement rates inadequate has also [delayed the minimum wage floor for the workers who actually provide the care](/articles/2026-04-11-trump-administration-resets-review-clock-delaying-minnesota-nursing-home-wage-fl/) — an act that reveals something about whose interests the system serves when the margins get tight. And the system now forces families to [spend down every dollar of their savings before Medicaid will cover a bed](/articles/2026-03-27-why-some-families-deliberately-spend-down-income-for-medicaid-nursing-care/), a process that used to be called something else when other institutions did it to other people.

I want to give the conservative objection its strongest honest version before I refuse it, because Couture is not the argument — she is the receipt, and the receipts come from somewhere. A conservative is allowed to ask whether the Medicaid formula is honest, whether the regulatory regime is competent, whether the inspections are doing any good, whether the unions that represent the aides are helping or hurting. The conservative position I was raised with — Burke, Oakeshott, the encyclicals — does not pretend the state is a competent manager of anything, and does not pretend that pouring more money through the same pipe will reach the bedside. That is the right skepticism. It is not the skepticism this industry is selling. The industry is not arguing for less state and more local. The industry is arguing for less staff and more return. Those are different propositions, and confusing them is the entire trick.

The shortage is the product of a specific decision, made by specific people in specific buildings: to buy the homes with borrowed money, load the debt onto the homes themselves, and extract the difference between the Medicaid check and the labor cost as a return on capital. When you load a nursing home with debt and pay yourself first, the first thing that goes is the staff. This is not a secret. It is the business model. The industry calls it a turnaround. The residents call it the call light that never gets answered.

I know how the financialization works; I once traded the desk where we priced the abstraction of real commodities into paper claims, and the logic is the same whether the asset is corn futures or a nursing home's balance sheet: treat the human cost as an externality, optimize the margin, let someone else absorb the consequence. The figures are not an accident of geography. A federal Government Accountability Office [study](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106163) found that private-equity-owned nursing homes had higher rates of serious deficiencies in care than the rest of the industry. [In Michigan, nearly six thousand cases of abuse and neglect were logged](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-01-05-michigan-nursing-homes-log-nearly-6,000-abuse-and-neglect-cases-as-reform-stalls/) in a single year as reform stalled. The market is responding rationally. The thing it is rational toward is not the patient in the bed.

In the best nursing homes there was a covenant — between the aide who knew the resident's name and the morning she preferred her coffee, between the nurse who caught the change in breathing and the family who trusted them with someone they loved. It was an intermediate institution, in the language the Church uses: not the family, not the state, but something in between, holding the weight by proximity and attention and care. What has replaced it, too often, is a financial architecture where staffing is the largest controllable cost line and every unfilled shift moves money upward — toward debt service, toward management fees, toward the quarterly return of owners who will never see the person in the bed.

Pius XI, in *Quadragesimo Anno*, set down a principle the encyclical treats as a fixed and unchangeable rule of social philosophy: it is an injustice and a grave evil to assign to a larger and higher organization what a smaller and lower one can do. The corollary the encyclical implies is that it is a worse evil to assign to an absentee owner what a community, a family, a parish, or a co-operative member can do. The people who own these homes have never seen Lynette Couture's room. The aides who work in them have, and they cannot recruit another aide at the wage the absentee owner allows, and the cycle grinds on. Half the homes in the state are short-staffed. That is not a labor-market story. It is an ownership story.

So what is the answer that is neither the buyout nor the bureau? It is the same answer the co-operative tradition has been giving since 1844 — the year the Rochdale Pioneers opened their little store — and the same answer the rural electric co-ops gave in 1936, when the REA loaned member-owned co-ops the money to wire a countryside the for-profit utilities had decided to leave dark, and the same answer the parish has been giving for longer than either. The home that is owned by the people who use it — the residents, the families, the aides, the community — is a different institution than the home that is owned by a fund. The Green House model — small homes where the aides know the elders and the elders know the aides — is one version. The faith-based nonprofit, where the operating margin is plowed back into staffing instead of distributed to limited partners, is another. The mutual — the resident- and member-governed facility — is a third. Each is a real, working, distributed answer. None of them centralize anything. None of them turn the bed into a yield. Each is harder to build than a leveraged buyout, and each is harder to abandon when the labor market tightens, and that is the point.

Lynette Couture will, I hope, walk out of Plymouth Harborside on her own leg. The next woman in that bed — or the next man — will not be a unit of analysis for any of us. They will be a person, in a room, in a place, who has trusted that the people who run the place will come when she calls. That trust is what the industry is selling. That trust is what the buyout is extracting. And when the call light stays on past 11:20 and 11:35 and midnight and 12:10, the people who own the home are not managing a turnaround. They are failing the only test a home exists to pass.
