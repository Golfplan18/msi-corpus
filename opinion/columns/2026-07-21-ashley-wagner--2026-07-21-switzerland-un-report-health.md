Governments built a food system that prices billions out of healthy meals. The United Nations released its annual hunger report this week with a number that should stop every finance minister at the morning briefing: a healthy diet now costs $4.28 per person per day, globally, in purchasing-power-parity dollars — up from $3.44 in 2021 and $2.94 in 2017. The international extreme poverty line is $3.00. Eating well costs more than the metric the world uses to define destitution.

2.7 billion people — roughly a third of everyone alive — cannot afford it. In Africa, the proportion is two out of every three. The report's authors noted that the share of people unable to afford a healthy diet has fallen from 37.4 percent in 2021 to 31.2 percent now, and called that progress. The cost of a healthy diet rose 24 percent over those same four years. The progress is real. It is being eaten alive by the cost trajectory. That is the kind of progress you measure from the donor podium, not from the kitchen table.

The kitchen-table version of this number is straightforward. $4.28 a day is $1,562 a year. For a family of four, that is $6,248 — just for food that meets the World Health Organization's definition of adequate, balanced, and diverse. Before rent. Before daycare. Before the mortgage. In the United States, the average SNAP benefit runs roughly $6 per person per day — technically above the UN's benchmark. The technicality is the trick. That $6 still falls below the low-cost food plan for a family of four in most US counties, and the UN's $4.28 figure assumes a globally averaged basket that reflects what $4.28 buys in the United States, not what it buys in Detroit or the Mississippi Delta. [The data the world already has](/articles/2026-06-26-us-falls-short-of-peer-nations-on-health-food-income-education-data-shows/) makes the same point from a different direction: on the measures that determine whether families actually eat well, the richest country on earth does not lead.

The numbers the UN published are for 2025. They will get worse. The FAO's chief economist, Maximo Torero, warned that development assistance to Africa — where two-thirds of the population already cannot afford healthy food — has been cut significantly, and that the disruption to commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz from the U.S.-Iran war will push commodity costs higher. So the trajectory the report documents is this: food gets more expensive, the people who need help most get less of it, and the supply chains that carry what remains grow more dangerous. Marion Nestle has been writing about the architecture that produces this outcome for decades in *Food Politics* — how the food industry shapes nutrition policy, how farm subsidies flow to commodity crops that become high-fructose corn syrup rather than the fresh produce the dietary guidelines recommend, how the entire system is organized around what is profitable to grow and process rather than what is healthy to eat. The UN's numbers are the global proof of the same structural failure. The pattern holds from Lansdale to Lagos.

The first two corporal works of mercy are feed the hungry and give drink to the thirsty. My grandmother's generation ran that obligation through the women's sodality at St. Stanislaus — the funeral luncheon, the Saint Vincent de Paul conference that quietly covered a family's January heating bill, the school principal who made sure the kid who needed shoes got shoes without anyone identifying who bought them. That was parish-level infrastructure for a society that understood feeding people was not optional. The UN report is the global accounting: 2.7 billion people the infrastructure is not reaching, and the institutional will to reach them being reduced in real time. WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus named the tools that work — front-of-pack labelling, taxes on unhealthy products, protections for children from harmful marketing. They exist. They have evidence behind them. The IFAD's president, Alvaro Lario, made the institutional pitch: "Resilient value chains must be a central part of the answer. Our task now is to turn the evidence SOFI provides into investment." Worth the airtime it got. If "resilient value chains" means more of the same donor-driven value-chain projects that have watched the cost of a healthy diet climb 24 percent over four years, the report's own numbers argue the approach is not working at the scale the moment requires. They are not deployed at scale because the food industry's lobbying budget outpaces the public-health budget in country after country where both are measured. It is the same architecture that makes a head of broccoli cost more than a box of cereal: the mechanism works, the implementation doesn't, and the reason traces to who funds the campaigns.

This is what Taylor Swift was naming in "Anti-Hero" — the moment the structural failure gets metabolized into personal failing. "It's me, hi, I'm the problem, it's me." 2.7 billion people are not the problem. They are the outcome of a food economy built to produce exactly this result — healthy food priced beyond reach, junk food priced for maximum consumption, and then the people who cannot afford the healthy food told the issue is their shopping habits, their cooking, their priorities. The song's diagnostic power is that it stages the internalization itself, the moment you mistake the system's architecture for your own character defect. Every parent who has stood in a grocery aisle doing mental math on whether the broccoli or the boxed cereal fits this week's budget knows that moment. The UN just quantified it at planetary scale.

A healthy diet is not a luxury. It is the foundation. The countries that have implemented junk-food taxes, front-of-pack labelling, and school meal programs have seen diet quality improve. The evidence base is not the problem. What is in question is whether governments will use the tools they already have or continue to let the food industry set the terms while 2.7 billion people pay the difference. The trajectory is toward worse. The choices that produced this outcome are reversible. Whether they are reversed depends on whether anyone with power reads this report and decides the math matters more than the lobby.
