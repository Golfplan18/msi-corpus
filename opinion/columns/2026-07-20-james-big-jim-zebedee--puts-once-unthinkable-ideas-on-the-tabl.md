Trump and Rubio are engineering a Lebanese civil war and calling it disarmament.

Here is the plan, stripped of the language Washington wraps around it: train Lebanese soldiers to fight Hezbollah. Hand them off from the Israeli troops currently sitting in southern Lebanon. Vet them unit by unit to make sure you are not arming the wrong ones. Send them into neighborhoods where Hezbollah has been part of the fabric for forty-two years, and tell them to rip that fabric out.

Secretary Rubio called this the "ideal outcome." Then he called it "a tall order." That is Washington-speak for a plan whose architects know it cannot work but have no other plan to offer. When a man at the shop counter says a repair is "a tall order," he means he already knows the truck is not worth fixing. He is just not ready to say so yet.

Now, I want you to think about what this looks like from the ground. The Lebanese army is under-resourced. Its own Treasury — our Treasury — reported in May that Hezbollah gets intelligence tips from inside Lebanon's state security agencies, including the military. Soldiers in that army do not want to be seen doing Israel's work. Many of them lack the will to fight their own countrymen. Every one of these obstacles was named by the planners themselves. They named them and proceeded anyway.

That is the pattern, and it has a name that does not require a book to understand: find a proxy, train a proxy, equip a proxy, and when the proxy fails, blame the locals. The training mission creates contractors. The equipment transfers create pipelines. The organizational momentum builds and builds until it outlasts every strategic reason it was supposed to serve. The plan to vet Lebanese units — to sort the reliable from the compromised inside an institution the government's own reports say is penetrated — is not a serious military proposal. It is machinery looking for a purpose.

The Lebanese soldiers know this. Their families know this. Their mothers know what happens when you send a young man to disarm his neighbors in a country that already fought a fifteen-year civil war. Those mothers also know something the planners in Washington do not have to learn: the planners will not be there when the fracture comes.

Hezbollah has said plainly how it will respond. Hassan Fadlallah, the group's parliamentary representative, said they would fight the Lebanese army "just as we fight the Israeli army." Forty-two years of embedded roots in Lebanese Shia society do not get pulled out by a training program funded from six thousand miles away. Former Lebanese general Khalil Helou, who opposes Hezbollah, said disarmament could take years and might require invading all of Lebanon. Israel itself — the strongest military in the region — acknowledges it cannot disarm Hezbollah alone. It has devastated Hezbollah's leadership and depleted its missile stocks, and it still cannot finish the job. What it can do is keep fighting and eroding capabilities. What it cannot do is hand a Lebanese soldier a rifle and a set of instructions and expect a different outcome.

And then there is the Syrian piece, which is where the plan goes from reckless to dangerous.

President Trump proposed that Syria help cut off the supply routes that feed Hezbollah through the Lebanese border. On paper, that sounds like a logistics fix. On the ground, it means introducing armed forces into a country that has every reason to fear exactly that. Syria occupied Lebanon from 1976 to 2005. That memory is not abstract. It is alive in the streets and in the families who lived through it. Former deputy prime minister Ghassan Hasbani, who opposes Hezbollah, said the proposals "bring back bad memories." In the Lebanese register, that phrase carries the weight of a generation.

But the sectarian dimension is what makes this genuinely dangerous. Syria's new military includes radical Sunni Islamists accused of killing Syrian minorities en masse. Placing those forces on Lebanese soil to confront a Shia organization, inside a state apparatus that is itself divided along sectarian lines, does not solve a supply-chain problem. It creates a civil-religious war. The planners may imagine this as a border-enforcement detail. What it is, on the ground, is a sectarian accelerant dropped into a country that already knows what that fire looks like.

The soldiers and their families are the ones who will carry this. They are being asked to do what the strongest army in the region admits it cannot do. They are being asked to fracture their own institution along lines the planners in Washington cannot navigate and will not live with. They are being asked to do this with the help of a foreign military whose presence in their country is a generational wound. And they are being asked by people who will fly home when the first shots are fired and write memos about lessons learned.

The cold ledger is simple: Washington is designing a proxy campaign that its own documents show cannot work, in a country whose last civil war lasted fifteen years, using an army its own government says is penetrated by the force it is supposed to disarm, assisted by a military whose presence in Lebanon is national trauma and whose sectarian character will turn a state operation into a war between communities. The Lebanese soldiers and their families will be the ones left holding the wreckage. The planners will not be there.
