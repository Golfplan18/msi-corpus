Nicolás Maduro's lawyers filed papers Wednesday asking Judge Alvin K. Hellerstein to dismiss a cocaine-trafficking indictment — before the court hears a single piece of evidence. They do not answer the allegation that Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, helped move cocaine through the United States; they say the court may not ask the question. "No American court has ever presided over the criminal trial of a foreign leader who was recognized by their own country as the sitting head of state at the time the charges were brought," the lawyers wrote. "That is not an accident of history. It reflects a rule older than the common law: heads of state are exempt from the criminal process of any national court but their own."

The motion treats a contested doctrine as settled law. It is not.

There is a real doctrine here, and it deserves to be argued in its strongest form. Head-of-state immunity is not a docket convenience. *The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon*, 11 U.S. 116 (1812), grounded sovereign immunity in the mutual independence of states: a foreign sovereign's public vessels pass through our ports by our permission, not by right. A sitting head of state enjoys similar protection because the alternative is to let one nation's courts sit in judgment on another nation's government — adjudicating foreign-policy disputes dressed as criminal cases, shielding leaders from prosecution motivated by political rivalry rather than law, preserving diplomatic relations that depend on mutual recognition of sovereign authority. The structural pillar is reciprocity: the protection of U.S. officials who travel abroad on state business. Custom, practice, and institutional interest converge on the same conclusion.

But the steel-man is precisely what it is, and no more. The reciprocity pillar supports immunity from foreign-court proceedings brought to embarrass or to settle political scores. It cuts weakest where the alleged conduct — narco-trafficking at the scale charged here — is criminal by every sovereign's domestic standard, with no plausible argument that international law tolerates it.

And the doctrine has moved. In 1999 the House of Lords stripped Augusto Pinochet of immunity for crimes against humanity, holding that international crimes fall outside the protection customary immunity provides. The International Criminal Court operates on the same principle: its jurisdiction over sitting heads of state for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity is the institutional expression of a proposition that absolute immunity cannot shield leaders from accountability for the most serious offenses. The defense will respond, as it must, that drug trafficking is not a Rome Statute offense, that the international-crimes category is the exception that proves the rule, and that ordinary criminal prosecution remains barred. The reply: the principle is not a closed statutory list but the broader proposition that absolute immunity yields where the gravity of what is alleged is grave enough. Trafficking at the scale charged here, with the apparatus of a state alleged to have been deployed in its service, sits inside that category. Reports of an early-2026 procedural ruling allowing the prosecution to proceed past a jurisdictional challenge fit the same pattern: the courts that have touched this case have not treated the indictment as one a sitting head of state's immunity automatically extinguishes.

The filing makes a second move worth naming. "Older than the common law" is rhetorical costume, not citation. The rule is a creature of the common law, datable to *The Schooner Exchange* and to the diplomatic practices of the European state system that preceded it. A brief that demands dispositive treatment begins with an overstatement. The motion treats the immunity question as settled rather than open, which is convenient because the authority does not quite say what the lawyers need it to say.

Recognition does the work. Whatever immunity a head of state has, it is a matter of recognition by other sovereigns, not self-anointing. The principle is what the modern doctrine applies. *United States v. Noriega*, 746 F. Supp. 1506 (S.D. Fla. 1990), turned on whether the indictment superseded any immunity that might have attached — Manuel Noriega was recognized by the United States as Panama's head of state — but the recognition principle is what courts have made operative in the years since. Recognition did the work then. It does the work now. The same Executive Branch that would normally file suggestions of immunity has instead filed an indictment here. The United States has not recognized Maduro as beyond its process; it has charged him. His lawyers point to recognition "by their own country" — but that is a domestic assertion, not a jurisdictional one. It is a premise to be litigated, not a reason to skip the litigation. Judge Hellerstein is not required to accept the premise just because the lawyers offered it.

Then there is Flores. Whatever immunity a head of state has, it does not attach to her by marriage. She is charged for her own alleged conduct. "First lady" is a title; it is not a separate sovereignty. If the rule were as capacious as the motion needs it to be, every head of state could add his family to his immunity the way he adds them to a palace. That is not the common law; that is a dynasty. It may be how Maduro has governed. It is not how the doctrine of immunity reads.

The structural parallel is exact. Head-of-state immunity, if granted, would shield a foreign leader from cocaine charges in a Manhattan courtroom exactly as the *Bivens* contraction shields federal officers from constitutional liability, exactly as the political-question doctrine shields executive killing from judicial review, exactly as the state-secrets privilege shields evidence from disclosure. In each case a legal doctrine operates as the mechanism that prevents a court from reaching the substance of what is alleged — the lock between serious accusation and substantive examination. The doctrinal stack differs. The function is identical: a rule exists, the rule holds, the substance is never examined.

Head-of-state immunity is legitimate. Courts should not adjudicate foreign policy, and political prosecutions of sitting leaders would carry dangerous consequences. But immunity is legitimate as a presumption courts weigh against the gravity of what is alleged, not as an absolute bar that extinguishes prosecution before the evidence is heard. The motion before Judge Hellerstein seeks to collapse that distinction.

November 17 is when the court takes up the question. The issue is not whether head-of-state immunity exists. It is whether the doctrine that protects sovereign authority functions as a shield against accountability for serious crimes — or as a presumption that, against allegations of this severity, courts must weigh and can overcome.

If it holds as absolute, the lock closes. The accused lives behind a rule that exists, in this application, to protect power from the consequences of what it is alleged to have done. The rule protects sovereigns from other sovereigns' courts. It does not launder cocaine.
