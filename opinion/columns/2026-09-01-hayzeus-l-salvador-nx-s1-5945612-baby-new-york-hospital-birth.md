Trinity Health runs 91 hospitals. Last year it booked more than $25 billion in revenue and an operating margin north of five percent. It is a Michigan-based Catholic nonprofit, tax-sheltered as a charity, named for the Holy Trinity — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, the One who said whatever you did to the least of these you did to me. And it decided to close Burdett Birth Center, the only birthing center in Troy, New York, because the unit was losing money.

Starletta Washington, who runs the YWCA in Troy, was born at Samaritan Hospital on the hill above the old factory town. She had her kids there. When Trinity announced the closure, she fought back. "It was devastating," Washington said. "Nobody else was going to be born in the city of Troy unless they were born on a city bus, in the back of a cab, or, disgustingly, on the side of the street."

Republicans, Democrats, midwives, doulas, the Catholic bishop, Planned Parenthood, and YWCA leaders stood together. Republican Mayor Carmella Mantello, Democratic Assemblyman John T. McDonald III, the Republican county executive, a Republican state senator, Catholic Bishop Edward Scharfenberger — even people who agreed on nothing else agreed that Trinity was wrong. New York Attorney General Letitia James opened an investigation. McDonald and the Republicans he teamed with scraped together $5 million in state funding. Trinity, which had sued the state to push the closure through, dropped the lawsuit and announced Burdett would stay open.

This is what should happen everywhere a nonprofit hospital is being treated like a line item.

The mothers of Troy won a real fight. Let me say that plainly, before I say anything harder. They organized. They rallied at the YWCA. Doulas and midwives printed pink Save Burdett signs and handed them out at the farmers market. They ran a community survey. They pulled the mayor and the statehouse onto the same page. They made the bishop show up. They forced a tax-sheltered Catholic charity to reverse itself.

Now listen carefully.

Burdett's C-section rate is about a quarter of deliveries. New York's statewide average is roughly a third. Preterm-birth and low-birth-weight numbers at Burdett are better, too. Midwives and doulas working alongside OB-GYNs produce measurably better outcomes for mothers — exactly the model patient-safety advocates have begged the rest of American obstetrics to copy. A community survey found one in four Troy residents didn't have access to a car. Trinity's plan was to send those mothers in labor to Albany, up to half an hour away. The executives never bothered to run that math.

Doula Jessica Hayek put it plainly: "Trinity Health is in the Midwest, and they are not in the community. So when you're looking at just the numbers from an office in the Midwest somewhere, they're not looking at the benefit that this place has on the community."

She is right. Trinity's answer to measurably better outcomes was to close the unit because it lost money. A nonprofit Catholic health system — tax-sheltered as a charity, named for the Trinity, claiming a gospel mission — decided keeping mothers and babies healthy in a low-income industrial city wasn't profitable enough.

The Catholic tradition calls that what it is. Pope Francis, off the coast of Lampedusa in 2013, named the failure to respond to people in extremis a "globalization of indifference." The U.S. bishops, with their Mexican counterparts in 2003, named the right of every human being to seek a dignified life, and the obligation of wealthy institutions not to make the search for it impossible. A system that shutters a working birth center in a working-class city because the spreadsheet on the fourteenth floor of the Michigan office said close it — that is the system of indifference the Pope and the bishops have named, in plain language, as sin.

I am a Catholic. I write from inside the tradition, not outside it. And I know what the tax-shelter buys. I have received Catholic schooling, Catholic hospital chaplaincy visits, Catholic Charities counseling, and the moral vocabulary of the tradition itself — much of it underwritten by the same nonprofit status Trinity claims. We have all benefited from the system the bishops keep naming as a system of indifference. That is why we have standing to say so. And the harder thing to say is this: what Trinity did in Troy is not an aberration. It is a pattern. Catholic hospital chains across the United States have consolidated, expanded, accumulated billions in net assets, and drifted from the mission their tax-shelter claims. Researchers who follow Catholic hospital systems have documented, year after year, what happens when the chains grow and the gospel language stays the same: charity care shrinks in the low-income ZIP codes, prices rise across the footprint, and the birthing units serving the poorest mothers are the first to close. I have read the alternative — that the chains are simply modern stewards of a complex system, that the spreadsheet is the modern version of discernment. I do not believe it. A discernment that always produces the same answer — close, consolidate, optimize, the poor last — is not discernment. It is the indifference Francis named, dressed in a coat of stewardship.

But the hardest truth is the one the celebration in Troy will not say out loud.

Hospitals have closed hundreds of maternity units since 2010. Most of those communities tried something. Most of them lost. Troy won because it had a sympathetic Republican mayor, an ambitious Democratic AG, a competent Democratic assemblyman, a YWCA director willing to make the issue famous, and a doula and birth educator who could run a campaign. The hundreds of maternity deserts being carved out across rural America have none of that. They have a hospital board, a press release, and a forty-minute drive to the next county.

What Troy did was substitute political power for the system's judgment. It worked once. It does not work at scale. The $5 million the state appropriated buys a few years. It does not fix the underlying arithmetic. Burdett's quality is a function of its unprofitability. The midwife-attended, doula-supported model is genuinely excellent care. It is also expensive, labor-intensive, and impossible to subsidize through normal insurance reimbursement. The next time Burdett's losses threaten Trinity's margins, the same politicians will be back demanding another check, another investigation, another round of threats. That is not a solution. It is a recurring crisis with a press release attached.

And the communities that lost their maternity units did not fail. They were failed. By the same indifference Francis named. By the same consolidation the bishops have watched for two decades. By a country that has decided a healthy birth is a privilege rather than a right, and that the mothers who live in places where the math doesn't work will simply have to make other arrangements.

McDonald, the Democratic assemblyman, said it best. "If we take down our swords, and put out our arms, maybe we can get something done." The swords are the right tool for one city. They are not a system. The next charity-sheltered hospital chain to try to strip a birthing unit from a town should expect exactly what Trinity got — a community that refuses to be erased by a balance sheet drawn up in another time zone. But after the press conference, somebody in the room has to ask what the bipartisan victory was actually built on, and whether the next town — the one without a media-savvy doula, the one without a politically connected bishop, the one where the Republican county executive has decided the issue is not worth his time — whether that town deserves more than a forty-minute drive to the next county and a press release. [Debt Jubilees](/articles/2026-06-28-moravian-church-s-debt-jubilee-retires-2-2-million-in-medical-debt/) retire the bills. They do not rebuild the unit.

Trinity's executives are not monsters. They are men and women doing a job inside a system the Catholic tradition names, plainly, as a system of indifference. The job is to keep ninety-one hospitals solvent across a footprint. The mission they claim is the gospel of Jesus Christ, who told his disciples that whatever they did to the least of these they did to him. The contradiction is not new. The Catholic hospital world has been living inside it for a generation. The contradiction is now in Troy, and the mothers of Troy forced it into the light. That is what the celebration is for. The work begins when the confetti is swept up and the bishop and the mayor and the assemblyman and the doula sit down in the same room and decide to fix the arithmetic, not just the announcement.

That is not a triumph. That is the floor.
