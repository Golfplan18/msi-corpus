*He sold the hammer, then offered to mediate.*

Benjamin Netanyahu and Israel Katz bombed an apartment building in southern Beirut and killed the people inside. Two are dead. Four more are wounded. They called it counter-terrorism.

This is not a breakdown in the peace talks. This is the peace talks.

Thomas Schelling taught in *The Strategy of Conflict* that violence conducted during negotiation is not a failure of diplomacy but its continuation by other means — a signal, transmitted at cost, designed to recalibrate the other side’s assessment of your resolve. The strike on Dahiyeh came hours after Israel accused Hezbollah of violating a [U.S.-brokered ceasefire](/articles/2026-06-06-israeli-airstrikes-kill-9-including-lebanese-army-officers-after-ceasefire-deal/). It came on the day the United States and Iran were supposed to finalize a deal Pakistan helped mediate. “The game of bad cop and good cop is outdated,” Iran’s chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, wrote on X. “If you lack the will and ability to fulfill your commitments, speaking of continuing the path is not possible.”

Whether every accusation in that post is true is beside the point. The perception alone — that Washington gave a green light — makes the deal worthless. An honest broker cannot supply one party with the weapon it swings and then pretend the blow was a surprise.

Barbara Tuchman, in *The March of Folly*, wrote that “insistence on a rooted notion regardless of contrary evidence is the source of the self-deception that characterizes folly.” The notion here — that a cycle of strikes, accusations, and “ceasefires” no party respects can yield anything but more dead in Dahiyeh — persists in the face of mounting contrary evidence. Days ago, Israel [traded direct strikes with Iran](/articles/2026-06-08-iran-and-israel-trade-strikes-as-mideast-violence-threatens-all-out-war/) after an earlier airstrike in Lebanon killed nine, including Lebanese army officers. The same pattern, the same ledger.

Robert Axelrod’s work on iterated games showed that when the shadow of the future is long, rivals can find ways to cooperate, because both sides know they will face each other again. The shadow over southern Lebanon right now is short — shortened further by each side’s conviction that the other cannot be trusted. That conviction is earned, on both sides, by years of exactly this: bombs on a negotiated ceasefire day.

Andrew Bacevich has spent two decades documenting the bipartisan commitment to military primacy in the Middle East, a commitment that survives every change of administration regardless of the cost, regardless of the [escalation cycles it produces](/articles/2026-06-08-iran-and-israel-trade-strikes-as-mideast-violence-threatens-all-out-war/). The weapons that hit that apartment building in Dahiyeh were almost certainly American-made. The diplomatic apparatus shielding Israel from the international consequences of the strike is American-operated. The ceasefire framework both sides are now violating was American-brokered. As Eisenhower warned in his farewell address — paragraph 24 — the acquisition of unwarranted influence by the military‑industrial complex “exists and will persist.” Sixty‑five years later, the arrangement is not neutral. It was never designed to be.

Under Michael Walzer’s just‑war principles, the attacker bears the burden of discrimination. A residential apartment building is a civilian object. Israel says it contained a Hezbollah command center. Israel has made similar assertions about residential targets before, rarely satisfying the evidentiary burden after the fact, and this strike, launched hours after an accusation of a ceasefire violation, deserves the same skepticism. Even granting the claim, proportionality demands that the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated not be excessive relative to the incidental civilian harm. Two dead. Four wounded. A building destroyed. The burden of justification rests with the party that fired the missiles into a neighborhood where families were living.

If two men brought their dispute to my shop and asked me to settle it, and I had sold one of them the hammer he was swinging, nobody in Redemption Springs would call me an honest broker. They would tell me to put the hammer down or stop pretending I was neutral. That is the position the United States holds tonight: the arms supplier posing as mediator, the weapons manufacturer calling for restraint, the veto cast at the United Nations shielding the strike and then the statement expressing concern about civilian casualties.

The region is drifting toward a wider war, and the great powers are not steering. Two people are dead tonight in Dahiyeh. Their names have not been released. They join a ledger that never balances. The men who ordered the strike will say it was necessary. The men who fired toward Israel will say the same. In the rubble of that apartment building is the foreign policy of a nation that cannot decide whether it is the mediator or the man selling the hammer.
