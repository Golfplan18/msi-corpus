> Politics requires alliance-building, and nobody knows that better than the pugnacious Teamsters President Sean O’Brien. He has spent the past few years cultivating ties with Republicans—chief among them Vice President JD Vance—to benefit his union. Mr. O’Brien is desperate for a win in Washington to sell to his 1.3 million members as he runs for re-election. Some Republicans in Congress seem eager to give him one—maybe two—as they seek to burnish their bona fides as defenders of the working class. These Republicans are doing more to help Democrats—the primary beneficiaries of Teamster campaign donations—than workers.

Frame-engineered relabeling (WSJ §A.1) and attribution of blame (Bandura) operate here through the character-assassination opener. O’Brien is “pugnacious” and “desperate.” Not a labor leader bargaining on behalf of 1.3 million members. Not an elected union president. Pugnacious and desperate. The adjectives deliver the verdict. In the cable years, we would open a segment exactly like this — attach a delegitimizing adjective to the target in the first reference, and every subsequent reference carries the taint. The reader never gets to evaluate the person; the evaluation is delivered in the adjective. Then the piece deploys the donor-class frame: Republicans who support rail safety are “helping Democrats.” Why? Because the union’s PAC — funded by workers’ dues — exercises its legal right to make political contributions, some of which go to Democrats. This is guilt-by-transitive-property, and it’s the shell game that lets the WSJ reader oppose a safety bill without having to say they oppose safety.

> The Teamsters’ membership has shrunk by nearly half since the 1970s amid a broader decline in organized labor. Between 2016 and 2025, members filed 373 petitions to decertify the Teamsters, according to *Reason* magazine. Some 60% of the decertification elections succeeded. You can’t blame union members for wearying of paying dues that bankroll Democratic candidates. In the 2023-24 election cycle, 92% of Teamsters PAC donations to federal candidates went to Democrats. An independent investigations officer issued a report in February accusing two former Teamster officials of treating the union credit card “as a blank check to permit them luxury living without limit,” including restaurant tabs topping $3,000. One was a close ally of Mr. O’Brien.

Frame-engineered relabeling (WSJ §A.1) and selective causal attribution (Bandura) run a three-part cascade: shrink the union, inflate the dissenters, and frame standard PAC spending as theft. The dues-bankrolling-Democrats line is the standard corporate-relief frame, laundered as worker outrage. The Teamsters PAC donates to the party that maintains the regulatory apparatus protecting their pensions and safety rules; the WSJ editorial board has spent seventy-five years lobbying for the financial-deregulation bills that drained those same pensions — including the 1999 repeal of Glass-Steagall, which opened pension assets to speculative banking risk. > Mr. O’Brien has taken a hard line in collective bargaining with the goal of winning rich pay packages he can showcase. In 2023, Yellow Corp., one of the country’s largest trucking companies, sought financial concessions from the Teamsters to stay in business. Mr. O’Brien refused and tweeted an image of a gravestone reading “Yellow 1924-2023.” The company filed for bankruptcy, and 22,000 Teamsters lost their jobs. After threatening UPS with a strike, Mr. O’Brien won a deal that increased average compensation for full-time drivers over five years to $170,000 from $145,000, including zero healthcare premiums and as much as seven weeks of vacation. Rising labor costs prompted UPS to cut 34,000 nonmanagement jobs last year. Meanwhile, his campaign to organize Amazon warehouse workers and drivers has met with little success.

The company cut 34,000 jobs — so the pay raise *caused* the layoffs. This is the donor-class inversion: when workers win, workers lose. The employer’s decision to cut jobs rather than accept lower margins is naturalized as an economic law. UPS chose layoffs. UPS could have chosen lower executive compensation, lower share buybacks, lower dividends. None of those choices appear in the paragraph. The paragraph is built to make the reader feel that $170,000 for a truck driver is the real problem — not the corporate decision to protect margins at the workers’ expense. We built this move.

Mr. Vance went to bat for the Teamsters last month by pressing President Trump to endorse the bill and calling House Republicans to press his cause. Perhaps he hopes Mr. O’Brien will return the favor if he runs for president in 2028. Fat chance.
