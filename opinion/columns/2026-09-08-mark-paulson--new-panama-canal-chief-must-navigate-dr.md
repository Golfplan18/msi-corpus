Global shipping is sacrificing Panama’s drinking water to keep its cargo moving.

The article is what I’m reading on the bench Monday night when I should be looking at the parts catalog. The headline in the *Adams County Times-Reporter* two weeks back was about a different shortage — feed prices for the dairy farms around here — but the mechanism was the same. Something a long way off moved a number on a piece of paper in Adams County, and by Friday somebody at the co-op was shaking his head about it.

The Panama Canal Authority is cutting daily transits because the freshwater lakes that feed the locks also supply drinking water to half of Panama. The fifty-mile canal between the Pacific and Atlantic handles 40 percent of the containerized goods that come into this country. There is not enough water for both the ships and the taps, and somebody has to choose.

The canal authority is choosing the taps.

That is the right decision. It is also an indictment of the system that made the decision necessary.

Ilya Espino de Marotta, a marine engineer who started at the canal in 1985, took over Monday at the top of the Panama Canal Authority. She came up when the United States still ran the waterway, worked on the widening project that lets today’s megaships pass, and watched the handover to Panama in 1999. She now oversees the locks, the lakes, and the drinking-water supply tied to both.

The water-gazers want to call this a crisis. They are reading the wrong chart.

Look at the auction. A tanker paid a record $5.3 million last month for priority transit. When somebody pays five million dollars to jump the queue, the queue is the whole market for a while. That is not a market in collapse. It is a market pricing scarcity so cleanly that the bidding clears in one round.

The [El Niño forcing this year’s water pinch](/articles/2026-08-29-strong-el-ni-o-disrupts-global-commodity-and-shipping-markets/) is forecast to be the strongest in decades. The shipping system has been raising its own prices for months. The canal is raising them in a different way: by letting fewer ships through and charging the ones it lets pass.

Forty percent of container traffic. Think about what that means in the parts room behind my bench. The Briggs & Stratton carburetor I ordered for a customer’s snowblower has a good chance of having come through the canal. The Honda EU2200i generator I sold in September did not grow in Adams County. Neither did the Stihl chainsaw parts I have been waiting on since July. The supply chain is invisible until it is not, and then it is the only thing you can see.

Diesel at the pump. Feed for the dairy farms. Parts on my shelf.

The canal is a shortcut, but it is also a dependency. The freshwater lake is infrastructure, drinking water, habitat, and a limit on how much global commerce can demand from one place. The shipping system treated that limit as someone else’s problem until the lake began keeping its own books.

Wendell Berry writes in *The Unsettling of America* about an extractive economy that treats land, water, and people as inputs, then moves on when they are used up. Panama’s canal authority is not doing that today. It is cutting the ships to keep the water available for the people who live there.

The system upstream of the authority is doing it.

Somewhere along the way, a global shipping economy made a freshwater lake carry a forty-percent trade route and assumed Panama would figure out the drinking water later. The canal’s current operating rule is a correction to that assumption. It is not a solution to the larger structure.

I have kept the ice-out date on Lake Petenwell for twelve years running. The trend is the same trend Panama is dealing with: the water does not come when it used to. I do not know what that means for container ships. I know what it means for the deer on my property and the syrup season at my uncle Pete’s old sugar bush. Things are different than they were. The climate has moved on from the place where the rules were written.

That is not a metaphor. It is the operating condition.

The geopolitical contest over the canal — the United States and China competing for influence over global trade routes — is usually described as a fight over who controls the chokepoint. That description is too small. The chokepoint belongs to Panama, and scarcity is compounding its leverage in real time.

The [port-side pressure campaign Washington is running to push back on China’s footprint across Latin America](/articles/2026-03-06-us-imposes-travel-bans-port-actions-in-push-to-counter-china-s-reach-in-latin-am/) only sharpens the contest. Both powers are courting the same waterway. Every move on either side of the Pacific makes the canal a prize both sides have to keep courting, and Espino de Marotta holds the auction gavel.

The drought makes the geopolitics worse because every saved transit becomes a favor somebody can ask for later. A ship that moves is not merely a ship that moves. It is a contract fulfilled, a factory supplied, a port kept busy, a government that can claim its route remains open. A ship that waits is a reminder that Panama controls the water and that water controls everybody else.

The same arithmetic appears in smaller form at Windrose Technology, the Chinese-European electric-trucking startup that was supposed to rival Tesla. Roughly 100 Chinese employees walked after a wage cycle went sideways. Partial back pay arrived only in time to meet an Aug. 30 deadline set by Chinese arbitrators. Former American employees are discussing six-figure back-wage claims of their own.

Read the situation backwards and you can see the corporate answer. Founder and CEO Wen Han stopped treating truck bodies as the center of the business and began moving toward the software that runs on trucks built by contract factories. That is a capital-light pivot toward the highest-margin slice of the trucking stack, made while legacy automakers are admitting the same thing.

The pain was the price of the refocus.

That is the sentence the balance sheet prefers. The workers call it unpaid wages.

The canal and Windrose are not the same business. They are the same lesson. When a system reaches a constraint, the people with control call the constraint a signal. The people without control pay for it. At the canal, Panama protects its drinking water by limiting global shipping. At Windrose, workers absorbed the cost of a company changing direction. In both cases, someone below the strategy deck carries the load.

The macro is the micro. Global airfreight rates per kilogram ran 24 percent above year-ago levels in August, according to Xeneta. Volume pressure forces repricing, and repricing funds the next round of investment that the Windroses of the world need to scale. But the repricing does not fall evenly. It reaches the customer waiting on a part, the dairy farmer buying feed, the mechanic trying to keep an old generator running, and the worker waiting for wages already earned.

A market can behave clearly without behaving kindly.

The Panama Canal is also the best possible test of what the global shipping system believes water is for. If water is merely another input, the ships get priority until the taps run dry. If water is part of a living community, the ships wait.

The canal authority has chosen the second answer. It should have been the first answer thirty years ago.

I am not going to tell you this is a sign the country is falling apart. It is a sign the country’s plumbing is showing. When the lake goes down, you find out what the system actually does. It cuts the ships. It charges the ones it lets through. It asks the country downstream to wait. None of that is an accident. All of it was decided somewhere, by somebody, with freshwater treated as the cheapest line item on the budget.

The El Niño will end. The water will come back. Transits will rise again. The parts catalog will refill.

The question is what happens to the structure that put a freshwater lake and a forty-percent trade route in the same place. One administrator in Panama cannot answer that. Neither can an auction price, a startup pivot, or an airfreight chart.

But the water has answered it already.

When the ships and the taps compete, the ships have to wait.
