On Monday, the Department of Homeland Security filed a regulatory proposal attaching a $103,265 fee to every new H-1B visa application, payable by the employer before the government decides whether the application has merit. The filing opens a thirty-day public comment window before the fee can take effect. The H-1B is the primary pathway for high-skill foreign professionals into the United States workforce, capped at eighty-five thousand new visas per year, with hospitals, research universities, and other nonprofit institutions already exempt from the cap.

The new fee replaces a $100,000 levy Donald Trump announced last year as a travel ban he signed personally. The United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, sitting in Boston, [struck that fee down in July](/articles/2026-07-24-appeals-court-denies-trump-administration-s-100-000-h-1b-visa-fee-bid/), ruling that the President exceeded his executive authority and used an improper process. The new fee proceeds through standard notice-and-comment rulemaking under the Immigration and Nationality Act — the procedure the court demanded.

The filing projects $8.8 billion in revenue. The filing is unusually candid about where the money will go. H-1B fees are normally justified by the cost of adjudicating applications. This filing admits the fee will fund "broader costs" of the immigration system, including the immigration courts that decide deportation cases. The H-1B program is being run as a slush fund for the deportation docket.

The new fee will land on the technology firms and Wall Street banks that have built their staffing pipelines on the visa. The previous $100,000 charge was structured as a condition for entering the country, which exempted those firms because most H-1B hires are already inside the United States on F-1 student visas by the time they apply. The new fee attaches to the visa filing itself, so it falls on the sponsoring employer rather than the worker at the border. The administration is also working through a separate $100,000 fee on Optional Practical Training — the program through which international students work on student visas after graduation. OPT is the actual pipeline: firms hire OPT workers first and sponsor them for H-1B only later. The OPT fee, if finalized, will choke the pipeline before the H-1B fee does.
