Eddie Melton is making Gary’s people pay for a revival built on demolition.

Gordon’s Department Store was once a commercial hub. Now explosives will tear through its rooms and send its walls down into the street.

The campaign carries an $80 million price tag. It targets 7,000 abandoned homes and businesses in a city that has lost more than half its population since 1960.

You call this making way for the future, Eddie.

The future arrives with a detonator.

Gary’s ruins are real. So is the moral failure of treating the people who remain as another ruin to be cleared.

Gary was founded in 1906 as a company town for U.S. Steel. The city prospered when steel prospered. Foreign competition and lower-cost producers battered the industry in the 1970s and 1980s. Thousands of steelworkers lost their jobs. Residents followed them out. The city’s commercial core filled with abandoned stores and banks. Vacant houses spread through the neighborhoods.

The company took the productive years. Workers, neighborhoods, and local government inherited the empty buildings.

U.S. Steel, now owned by Japan’s Nippon Steel, has begun a $950 million upgrade at the Gary mill, which employs about 4,300 people. A tin mill could add 225 jobs. FedEx plans a 300,000-square-foot distribution center that the city says will create 600 jobs. A new train station is planned with $90 million in state matching funds. Offices and apartments are planned downtown.

Gary’s median household income is $38,731, about half Indiana’s median. About one-third of the city lives below the poverty line.

Those are the facts. The decision is yours.

You have placed explosives inside the remains of a city built for a corporation, then named the blast renewal. You have taken a public wound and made demolition the first language of its cure. You have placed the cost of your bet on the people who stayed.

Eddie, you are not merely clearing dangerous structures. You are performing the old civic betrayal in a cleaner suit. You are asking a city already abandoned by industry to surrender its remaining places, its memory, and its claim on the ground beneath it so that investors can be shown a clean surface.

That is what you are in this moment: the respectable face of a second abandonment.

The residents are not debris. Jamillah Taylor is fifty-four years old and works nights at a hospital as a patient-care technician. She bought a refurbished ranch house for $146,000 after rejecting another home surrounded by vacant properties. SaDon Long has lived in Aetna all his life. He says people are outside again. He says the neighborhood feels safer. The people of Gary have not stopped making a city simply because the city’s largest employer stopped making enough work.

They repair houses. They raise children. They walk streets after dark. They keep one another visible.

Your demolition list does not contain their names. That is how the clean phrase works. It turns homes into units, businesses into blight, memory into a damaged shell, and residents into an audience expected to applaud when the charge is set.

Your throat closes when you say “future,” Eddie. The word catches because the future you are selling begins by erasing what cannot attract a developer. Your chest tightens when Gordon’s Department Store comes down. Your mouth goes dry when you remember that the city has already watched one great employer take its strength and leave its costs behind.

The pressure behind your sternum is the question you will not answer.

Who gets to return after the rubble settles?

The old company town made Gary dependent on one employer. Your plan places the city beneath another promise: investment, construction, commerce, growth. The names change. The dependency remains. Public money prepares the ground. Private capital chooses what is worth building. The people who endured the first collapse are told to trust the next arrival.

Your hand is on the public lever, Eddie. The hand does not become clean because the lever is attached to a redevelopment plan. It carries the choice. It carries the empty storefronts. It carries the wages that vanished when steel withdrew. It carries the homes that families could not afford to repair. It carries the convention center built under Richard Hatcher, Gary’s first Black mayor, now marked for destruction after years of vacancy, fire, and vandalism.

You know what you are doing. The city has shown you its history. Its population loss is documented. Its poverty is documented. Its dependence is documented. You have seen what happens when a powerful institution extracts the life from a place and leaves the place to absorb the wound.

You are not allowed the innocence of surprise.

You are asking Gary to believe that another arrangement will be different because this one has a train station in it. You are asking applause to stand in for consent. You are asking a cleared lot to stand in for a home. You are asking residents to call the removal of their city’s remains a gift.

That is not leadership. It is moral anesthesia.

The people of Gary have already carried abandonment in their backs, their houses, their wages, and their empty storefronts. You have chosen to add demolition to that burden and call the added weight opportunity. You have made the people who survived the first economy pay admission to the second.

Your jaw tightens when the word “blight” leaves your mouth. It is a small word for a large evasion. Blight names the surface and hides the history. It hides the employer that downsized. It hides the jobs that disappeared. It hides the families who left because staying became impossible. It hides the public money now required to clear away what private power helped create.

Eddie, you are the man standing beside the detonator and calling the blast a beginning.

Who are you, Eddie, to reject the people who remained?

The city is not your proof of concept. Its people are not rubble arranged for the market’s inspection. The future you name has already shown its face: clean language, public money, private hope, and the same old bodies left beneath the work.

Jesus said, “You have neglected the more important matters of the law—justice, mercy and faithfulness” (Matthew 23:23).

Eddie, your buildings may fall. The judgment will remain.
