A detained pastor is free. That is good. We should be glad about it and not pretend otherwise, because the habit of refusing good news to preserve a rhetorical position is a spiritual sickness, not a political strategy.

Ezra Jin Mingri, 57, leader of one of China's most influential underground churches, was released on July 3 from a detention center in southern China and flown to Los Angeles, where within 24 hours he was eating an In-N-Out hamburger and watching fireworks with his family on the eve of America's 250th anniversary. He told the Associated Press his release came after President Donald Trump raised his case directly with Xi Jinping in May — an intervention [MSI previously reported](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-05-16-trump-says-xi-will-consider-detained-pastor-ezra-jin-jimmy-lai-case-tough/) when Trump described freeing Hong Kong activist Jimmy Lai as "tough." Jin called his own release "a miracle."

I want to name the reflex before saying anything else: people who share my politics will be tempted to say nothing, or to say something grudging, or to change the subject. Resist it. The person left the cell. The person ate the hamburger. The person watched the fireworks with his family. The first and most important thing for anyone who claims the prophetic-pastoral tradition is to say: that is good. Do not make it smaller than it is because the president who did it is not your president.

He is not wrong to call it a miracle. The release of a high-profile Chinese detainee after a U.S. request is a genuinely rare event — the Associated Press noted that Beijing's leadership has become more unyielding to foreign pressure under Xi, making Jin's freedom an outlier. Trump's intervention, whatever one thinks of the man, produced a result that months of quiet diplomacy from other quarters had not.

Now — and this is the part that matters more.

One pastor's release does not change the architecture of religious control that made his imprisonment possible. The Chinese Communist Party's system of government-registered religion, which grants the right to worship only to organizations the state approves, is still the law of the land. Underground churches like Jin's — which operate without registration and face official restrictions — are still underground. The crackdown on religious groups and political dissent that put Jin in a detention center in October is still running. The same leadership that agreed to release this one pastor on this one request did not change its policy; it made an exception. Exceptions for the high-profile do not open the door for the low-profile, and the low-profile are the ones who fill Chinese detention centers.

And we should not forget the context in which this rare act of release took place. The same intervention that freed Jin did not free Jimmy Lai, who remains imprisoned. The administration has spent months [demonstrating leverage over China when it serves its own purposes](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-07-14-china-charges-u-s-seismologist-with-espionage-after-secret-detention/), even as American citizens like the [scholar-journalist arrested on espionage suspicion](https://mainstreetindependent.com/articles/2026-06-12-china-arrests-american-scholar-journalist-on-espionage-suspicion/) remain in Chinese custody without the same level of public pressure applied on their behalf. The pattern reveals not a consistent commitment to freeing the detained, but a selective willingness to use presidential intervention where the political upside is highest. The difference between the pastor who came home and the activist who did not is not a moral distinction the Chinese leadership recognizes; it is a political calculation about which case is worth the cost of the diplomatic gesture and which is not. We should not mistake the calculus for a principle.

So what do we do with this?

We hold two things at once. The first is gratitude that Ezra Jin Mingri is free. The second is clarity that his freedom is a single data point in a system designed to produce the opposite outcome for everyone who does not have a president raising their name with the other president. The first is the pastoral side of the register — the celebration of what is good. The second is the prophetic side — the naming of what is still wrong and the refusal to let the good news obscure the continuing structure of injustice.

The Torah commands the people of Israel again and again, in dozens of places, to love the stranger because they were strangers in Egypt. The memory of having been the stranger is the moral mechanism that keeps the door open. China does not have that memory; it has the memory of having been humiliated by foreign powers, and it runs its religious policy through that lens. The U.S. does have that memory — the stranger's memory — and it is the only thing that makes American pressure on behalf of a Chinese pastor morally legible rather than merely strategic.

The pastor is home. The family had its fireworks. The hamburger was eaten. That is real, and it is good.

And tomorrow, in the same China that released him, there will be other pastors in detention centers whose names no American president has raised. The miracle for one is not a system for all. The question for anyone who claims to follow the one who said "I was in prison and you came to me" is what kind of attention we give to the ones whose names never reach the conversation between the two powers.
