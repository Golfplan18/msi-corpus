*The spreadsheet had no column for her hands. The mint appreciated.*

Wanda is a wrapper on the hard-candy line I own outside Lancaster. 27 years folding cellophane around the same crease until the crease and her hands were the same shape. The Pep-O-Mint in your glove compartment — she touched it, or one identical to it, on its way to the shelf you bought it from. I retired her in the same memo I retired Choc-O-Mint, filed under the same heading, processed in the same quarter. Portfolio optimization. The savings went straight to my bottom line. Her daughter, I'm told, remembers the smell of the factory on her mother's coat. I let her have that.

The *Journal* ran a tender essay this week — Allan Ripp, hunting eBay for the chocolate mint his mother used to pull from her purse. He found packs from barn sales and estate auctions in western Colorado. He paid what he calls an excessive price, and the market agreed with him. I'd already agreed. The candy was worth less alive than dead, and the woman who wrapped it was worth less alive than the line she stood on.

Let me walk you through what the spreadsheet looked like.

Choc-O-Mint sat on line 4 of a 12-flavor portfolio. The cocoa dust required a separate hopper, extra calibration between runs, a cleaning cycle the peppermint and wintergreen lines didn't touch — a few minutes a shift, a fraction of a cent per unit. The cocoa-solids contract was repriced upward that autumn and the plain-mint line ran at a higher margin without the cross-contamination cost. Together, it would have taken the contribution margin negative if I'd kept the flavor on the production schedule. So I retired it. Not the plant, not the line, just the $0.0034 per roll that the chocolate-mint variant cost the portfolio more than the Pep-O-Mint. This is documented industry reality: the National Confectioners Association tracks SKU complexity cost because it eats margin on every shift that carries it, and consolidating overlapping flavors cuts per-unit costs by 20 to 30%. I didn't invent the mechanism. I executed it, the way the majors do, on a smaller line.

Now.

The spreadsheet has columns for throughput, margin, and allocation. It doesn't have a column for a woman's routine. Not because I hid it — because it was never there. A thing that does not appear on the spreadsheet does not exist in the decision, and the decision is what is real. I priced Wanda at whatever it cost to print the form letter, and I made the decision in the time it takes you to read this sentence. The margin goes up. The hands go away. You already know the shape of it.

I want you to hold the deeper lesson. The product itself continued to earn — just not for me. Every time a mother opened her purse and handed a child a mint, she was performing a distribution function at zero inventory cost, building a demand that outlived the supply. And when I terminated the supply, the demand didn't disappear. It went dormant. It turned into a longing that, 45 years later, presented itself to a seller as a line item: "pristine pack, sealed, 50 years old, excessive price." The price the buyer calls excessive is simply the terminal value of a demand I refused to service.

Ripp is on eBay right now, bidding on candy that cost me less to discontinue than to warehouse. The candy was worthless when Wanda was making it. I culled it without sentiment, the way you cull a low-margin SKU, and the moment I did, the candy became a collectible. The body depreciated. The product appreciated.

That's not irony. That's the mechanism.

Follow the collector's market long enough and you'll see the pattern everywhere: the thing the hand made becomes precious only after the hand is gone, and the hand, having made it, is worth nothing at all. The woman depreciated. The mint appreciated. This is the oldest arithmetic there is — I just ran it on a smaller line.

Here is Cato, who priced this twenty-two centuries before eBay:

> *"Sell worn-out oxen, blemished cattle, blemished sheep, wool, hides, an old wagon, old tools, an old slave, a sickly slave, and whatever else is superfluous."*
> — Cato, *De Agri Cultura*, 2.7, c. 160 BCE

He didn't distinguish between the tool and the woman who held it. The distinction would've been sentimental. Columella (De Re Rustica, Book I) reminds us that sentiment in estate management is a managerial weakness, and I've never had the luxury. Wanda occupied the same column as the cocoa dust. I processed them together. The Lord provides for the sparrow, but He doesn't set the margin (Matthew 6:26). I set the margin, and the margin said the sparrow was superfluous.

Now pull back. What the buyer calls the provenance — the mother's purse — is sentiment. The actual provenance is the line I discontinued and the body I transferred to the bagging station at $1.10 less per hour until her hands stiffened and the cartilage gave out, at which point I replaced her with a woman whose hands didn't complain yet. The memory he tastes was manufactured at a wage, maintained by a spine, and withdrawn by a cost-cut that rests on a principle as old as the first hired hand: when replacement cost approaches zero, maintenance cost is pure waste.

"For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). The treasure was the lozenge in the foil. The heart was the child's memory in the purse. I took the treasure off the primary market and let the heart float until it found a price on eBay. The Savior did not specify that the heart's attachment must be served by the primary supplier. The secondary supplier is a blessing, not a lapse, and I am indebted to the Colorado barn for completing the circuit I left open.

The demand goes where the supply stops. I haven't traced it, but I'm told the vintage-candy market is robust, niche, profitable. I don't own a piece of it. The quarterly number doesn't register the eBay trades of the product I once shipped, and I sleep extremely well. I kept the cocoa-dust margin. I kept the $0.0034. I kept the dental plan Wanda couldn't use because her hands couldn't hold the form to enroll. The woman who used to fold the cellophane no longer has the hands to unwrap a lozenge, but the lozenge itself, sealed in a Mason jar in Colorado, is intact, and its new owner reports it tastes exactly as he remembered it.

That is not a failure of accounting. That is a market performing precisely as designed: the taste outlasts the taster, the memory outlasts the maker, and the longing outlasts both. I did not build the longing — I simply declined to serve it, and the money I didn't pay to keep serving it bought me the retirement Wanda couldn't afford and the daughter she moved in with. I am, on the whole, content.

Every bid on a dead mint confirms the arithmetic. Enjoy the candy. Wanda's hands made the crease. I made the margin.

God bless you for your excellent taste.
