Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin is weaponizing the federal travel apparatus to punish political dissent, and calling it border security is a deliberate lie. The proposal to withhold Customs and Border Protection officers from airports in sanctuary jurisdictions is not a measure to improve enforcement; it is a mechanism of structural corruption that treats federal infrastructure as a partisan cudgel.

By mapping the Justice Department’s formal list of sanctuary jurisdictions against the nation’s major international air entry points, the administration has spent months [building this very enforcement leverage](/articles/2026-05-23-travel-industry-condemns-mullin-plan-to-pull-customs-officers-from-sanctuary-air/) over a defined set of jurisdictions. This is the same administration that has repeatedly ignored procedural constraints, yet they now expect the public to believe that Customs and Border Protection staffing is a flexible administrative lever that can be yanked from civil aviation operations without shattering the baseline economy of international passenger and cargo flows.

International arrival facilities at major airports are calibrated to specific headcounts of federal personnel. When these staffing levels are reduced for non-operational, partisan reasons, both passenger throughput and cargo clearance grind to a halt. Trade groups are right to describe the consequences as “devastating,” but the administration knows that. The damage to the travel industry is not a bug; it is the feature.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy’s public protest — he told lawmakers that “we shouldn't shut down air travel in a state that doesn't agree with our politics” — is more than a policy disagreement; it is the sound of a cabinet officer trying to contain a political firebomb. His warning that “you will all switch spots at one point” underscores the administration’s fear that this weapon will be turned against its own allies the moment the political winds shift. Even within a cabinet dominated by donor-class agendas, the specific play of turning air travel into a function of local political compliance risks destabilizing the very globalized logistics networks that corporate donors rely on.

History is a cold record here. In 2017, federal courts categorically rejected the effort to use broad funding cuts as punishment against sanctuary jurisdictions. The current attempt to use Customs staffing — an essential operational service rather than a discretionary grant — is merely a more acute, more disruptive repetition of that same failed legal theory. It is a cynical, bad-faith attempt to use the federal bureaucracy to bypass the judiciary’s 2017 ruling.

This is not a sudden escalation; it is the culmination of a [broader, months-long budget fight](/articles/2026-03-24-senators-near-deal-to-fund-homeland-security-but-leave-ice-enforcement-unfunded/) where the administration has consistently prioritized ICE enforcement over the stability of the nation’s transportation systems. When a cabinet secretary treats federal personnel as a private militia for political retaliation, the goal is not to improve the border. It is to demonstrate that no piece of public infrastructure is safe from the reaching arm of an administration determined to break the norms of shared governance.

The administration is not failing to manage the border; they are wielding executive power like a wrecking ball — dismantling the rule of law to fund a vendetta.
