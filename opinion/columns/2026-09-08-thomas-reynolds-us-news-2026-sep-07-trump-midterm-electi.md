Donald Trump is using the post office to take over the midterms.

The strongest argument for the administration begins with a legitimate federal interest, and it is the argument Solicitor General John Sauer has now made twice to the Supreme Court. The Postal Service processes election mail. Sauer argues that freezing the new USPS rules would change the agency's operations before an election and impose real costs on the federal government, the states, and voters. He told the Court that the freeze would cause "serious, irreparable harm" and that the rules were "reasonable preparation requirements for certain election-related mail." That is the government's case at its strongest.

It is not the record Judge Indira Talwani made.

On September 4, Talwani converted her temporary restraining order into a preliminary injunction. She wrote that implementing Trump's order so close to the election "threatens disenfranchisement of millions of United States citizens who seek to vote by mail." She also stressed that "the record includes no evidence relating to fraudulent mail voting to support the rushed implementation." That last sentence does the work the administration's emergency papers cannot do. The government asserts harm from preparing for the rules. The district court found no evidence supporting the rules themselves. The difference is dispositive. A court does not create a factual basis for election administration because a president demands speed.

About fifty million Americans vote by mail. The administration has offered a categorical theory of fraud — "mail-in ballots are inherently corrupt," Trump said in July, without evidence — to justify changing the rules under which those ballots move. Trump voted twice by mail himself this year. That does not prove every ballot is lawfully cast. It does defeat the claim that mail voting is inherently corrupt. Talwani applied that evidentiary discipline where it belongs: in an adjudication, not in a political speech.

The constitutional architecture does not permit what the executive is attempting. The Constitution grants the states primary authority over the "Times, Places and Manner" of federal elections. U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1; art. II, § 1. The Supreme Court has treated Congress's Elections Clause authority as binding on the states in this domain. *Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council of Arizona*, 570 U.S. 1 (2013). That authority belongs to Congress. The administration's theory runs through the Postal Service's general statutory authority — chiefly the universal service obligation Congress wrote in 39 U.S.C. § 403 and the Postal Reorganization Act of 1970 — and asks the Court to read it as a delegation of power to dictate how state-administered elections handle ballots. Operating the Postal Service is a congressional delegation to run a mail system. It is not a delegation to prescribe the rules under which states conduct elections. The executive may direct federal agencies only within the authority Congress gives them. Federal authority over USPS does not translate into federal authority to dictate how states administer voting.

Sauer attempts the translation by characterizing the order as imposing "preparation requirements" on USPS itself. Judge Talwani's finding, that the order is not administrable without "unprecedented demands" on state and local election officials two months before the election, suggests the translation does not hold. Washington Attorney General Nick Brown put it plainly: state compliance would be "practically impossible" before November. The "reasonable preparation" the SG invokes is preparation that cannot be done.

The conduct record confirms the trial court's finding. The Heritage Foundation's election-fraud database, cited by administration allies as proof of the problem, records one hundred cases of noncitizen voting over a quarter-century. The administration's "crisis of election security" is a database of four noncitizen voters per year. The Department of Homeland Security has nonetheless deployed hundreds of agents to hunt noncitizens on registered-voter rolls. The department's secretary has said ICE agents could be sent to polling places "if there is a threat to that polling place or we're serving a warrant." The Department's Civil Rights Division, run by Harmeet Dhillon, has threatened criminal liability against state election officials who fail to remove names — a threat unsupported by the underlying evidentiary record the trial judge reviewed. A whistleblower complaint Senator Richard Blumenthal released last week alleged that USPS is proceeding with a "secretive, rushed" implementation of the order capable of "catastrophic failure." The agency's own conduct confirms the trial judge's finding that the process is rushed, and the SG's "reasonable preparation" framing is contradicted by the people doing the preparing.

The order is one leg of a broader federal election machinery, and the three legs of that machinery are visible. In July, the Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights, Harmeet Dhillon, wrote to election officials in all fifty states warning that any state officer who retained noncitizens on the voter rolls could face criminal liability; the broader federal posture is to demand sensitive state voter data and assemble it into a federal file — a posture the ACLU Voting Rights Project has tracked across twenty-six lawsuits, twenty-three of which the government has already lost. That is federal acquisition directed at the voter-list machinery the states administer, in a record where the underlying fraud claim rests on roughly one hundred cases across a quarter-century. USPS rules purport to control ballot movement. And the administration has announced plans to deploy one thousand lawyers nationwide to monitor voting, with hundreds of Homeland Security agents separately searching for noncitizens among registered voters — that is federal deployment controlling election observation and enforcement. Any one of those measures can claim a legitimate administrative purpose. Together, they give the executive branch influence over the mechanisms through which an election is prepared, observed, and enforced. That is not neutral election administration. It is an attempt to federalize the machinery without the statutes, evidence, or constitutional assignment that would permit presidential control.

The procedural vehicle is the shadow docket. Professor Stephen Vladeck's framework on emergency-docket abuse identifies the use of summary procedures to effect rights-changing outcomes that ordinary process would require full merits briefing to reach. Sauer's first emergency appeal was filed the day after Judge Talwani's fourteen-day restraining order; the second was filed two days after her permanent injunction. No merits briefing. No oral argument. No signed opinion. The Roberts Court's emergency docket has been used in the second Trump administration to grant the executive's emergency applications at a rate the Brennan Center has documented at roughly eighty percent — a pattern Professor Vladeck has described as partisan, not principled. An order granting Sauer's request would be more than emergency docket housekeeping. It would be a Supreme Court decision changing national election administration through emergency papers rather than the Court's ordinary merits process. This application fits: the SG is asking the Court to bless an executive order the trial court found unsupported by evidence, on a timeline that does not permit ordinary merits review, in a posture where the rights at stake are the votes of fifty million Americans.

The resistance is bipartisan. The Democracy Defenders Fund, the Campaign Legal Center, the American Civil Liberties Union, and a coalition of former state and federal judges — including the former Fourth Circuit Judge J. Michael Luttig and the former District Judge Nancy Gertner — have filed amicus briefs opposing the order. The ACLU has been involved in twenty-six of thirty legal challenges to the administration on voter-data seizure; the administration has lost twenty-three of the cases where rulings have issued. An appellate court has already upheld the injunction against the same order. That is not the work of a department winning on the merits and losing in the court of public opinion. It is the record of a department losing on the merits.

An honest constitutional posture gives Congress power to establish the rules for federal elections under the Elections Clause. It lets the states administer those elections under that law. It permits the executive to direct the Postal Service only within the statutes Congress enacts — not turn the agency into an instrument for changing the election. It would require evidence of actual fraud before restricting a voting method used by fifty million Americans, and would not deploy the Civil Rights Division as a tool of voter suppression. The Department of Justice is not, on this record, engaged in that application.

Talwani found the factual record empty. The appellate court has already affirmed. Sauer is asking the Supreme Court to let the President take the midterms through the post office anyway. The Court can take the application. It can also decline to bless it on the record Judge Talwani compiled. The constitutional answer to that choice is not hard.
