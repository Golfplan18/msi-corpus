Authorities charged a man with arson in one Spokane wildfire.

Three fires near Spokane destroyed about 850 buildings and forced roughly 67,000 people to evacuate after erupting last weekend, according to the Associated Press. No deaths have been reported. Firefighters established containment lines around all three fires after two cooler days gave them room to work.

That progress is conditional. Forecasts called for temperatures in the upper 90s, lower humidity and stronger winds later in the week. Crews were reinforcing the lines before the weather turned against them.

The arson charge matters. So does the word *charged*. The source reports an arrest and a criminal allegation, not a conviction. If the charge is proved, the person responsible should answer for the damage the law can properly assign to him. That is one account.

It is not the whole account.

On the Adams County sand plain, grass can be the color of straw by the middle of July. Dry conditions do not need a grand theory to become a public problem. They put more pressure on the people and institutions that have to keep a community functioning when roads close, power is interrupted and families cannot get back to their homes.

A wildfire here would be an Adams County problem before it became a national climate story. The first calls would run through local dispatch and fire departments. The first evacuation instructions would depend on county roads, town officials and people who know which routes can move traffic when Highway 13 or a county road is blocked. A road that works for ordinary traffic can become a bottleneck when residents are leaving at once and fire apparatus is coming the other way.

The county’s emergency response would also depend on communications, fuel, water, trained volunteers and equipment that has to be maintained before the smoke arrives. Adams-Columbia Electric Cooperative would have to protect service and restore it under conditions that can damage lines and equipment. Adams County Memorial Hospital and the school district would have to keep serving people while staff, families and patients were dealing with the same evacuation orders.

Those are not abstract public goods. They are the reason a household can get a warning, reach a safe place, charge a phone, fill a prescription, keep children supervised and return to a house that still has electricity.

Dry weather strains each one. Fire departments may need more crews and more time on every call. Utilities face greater risk around poles, substations and access roads. Counties face the cost of shelter, transportation, traffic control and overtime. Families face gasoline, hotel rooms, missed shifts, damaged vehicles, insurance deductibles, temporary housing and the long work of replacing papers and belongings that cannot be replaced at all.

A house can be standing and still be financially broken. A worker can be safe and still lose a week of wages. A business can reopen and still carry the debt from smoke damage, lost inventory or a closed road. Recovery is measured in claims and invoices long after the television cameras leave.

That is why the Spokane arrest cannot be allowed to carry the whole meaning of the fires. The charge may establish responsibility for one deliberate act if the evidence and trial do so. It does not establish responsibility for the hot, dry and windy conditions that gave the fire greater reach. It does not explain why a community’s evacuation routes, utility network, emergency services and household budgets must absorb the consequences.

The source article does not establish a national trend about fires arriving earlier or burning more often. I will not build one from a single Spokane forecast. The narrower fact is enough: two cooler days helped firefighters make progress, and hotter, drier, windier conditions threatened to take that progress back. That is already a practical warning for any county whose public systems have to operate across long roads, scattered homes and limited local capacity.

Wendell Berry’s *The Unsettling of America* gives this problem an older vocabulary. A place is not merely a supply of usable things. It is a membership of people, work, land and obligations. When the land is treated as an input and the public systems that protect it are treated as costs, the bill does not disappear. It moves to the volunteer department, the electric cooperative, the county budget, the homeowner’s policy and the family trying to get through the next month.

The immediate work in Spokane belongs to the firefighters reinforcing containment lines and the officials moving people toward safety. The legal work belongs to the courts. The recovery work will belong to families, insurers, employers, utilities and local governments for years.

The longer public work is more ordinary and less dramatic. Counties need evacuation plans people can use, roads that remain passable, communications that function, fire departments with equipment and staffing, utilities with resilience, and recovery systems that do not leave a household alone with a deductible it cannot pay. Those needs are not luxuries added after the emergency. They are the infrastructure of staying in a place.

The alleged arsonist, if convicted, should be held accountable for the conduct proved in court. But accountability must not become a way to stop looking. One criminal charge can identify one alleged act. It cannot repair 850 buildings, return 67,000 people to their homes or pay the costs carried by public institutions and families.

That is the Adams County question I see in the Spokane report: not whether one person should answer for an arson charge. He should, if the charge is proved. The question is whether the rest of us will count the systems that have to hold when the weather leaves less room for error.
