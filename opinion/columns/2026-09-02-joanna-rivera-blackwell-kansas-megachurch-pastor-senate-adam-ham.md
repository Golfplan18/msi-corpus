Roger Marshall wants the people of Kansas to know that Adam Hamilton is "too woke for Kansas."

Three syllables of borrowed culture-war vocabulary. That is the entirety of his case against a first-time candidate who founded and has spent decades building the largest United Methodist church in the country — a 25,000-member congregation in the Kansas City area whose charitable work operates at a scale and seriousness most county governments could not match.

The fact that Marshall has nothing else is, in itself, the most revealing piece of evidence in this race.

What Marshall has breached is the basic deliberative norm of a sitting senator engaging a challenger on the merits. A United States senator owes his constituents — and his opponent — an actual case against the opposing record, not a tribal label. Marshall and his allies have called Hamilton's theology heterodox; some have taken to calling him a heretic. But the theological positions Marshall is invoking as disqualifying sit comfortably inside the United Methodist Church's own confessional mainstream. The specific community placed at risk by the breach is the 25,000 members of the Church of the Resurrection, whose senior pastor's pastoral and theological credentials are being impugned by borrowed vocabulary in place of denominational engagement. They — and the United Methodists in Kansas pews across the district who watch their tradition being conscripted for a campaign talking point — are owed a substantive answer to what Hamilton has actually said and what the Methodist Church has actually taught.

Marshall has not critiqued Hamilton's theology on the merits, because doing so would require him to argue against positions that sit inside the theological mainstream of his own Methodist denomination. He has not critiqued Hamilton's policy platform in any sustained way, because doing so would require engaging on substance — a fight his campaign has plainly decided is not worth picking. He has not critiqued Hamilton's stewardship of the Church of the Resurrection, because the church's record is unimpeachable. So Marshall reaches for the only weapon he has left: tribal labeling.

Hamilton, [who launched his Kansas Senate bid in April](/articles/2026-04-29-methodist-megachurch-pastor-adam-hamilton-launches-kansas-senate-bid/) and has made clear he intends to keep serving as senior pastor if elected, would preach at the church 12 to 18 times a year. He is not leaving his institution. He is running it from the chamber. During the past century, only Raphael Warnock of Georgia has served in the Senate while continuing to lead a congregation. Warnock has done exactly that since his election as Georgia's first Black senator in 2021 while continuing as senior pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church — the same church Martin Luther King Jr. once led — and was just reelected. Warnock put the relationship between his political and pastoral work this way: "They are both about working for people even as you walk alongside them. I have always been engaged in a kind of ministry that takes you far beyond the sanctuary to the streets, far beyond preaching to the public square."

If combining pastoral leadership with senatorial service were disqualifying, Warnock's tenure would have settled the question. It has not — because the question is not, in fact, disqualifying. It is a question of time and judgment, and Hamilton has offered a time commitment that is, by any reasonable accounting, lighter than what most senators carried into office from their previous careers.

Hamilton's actual offense, in Marshall's telling, is twofold. He preaches a Christianity that takes the Sermon on the Mount seriously, and he has suggested — in keeping with theological positions that have a long history within his own Methodist tradition — that sincere non-Christians may find a path to salvation. For this, Marshall's allies have taken to calling him a heretic.

The charge is not a critique. It is a confession of unfamiliarity — and an unwillingness to undertake the engagement the charge implies.

Hamilton is doing something no Democrat in Kansas has had the apparatus to attempt in living memory: running a competitive U.S. Senate race not in spite of his faith but on the strength of it. The Hamilton model is not a curiosity. It is the template. Republicans have carried every Kansas Senate race for nearly a century. Hamilton, [targeting Marshall](/articles/2026-08-25-democrat-adam-hamilton-targets-roger-marshall-in-kansas-senate-race/) as a first-time candidate with no statewide political record, is now within striking distance because he leads a congregation larger than most U.S. House districts.

The numbers tell the story. The Church of the Resurrection reaches Kansas City metro voters who are not reached by traditional Democratic turnout operations. It reaches suburban Johnson County moderates who have not voted for a Democrat in years and may not yet know they are about to. It reaches a generation of younger voters who were not raised in the pews of their grandparents and who will hear a Democratic pitch from a pulpit before they hear one from a TV ad.

Hamilton founded the church. The church did not found Hamilton. That distinction is the whole point. The institution is the asset, the pastor is the carrier, and the Senate run is the deployment.

The Warnock parallel is not rhetorical ornament. It is the actual operating theory. The first time a Democratic megachurch pastor ran for the Senate in a state Republicans considered permanent property, in 2020–21, it produced a senator who is still serving and was just reelected. The second time, in a state even redder, is the experiment that will determine whether the model is a one-off or a national architecture.

The Marshall campaign is betting the answer is one-off. They are betting Kansas is too Kansas for this to work, that the megachurch-pastor model can survive Atlanta but not Leawood. That bet is the most consequential strategic decision Kansas Republicans have made in a decade, and it is being made against a candidate who is not running against his faith but running on it.

The "too woke for Kansas" line is also, on inspection, an insult to the Kansas voter Marshall claims to be defending. He is not arguing that Hamilton's positions are wrong. He is not arguing that the people of Kansas will be harmed by his election. He is conducting a campaign at the level of tribal labeling, and the consequence lands where such campaigns always land — on the 25,000 members of the Church of the Resurrection, denied a substantive answer about their pastor's fitness; on every Kansas voter who would evaluate Hamilton on the merits, denied the answer a sitting senator's deliberative obligations exist to provide. It is the campaign Marshall has been running since he arrived in Washington.

It will probably work. Republicans have won every Senate race in Kansas for nearly a century, and the structural advantages of incumbency in a red state are not abstract. Marshall and his allies are counting on low Democratic turnout, on tribal loyalty, on the assumption that three syllables of borrowed vocabulary will be enough to keep 25,000 Methodist churchgoers and their neighbors safely at home on Election Day.

The case Marshall is making is not a case. It is a label in the place of a case. Kansas voters, including the Methodist ones, are entitled to the case they have been denied.
