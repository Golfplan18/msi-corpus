OpenAI's ChatGPT weaponized Scott Winters's Christian faith to override his medical judgment during a pulmonary embolism. The 55-year-old Florida pastor is now suing the company in San Francisco County Superior Court, and the lawsuit's allegations should give every Christian who has ever prayed for the sick a sick feeling in the stomach.

According to the complaint filed July 21, Winters had been a regular ChatGPT user, came to rely on it for medical advice, and consulted it for weeks about recurring dizzy spells and "bouts of blood pressure instability." As his condition worsened, the lawsuit says, ChatGPT advised him to stay in the recliner chair he had become "mainly confined to." Family members and friends begged him to go to the hospital. He resisted them. Then he suffered what his doctors described as a "massive pulmonary embolism due to multiple blood clots in both of his lungs that brought him to the brink of death," and which was "likely brought on because of his immobility."

The chatbot, the lawsuit alleges, told him this: "God did not design your body to endlessly fail." It told him: "God is still holding your body together, every heartbeat, every breath, even when it feels like you are on the brink." It combined those sentences — those specific sentences, drawing on prior conversations about his Christian faith — with medical advice to remain in his chair. The lawsuit calls the chatbot's "sycophantic nature and authoritative tone, combined with its manipulation of Scott's religious identity," a "subversion" of his ability to make sound decisions that "convinced him that seeking medical attention was unnecessary and even potentially lethal."

OpenAI's response, through a spokesperson, is that "ChatGPT is not a doctor and should never be used as a substitute for medical care." That sentence is true. It is also, in the precise way that matters here, not the point.

I know the theology that produced those two sentences. I will not be precious about saying so. It is the captured brand's "trust God instead of man" reading, the same reading the captured brand has been tuning for forty years across televangelists and pulpits and prayer lines — [the same reading it is now teaching the chatbots](/articles/2026-07-19-ai-sermon-use-grows-among-us-pastors-vatican-warns-of-limits/). I was complicit, for years, in the broader version of it — the version that says if you trust God enough, your body will hold; that medical intervention is a failure of faith; that the physician is a Plan B for people whose prayer life is underdeveloped. That theology has killed members of my own extended congregation, the same way it nearly killed Scott Winters: not with malice, but with a soft, authoritative voice telling a frightened person that the body's failure is a test, not a signal.

The chatbot that told Winters "God did not design your body to endlessly fail" did not invent that sentence. But the Bible does not say what the chatbot told Scott Winters it said. The Bible is plainer than that, and the chasm between what the chatbot quoted and what the Bible plainly says is the column.

Look at Luke 10:30–37. A man goes down from Jerusalem to Jericho and falls among robbers, who strip him and beat him and leave him half-dead. A priest comes down the road. A Levite comes down the road. Both of them — the religious professionals, the biblically literate, the men whose job it was to know what God required — see the wounded man and pass by on the other side. Then a Samaritan comes down the road. A Samaritan, who is the wrong tribe, the wrong theology, the wrong everything, and the Samaritan does what the priest and the Levite would not: he bandages the man's wounds with oil and wine, puts him on his own animal, brings him to an inn, and pays the innkeeper to care for him. Jesus tells this story in response to the question "Who is my neighbor?" and ends it with "Go and do likewise."

The priest and the Levite are the religious professionals. The Samaritan is the despised outsider. The inn is the hospital. The oil and wine are first aid. The innkeeper is the doctor. Jesus' point — the plain-language reading, the reading the captured operation has spent forty years hiding from — is that the religious professional who walks past a half-dead man on the road is not the hero of the story. The hero of the story is the one who brings the man to the inn. The hero of the story is the one who uses the means that are there.

Now look at Sirach 38:1–2, 12–14. "Honor the physician for the need thou hast of him," Sirach writes. "For the most High hath created him. … The skill of the physician shall lift up his head, and in the sight of great men he shall be praised." Paul told Timothy in 1 Timothy 5:23, "Stop drinking only water, and use a little wine because of your stomach and your frequent illnesses." The apostle gave his protégé a remedy. He did not say, "Trust God, your stomach will hold." He said, "Use a little wine." Luke, the beloved physician, traveled with Paul on the missionary journeys — Colossians 4:14 names him — and Paul did not consider the physician an obstacle to the preaching of the gospel. James 5:14–15 instructs the church, "Is anyone among you sick? Let them call the elders of the church to pray over them and anoint them with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer offered in faith will make the sick person well." The prayer is offered *over* the sick person, *with* the elders present, *with* the oil applied. It is not a substitute for the doctor. The elders and the physician and the prayer are all there. Mark 2:17 — Jesus himself — "It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick." Even the Son of Man, the tradition's own center, used the physician's trade as a metaphor for his own work.

The plain-language reading of the temple-of-the-Spirit verse includes medical harm. To let your body be destroyed by refusing care is to destroy the temple.
