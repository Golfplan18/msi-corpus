Trump and Khamenei are destroying each other's water plants and calling it strategy. On Saturday, CENTCOM announced it had struck Iranian "surveillance sites, military logistics infrastructure, underground weapons storage and maritime capabilities," using fighter aircraft, drones, and warships. Iran hit back by destroying infrastructure in southern Iran — bridges, roads, and a water desalination plant in Jask that now leaves roughly 10,000 people without drinking water.

Iran then retaliated against Kuwait, striking its desalination plant and an oil facility. Kuwait gets 90 percent of its drinking water from desalination. Kuwait's petroleum corporation reported injuries and "severe material losses." Jordan shot down ten Iranian missiles. Israel escalated strikes on Nabatieh and the Tyre district in Lebanon.

The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes, remains shut. Both sides enforce blockades.

Think about what a desalination plant means. It is not a bunker. It is a building full of pumps and pipes and the people who maintain them. The pipefitters, the electricians, the control-room operators. When that plant goes down, those workers have no jobs to go to. Their families have no water. In the desert, that is not a hardship. That is a crisis.

Ten thousand people in Jask. Kuwait's entire drinking-water supply. These are not military targets. These are the places where people fill their glasses and wash their children and keep their households running. The operators at the Kuwaiti plant did not choose this war. Neither did the families in Jask. Neither did the truckers at the Strait of Hormuz who cannot move cargo and cannot feed their families. They are just the people downstream of decisions made by men in rooms they will never enter.

The two sides frame this in incompatible languages. CENTCOM calls it "accountability." Iran's ambassador to Pakistan says the US "interpreted [the memorandum of understanding] contrary to its terms and gained control over parts of Strait Hormuz to obtain what it couldn't in the battlefield." Whatever you make of either characterization, it does not change the physical fact. The water plants are down. The roads are broken. The commerce has stopped. And the people who bear the cost had no vote on any of it.

I have watched this pattern for twenty-five years. Bush launched ground wars. Obama expanded air campaigns and normalized targeted killing. Trump and Biden sustained both while adding layers. The machinery of permanent war does not malfunction between administrations. It accumulates. Each president inherits the apparatus, uses it, passes it along. The generals who build the targets brief the next president the same way they briefed the last one. The defense contractors who build the weapons sell to every administration at a markup that would get a man run out of a small town for trying it on a brake job.

When the strait closes and oil prices spike, it is not the defense executives who struggle. It is not the officials in Washington or Tehran who worry about keeping the lights on. It is the truck driver in Redemption Springs, the mechanic in Kuwait City, the mother in Jask trying to figure out where her family will get water tomorrow. The machinery was built to serve those at the top. The cost flows downward. It always does.

I will not pretend there is a clean version of this. You cannot bomb a water plant to win a war. You cannot destroy a nation's drinking water and call it strategy. The people at those plants — the workers, their families, the communities they serve — are not obstacles to be cleared. They are the ones paying the price for decisions made by men who will never go thirsty.

Khamenei will say he had no choice. Trump will say he had no choice. Both will be wrong. The choice was to stop. Neither did. The machinery runs, and the people at the bottom carry it on their backs.
