Moscow is arming North Korea while calling South Korea's cooperation with NATO a threat to Russian security. That is not a misunderstanding. It is a protection racket operating at the scale of nations, and working people on both sides of the Pacific are the ones who pay for it.

Russia's foreign ministry issued a statement this week expressing "serious concern" over Seoul's deepening military cooperation with NATO, warning that South Korea had become a "de facto participant" in the alliance's rearmament and that NATO was "openly declaring preparations for war with Russia." This from the same Moscow that signed a comprehensive strategic partnership treaty with Pyongyang two years ago, that has received — by Seoul's own intelligence estimates — at least 15,000 North Korean combat troops and conventional weapons for its war in Ukraine, and that is [deepening its strategic dialogue with North Korea](/articles/2026-07-21-lavrov-choe-meet-in-moscow-for-third-russia-north-korea-strategic-dialogue/) even as it lectures Seoul about drifting toward the West.

The hypocrisy is the point, and it is the kind of thing a man who works for a living can spot without a graduate degree in international relations.

South Korean President Lee Jae Myung attended the NATO summit in Ankara earlier this month and proposed elevating defense cooperation to what he called a "Korea-NATO Defense Industry Partnership 2.0" — joint research, shared production, standardized operating concepts for weapons systems. The proposal extends beyond weapons sales into co-development and co-manufacturing. That means contracts. That means production lines. That means shifts. And that is where the story stops being abstract and starts hitting people where they live.

Consider what "joint defense production" actually means in material terms. When South Korea's Hyundai Rotem or Hanwha Aerospace co-produces weapons platforms with NATO member states, the contract flows go somewhere specific — to shipyards in Busan, to munitions plants in Changwon, to the engineering firms that write the technical packages. Those are real jobs. Real payroll. Real local tax revenue. But every dollar that moves through a defense contract is a dollar that has to come from somewhere, and it does not come from the defense primes. It comes from national budgets. It comes from the same budget lines that fund roads, schools, and hospitals in Pohang and Daegu and every small town in between.

The defense industry has an opinion about all of this, and its opinion is always the same: more.

Lockheed Martin, RTX, Hanwha, Hyundai Rotem — these companies do not care whether Russia's objection to Seoul's NATO ties is sincere or cynical. What they care about is that every round of escalation, every diplomatic crisis, every new axis-of-threat story produces exactly one reliable outcome: more procurement. The "Korea-NATO Defense Industry Partnership 2.0" is a procurement pipeline dressed in the language of alliance solidarity. Joint research means joint contracts. Shared production means shared revenue. Standardized platforms mean a customer base that cannot easily switch to a competitor once the standard is locked in.

I served in an Abrams tank, and I can tell you from where I sat in the gunner's seat that a tank is a machine made of steel, rubber, electronics, and the labor of thousands of people whose names never appear in the strategic studies journals. When a government decides to build or buy a new weapons platform, that decision ripples through supply chains that reach into towns most Americans could not find on a map. The people who manufacture the armor plate, the optics, the engines, the ammunition — they are workers, not strategists. They are doing what the labor market offers them. And what the labor market offers them is shaped, more than they usually know, by decisions made in ministries of defense halfway around the world by people who will never set foot in their shop.

Russia's objection to all of this is not that Seoul's defense partnerships threaten Russian security. Russia is a nuclear-armed state with the largest landmass on earth. South Korea's K239 Chunmoo launchers do not threaten Moscow. What Moscow wants is a South Korea that is isolated, that cannot coordinate with the broader Western alliance, and that has to deal with the Russia–North Korea axis alone. That way Russia can squeeze from both ends — pressure in Europe, pressure in Asia — without worrying about a coordinated response.

Here is what that squeeze actually costs working people. North Korea has sent around 15,000 combat troops to Ukraine. Those troops are not fighting for Korean interests. They are fighting for Moscow's war of territorial expansion. And the weapons flowing the other direction — from Russia into North Korea — do not just sit in warehouses. They are pointed at Seoul. They are pointed at the 28,500 American service members stationed south of the DMZ. They are pointed at the Korean families who live within artillery range of the border, which is to say the families of the workers who staff the Samsung and Hyundai plants that form the backbone of the South Korean economy.

That is the material reality of the Russia–North Korea axis that Moscow does not want discussed when it issues statements about Seoul's NATO ties "posing a threat" to Russian security. The threat is not to Russia. The threat is to every working family in the Seoul metropolitan area, which happens to contain roughly half of South Korea's population.

Now, I am not saying the defense industry is wrong that South Korea needs military capability. I rode an Abrams into Iraq; I know what it costs to be unprepared. What I am saying is that the people who profit from this cycle — the primes, the consultants, the revolving-door crowd who move between Pentagon offices and defense-contractor boardrooms — have a financial interest in making the cycle permanent. Every crisis is a procurement opportunity. Every axis-of-threat story is a budget justification. Every new partnership framework is a revenue stream.

The South Korean foreign ministry put it in diplomatic language: cooperation with NATO will "reinforce each side's role and contribution to international peace and stability." The ministry also noted that the Russia-Ukraine war and North Korea-Russia military cooperation "have a negative effect on international peace and security on the Korean Peninsula." That is true. But it is not the whole truth.

The whole truth is that defense spending is a transfer — from public budgets to private shareholders, from communities that need infrastructure to corporations that build weapons systems, from workers who pay taxes to executives who collect contracts. Eisenhower said it in his farewell address on January 17, 1961: "In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex." The sentence that followed is the one people forget: "Only an alert and knowledgeable citizenry can compel the proper meshing of the huge industrial and military machinery of defense with our peaceful methods and goals, so that security and liberty may prosper together." He was talking about the kind of citizenry that reads these stories and asks who profits, not just who threatens.

South Korea is right to pursue defensive partnerships. Russia is wrong to arm Pyongyang while denouncing Seoul. But the defense primes are right now writing proposals to both governments, and they do not care who is right. They care about the contract.

The citizens of South Korea and the citizens of the countries whose taxes will fund these programs deserve to know how much of the money is going to actual security and how much is going to the same defense-contractor margin that has been growing for thirty years. That is the question Moscow's propaganda is designed to make unsayable — because if the conversation is only about threats and alliances, nobody has time to ask about the invoice.
