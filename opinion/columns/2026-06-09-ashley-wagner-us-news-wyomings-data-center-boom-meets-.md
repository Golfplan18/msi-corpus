Meta and Microsoft are warehousing workers in man camps while stripping local housing markets. Twenty years of quiet—cattle, antelope, coyotes on the shortgrass prairie—erased by the kind of round‑the‑clock construction that follows Silicon Valley’s promises like a dust cloud. That’s what Stan and Tammy Higgins see from their back window now, outside Cheyenne: earth movers and heavy trucks, the front edge of a Meta data center that has already devoured their horizon and is about to swallow more. The Higginses are at their wits’ end, but the thing that should worry the rest of us isn’t the noise or the lost view. It’s the Safeway cashier.

When the Laramie County planning department finally laid the cards on the table, Justin Arnold didn’t sugarcoat it. The “man camp” proposed for up to 5,600 transient construction and trades workers—a temporary city larger than most Wyoming towns—was not some developer’s bonus ask. It was the mitigation. Without it, thousands of well‑paid laborers would flood the existing rental market, and “the cashier on South Greeley Highway that’s working at Safeway,” Arnold warned, “is going to get priced out as soon as their lease comes up on their rental.” He could have been talking about any one of us.

The man camp is not a solution; it is a pressure valve. It keeps the capital flowing for AI infrastructure while keeping local rents low enough to prevent immediate revolt. The kitchen-table version of this arrangement is the math we have been watching in every boom town for thirty years: developers arrive, housing supply contracts, and the people who actually live there are told their quiet way of life is the collateral damage of progress. Arnold captured it perfectly: “If you’re renting, you are up a crick.” That isn’t a rural quirk; it’s the late‑stage American housing contract, now landing in the least‑populous state in the union because the tech industry has figured out there’s cheap land and even cheaper political will.

This isn’t a one‑off. Two‑thirds of planned U.S. AI data centers are already rising on drought‑hit land, putting another squeeze on families and small farmers. And after Ohio finally suspended its data‑center tax break because the costs had become undeniable, Wyoming is racing to hand out the same blank check.

Mayor Patrick Collins touts the young trades workers who moved away for jobs and are now coming home to build the data‑center infrastructure. He calls it “really gratifying.” And I don’t blame those kids for taking the work—millennials have spent their adulthoods navigating a dead‑end economy where a union trade is one of the only paths left to a middle‑class life. But the story being sold to Cheyenne is the same story that has hollowed out urban neighborhoods from coast to coast: a wave of transient, high‑wage labor props up a few service‑sector jobs while quietly evicting everyone who was already scraping by. The people who can finally afford to return are the exception; the people who can’t afford to stay are the rule.

“Men who are away from their families with no ties to this community,” a resident warned online, capturing the isolation the arrangement manufactures. Swift diagnosed this exact architecture in “You’re On Your Own, Kid,” tracing the shift from waiting for community validation to realizing you have been placed in a landscape where support is not coming. Cheyenne’s labor import strategy makes the lyric literal.

The developer, Iron Guard Housing, pitches a “lifestyle experience” with linen service and pickleball courts, but the modular grid is designed for turnover rather than belonging. Workers are housed as units of labor, not neighbors.

Census data and housing studies track the housing-cost divergence, showing that nearly half of all U.S. renters are now cost-burdened while median income growth has lagged behind. The Cheyenne data-center boom is the localized expression of that national arithmetic. When a developer sells temporary comfort as a substitute for permanent shelter, the structural reality does not change because the modular unit has a shower. A town cannot function when its service workers and its incoming construction labor are both priced out of the same zip code.

The Catholic working-class formation tradition treats housing as the foundational material of mutual aid. Dorothy Day wrote about the long loneliness of the isolated worker, and the parish infrastructure she organized was built to dissolve that isolation through shared material life. Warehousing five thousand people behind a high-security perimeter is the inverse of that project. It extracts the labor and externalizes the social cost onto the residents living next to the heavy trucks and rumbling earth movers.

Real housing policy looks like community land trusts that decommodify the land under workers’ feet, paired with zoning reform to permit dense multifamily housing instead of shuffling disputes to a three-minute lectern slot. It requires employer impact fees that fund permanent housing inventory before ground breaks, rather than after local renters are priced out. Laramie County already has conditional-use permitting that could be updated to require workforce-housing impact fees before a single modular unit is delivered. That is what a town demands when it treats labor as a community instead of temporary inventory.

The planning commission voted unanimously to advance the project. A few weeks later, the county commissioners pulled it at the last minute—maybe to shop for a less conspicuous site, maybe to let the heat die down. The trucks will keep trundling past the Higginses’ irrigation lines whether the camp sits on site A or site B. The labor is already imported. The boom will go on, and the tech giants will tell us it’s necessary. But we don’t have to pretend it’s good for the people who actually live where the concrete gets poured. The only question is whether these towns demand permanent housing investment, or keep accepting man camps as the condition. The Safeway cashier, the young family in a two‑bedroom rental, the retiree who thought she’d found her last quiet street—they’re the price of AI’s next leap forward. Again.
