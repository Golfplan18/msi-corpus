Representative Nancy Mace, a Republican from South Carolina, designated every Muslim American officeholder a Trojan horse and a threat to the republic. She posted the sentence on X within days of Abdul El-Sayed's victory in Michigan's Democratic Senate primary this month — the win that, by the Associated Press's count, made him the first Muslim nominated by a major party to run for the United States Senate. President Donald Trump and other Republicans had already begun referring to the nominee by his full name, Abdulrahman Mohamed El-Sayed, while depicting him as a danger to America. The Associated Press, in its coverage of the milestone, described it against "persisting and widespread anti-Muslim sentiment across much of the nation."

She said it on X. She meant it.

Let that register before the analysis. A sitting United States Congresswoman did not say some particular Muslim officeholder had engaged in some particular conduct warranting some particular scrutiny. She said every single one. By category. Across all fifty states, all levels of office, all party affiliations. By the religious identity of the people holding the office, not by anything any of them had done. The accusation Mace has filed is not against any individual American Muslim. It is against the category. The category she named is the category her party is now working to mobilize against. The category her president reinforced the same week.

The work the apparatus is doing in unison — the tweet, the full-name deployment, the depiction of a Senate nominee as a danger to America — is a single coordinated operation, and the operation runs for the benefit of the political economy that produced it.

The "Trojan horse" trope has structural work to do. It is not a description. It is a centuries-old device for collapsing a religious or ethnic minority's public presence into foreignness, by treating the public presence itself as cover for subversion. The Jewish-American officeholder is a foreign agent. The Catholic officeholder during the Kennedy era was a foreign agent. The Japanese-American during World War II was a foreign agent. The content of the accusation is interchangeable because the function is identical: to mark an entire category of American citizens as fundamentally not American, such that their exclusion from the body politic becomes a matter of national survival rather than a violation of it.
